## Capítulo 1 — Management and Leadership (págs. 1–32)

**Central idea:** Managing means achieving goals through others, using resources efficiently and effectively. Leading means inspiring people, getting them to take ownership of goals, and changing what they believe is possible. They are distinct but complementary activities, and the security professional needs both.

### 1.1 Management

- **Management:** activities for using resources (people, time, money) efficiently and effectively in pursuit of one or more goals.
- **Manager:** a person who coordinates and oversees the work of others in order to meet the organization's goals.

**1.1.1 Who are managers?**

- They range from millennials to baby boomers. Women are now the majority among graduates and are increasingly entering management.
- **Demographics:** statistical data about the population and its groups.
- The line between manager and non-manager is blurring. A lone security manager with no team who acts across the whole company is already managing.
- The manager's job is to get others to perform. Their success is the group's success, not personal achievement.

**Case — the director who did not take the investigation:** a lone security director obtained budget for four professionals. Faced with a complex, high-profile fraud, he considered handling it himself. He chose instead to guide his subordinate and give him resources. Lesson: the manager stops being an individual contributor; taking the case away would slow the team's learning. He also reported to a superior, and his staffing request proved to be a good investment.

**Figure 1.1 Levels of Management** — pyramid, from base to top:

| Level | Who | Key function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nonmanagerial employees | Base | Operational tasks |
| First-line / frontline managers | Foreman, line manager, shift supervisor, section head, nursing supervisor, sergeant | Lowest management level; they supervise about 93% of all non-supervisory employees |
| Middle managers | General managers, branch managers, department managers | Turn strategy into action; in charge of line managers and specialists; answerable to top management; spend more time on organizing activities than top management does |
| Top / senior / executive / upper management | Board, president, vice presidents, chief executive officer (CEO) | Direction of the organization; manage ideas more than people; goals, strategic plans, and policies |

- In matrix structures managers have more autonomy, but top management still sets goals and strategies.

### 1.2 Organizations

- **Organization:** a deliberate arrangement of people to accomplish a specific purpose. Examples: companies, hospitals, nonprofits, museums, schools, political campaigns, sports teams, music tours.
- The ASIS ESRM guideline (2019) applies within the organization. ESRM = Enterprise Security Risk Management. Primary elements: mission, vision, core values, and stakeholders.

| Common characteristic | Content |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Distinct purpose | Mission statement and goals |
| 2. People | They do the work of the mission |
| 3. Deliberate structure | Bureaucratic: rigid roles (large companies, federal agencies). Flexible: matrix, with small, fast teams |

- A good manager inspires and motivates, coaches to solve problems, gives performance feedback, and reports regularly.
- People often leave because of their boss; bad managers cause unnecessary turnover.
- Being sustainable requires profitability, not just growth.
- **Gallup** (millions of employees, thousands of managers): what weighs most on productivity and loyalty is the employee–manager relationship. Pay, benefits, and environment do not.
- Choosing managers for talent (interviews and assessments): profitability +48%, productivity +22%, engagement +30%, customer satisfaction +17%, turnover −19%.

### 1.3 What Is It That Managers Do?

| Concept | Definition | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Efficiency | Maximum output with minimum input | Cost strategy; security is also expected to contain spending and justify every resource with valuable results |
| Effectiveness | Achieving the intended result | Not always cheap; focused on meeting goals |

- Security is compared with industry leaders through benchmarking and standards.
- They are not mutually exclusive: a low-cost airline cannot save money at the expense of safety. New aircraft and trained personnel cost money, but they sustain service and profitability.

### 1.4 The Evolution of Management

- **First Industrial Revolution:** late 18th century; shift from agrarian and craft work to industry.
- **Frederick Taylor (1911)**, mechanical engineer: scientific management, the “one best way.” He sought efficiency and lower cost. According to the book, it was more method than science.
- **Henri Fayol**, French businessman: managers perform basic functions that define good management. Today they are summarized as **PODSCORB**:

| Letter | Function | Key points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| P | Planning | Short term: weekly, monthly, quarterly. Medium: annual. Long: several years. They affect capital projects (long), staffing (medium), and daily operations (short) |
| O | Organizing | Assigning tasks; gathering funds, materials, personnel, technology, and equipment; division of labor into narrowly defined tasks |
| D | Directing | Setting the course; ultimate responsibility; controlling, monitoring progress, motivating |
| S | Staffing | Hiring, training, retaining; the right person in the right job |
| CO | Coordinating | Connecting people and functions; synchronizing areas the manager does not control |
| R | Reporting | Visible evidence of management; includes personnel problems, new processes, evaluations, and sales |
| B | Budgeting | Projecting, executing, and controlling the area's budget |

Budget tasks: base budgets; incremental increases and cuts; short- and long-term forecasts; anticipated and unanticipated contingencies; forecasting lower spending; proposing and justifying increases; identifying contingencies; explaining one-time expenses; prioritizing capital investments with analysis; articulating contingencies; justifying the sufficiency of the budget.

**Exam note:** the book says PODSCORB includes “Deciding” (8 functions), but it develops only 7, and its D is Directing. Its bibliography cites POSDCORB, the classic 7-function acronym. Outside the book, POSDCORB is attributed to Luther Gulick (1937), inspired by Fayol.

**Skills by level**

| Skill | What it is | Where it weighs most |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Technical | Mastery of operational work; e.g., knowing the guards' functions in depth | Lower levels; deeper than at the top level |
| Interpersonal | Relating, communicating, building trust, motivating | Grows as one moves up |
| Conceptual | Thinking about the abstract and complex; seeing the whole organization; e.g., strategies to grow and attract clients | All levels, especially top management |

To move up you have to go beyond the technical.

### 1.5 Managers Today

**Five risk buckets of the late 20th century:** strategic (competitors); financial (financing growth, managing debt); operational (exposure of the entire value chain); compliance or regulatory (new rules); reputational (mistakes that damage future operations and profitability).

Today these contain more specific risks, which differ by position. **Recent list (10):** slow growth and pressure on profits; regulatory and policy risks; cyber threats and loss of intellectual property; liquidity (interest rates, access to capital); terrorism; collapse of a major government; inability to manage crises or repair reputation; inability to attract or retain talent; pandemics and natural disasters; disruptive innovation.

- In security, some risks are handled with programs (corporate crisis management) and others when they arise (terrorism, theft of intellectual property).
- Today's global company differs greatly from the multinationals of the 1960s and 1970s.

**Management challenges of the last decade (11):** responding to competitive markets; closeness to customers; outsourcing and alliances; speed, quality, and productivity; borderless groups and the global enterprise; empowerment, self-direction, and diversity; lean structures; focus on core processes; high-performance teams; technology and expert knowledge to create value; shift from “command and control” to leadership.

**Three ideas:** (1) management is universal across size, type, level, and place. (2) You either manage or you are managed. (3) Management is central to achieving goals.

**1.5.1 Leadership**

- Management deals with complexity and provides order and consistency. The leader inspires trust, fights fear and hopelessness, defines goals, and sketches a better future.
- **Paradoxes of leadership:** art and science; change and stability; personal attributes and relationships; vision and action; past and future; manages things and leads people; transformational and transactional; serves employees and customers; learning and unlearning; values and behaviors.

| Aspect | Manager | Leader |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goals | Impersonal; arise from need and culture | Personal; takes ownership of them |
| Stance | Reactive | Proactive |
| Influence | Responds | Changes what is seen as desirable, possible, and necessary |

- They differ in motivation, personal history, and way of thinking.
- Leadership is not mystical and does not depend on charisma. It is not better than management and does not replace it: they are complementary.
- A high position does not make anyone a leader. Success in one context does not guarantee success in another: military officers failed in government and CEOs in another company.

### 1.6 An Evolutionary View of Leadership

In the early 20th century the search was for what distinguished leaders from followers. Leadership was defined as: behavior, style, skill, process, responsibility, experience, management function, position of authority, relationship of influence, characteristic, and unique capability.

### 1.7–1.10 Leadership theories

| Theory | Idea | Limits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1.7 “Great Man” | Innate, male, military: “leaders are born”; enlightened, incorruptible hero | Many were corrupted by power, greed, and money |
| 1.8 Trait theory | Leader traits are inherited | Ignores the situation; treats traits as fixed; there is no universal list |
| 1.9 Situational theory | Action is chosen according to the situation; capabilities must fit followers, goals, and context | A successful leader may fail in another situation |
| 1.10 Behavioral theory | Looks at how the leader acts | Not a complete recipe; opened a paradigm shift |

- **Trait:** a distinguishing quality of a person. **Leader traits (10):** drive for responsibility and task completion; vigor and persistence; risk-taking and originality; social initiative; self-confidence and sense of identity; willingness to accept consequences; ability to absorb interpersonal stress; tolerance for frustration and delay; ability to influence others; ability to structure interaction toward the desired end.
- **What alters the situation:** members joining and leaving; changes in relationships, goals, internal influence, resources, relationships between groups, and the environment.
- **Situational variables (11):** history of the organization; the leader's experience; the environment; the group's tasks; psychological climate; group size; the group's capabilities and experience; cooperation permitted or required; expectations; personalities; time to decide.
- Deciding in turbulent environments differs from deciding in stable ones. Security lesson: some people do not perform in a crisis.
- **Two behavioral propositions:** (1) the leader's behavior is satisfying if it is seen as a source of immediate or future satisfaction. (2) It is motivating if it ties satisfaction to good performance and provides coaching, guidance, support, and rewards.

### 1.11 A New Leadership Paradigm

- Achieving goals may depend on their difficulty, the resources, or the group, not on the leader.
- Leadership is a **collective process**: social, embodied, subject to material limits, context-dependent, and sensitive to contingencies. It is not individual, abstract, or general.
- **Competency:** an underlying characteristic of a person: trait, motive, skill, self-image, social role, or knowledge. In leadership, behavioral dimensions.
- **Competency models:** catalogs of traits, capabilities, and attributes. They set key behaviors and provide a framework for comparison (benchmark).
- Limits: the ideal goes out of date; each level, function, and unit calls for different competencies. Risk: a “shopping list” that prioritizes teaching over learning.
- **Leadership development:** a continuous process, not a one-time event. Many programs are quick, technical fixes; a good one is practical and focused on the ethical and moral. The key is the will to learn, grow, and change. Anyone can develop through a process.

**Benefits of competency-based programs:** (1) tangible, measurable dimensions (values, behaviors, activities); (2) a clear message about what is valued; (3) a common language about leadership; (4) a link to rewards and performance measures; (5) a framework for comparison.

A competency model is useful for: evaluating performance; profiling positions and candidates; assessing the management pipeline; guiding development initiatives; providing strategic value; being a competitive advantage.

### 1.12 The Dynamics of Leadership

- Teams are no longer visible: they are geographically dispersed.
- Leadership changes according to level, moment, environment, and whether one acts alone or in a team. Each generation asks for something different: Baby Boomers, Generation X, Generation Y, Millennials, and Generation Z.
- **Exam note:** the book treats Generation Y and Millennials as distinct groups; in common usage they are the same.

### 1.13 Leadership Styles

- **Leadership style:** what the leader does and how they act according to context; it reflects their philosophy.
- It has been studied for almost a century (the bibliography cites Lewin and Lippitt, 1938). Styles overlap.

| Style | View of the subordinate | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Authoritarian | Needs direction and control; everything goes through the leader | Efficient and productive; useful in a crisis, such as a security manager in a major disaster | Stunts development; dependence and submission; later discontent, hostility, and aggression |
| Democratic | Fully capable; is guided, not ordered | High morale, motivation, a committed and cohesive group | Takes more time; effective but less efficient |
| Laissez-faire | Is ignored: “non-leadership” | Can work with a high-performing group | Mostly negative results; with poorly qualified staff, chaos and failure |

**1.13.4 Case — security director at a consumer goods company:** a multibillion-dollar division with theft and diversion of product. She came from a democratic boss and wanted to imitate him. She found:

- Security was risk-averse after clashes with management.
- Her two regional managers had less training and experience.
- Legal and the chief financial officer (CFO) replaced the contract guard force with a proprietary one. They sought lower cost and liability, and did not consult security.
- Officers were poorly trained, poorly uniformed, and poorly paid, with high turnover; losses rose with the change.

Response: authoritarian with field security and directive with the weakest manager. With the manager with potential, a mix of authoritarian and democratic. With top management, democratic and laissez-faire. Lesson: the situation dictates the style, without betraying one's values.

**Figure 1.2 Fatal Flaws that Derail Leaders**

| Flaw | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Failing to learn from mistakes | The biggest cause of failure; from mental sluggishness or arrogance |
| 2. Lack of basic interpersonal skills | Sins of commission (what was done) and omission (what was not done) |
| 3. Closed to new ideas | Risk aversion or arrogance |
| 4. Lack of accountability | Not taking blame or hoarding credit; with subordinates, with top management, with other groups, and in personal attitudes |
| 5. Lack of initiative | Risk aversion; not making good things happen |

### 1.14 Values

- **Values:** a company's foundational ideas that guide everyone's conduct. Sometimes they are used as mere marketing.
- Leader and followers have different values. The leader must be true to their own and sensitive to others'.
- Examples: authenticity, truth, justice or fairness, integrity, kindness, openness. Forcing people to act against them generates distrust and failure.
- **Values unite because they (10):** reinforce personal effectiveness; raise loyalty; facilitate consensus on goals; foster ethics; create norms of effort and care; reduce stress; generate pride; clarify expectations; drive teamwork; generate trustworthiness.

### 1.15 Ethical Leadership

- **Ethics:** the “rightness” (fairness and uprightness) of the leader's actions and motives.
- **Ethical leadership:** moral influence to do the right thing, in the right way, and for the right purpose.
- The first cause of departure is the direct boss; their lack of ethics often appears in exit interviews.
- **Triple Bottom Line:** People, Planet, Profit. It adds social and environmental impact to profit. The 2008 crisis, the 2010 BP spill, and climate change put the focus on ethics.

**Six factors of ethical leadership**

| Factor | Content |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Character | Perceived as trustworthiness, respect, responsibility, fairness, caring, and loyalty |
| 2. Actions | Appropriate means; against Machiavelli, the end does not justify all means |
| 3. Goals | Worthwhile goals that consider the group, the organization, and outsiders |
| 4. Honesty | Most requested and the basis of ethical leadership; it does not require revealing everything (that could be imprudent or illegal) |
| 5. Power | Formal ability to influence actions, attitudes, and beliefs |
| 6. Values | Justice, kindness, fairness, integrity, responsibility |

**Five bases of power:** referent (liking for the leader); expert (perceived competence); legitimate (formal authority); reward (bonuses, raises); coercive (punishing). Common mistake: believing that coercive power explains everything. Expert power is essential, and without referent and reward power there is no success.

- **Ethical dilemmas in security:** a vendor offers benefits to the decision maker; even gifts, tickets, or meals may violate rules. Harder: executives who believe they are above ethics.

**Case — expansion in Europe:** two vice presidents (VPs) were buying land for new facilities. After a complaint, internal audit and security found that some “site finders” were reselling land at inflated prices through shell companies. Part of the markup allegedly went to the VPs. The development team was fired; with the VPs there was hesitation because of their plausible deniability. A witness provided checks made out to their spouses and they were fired. Top management decided not to prosecute anyone, to protect reputation and business. The security manager accepted this because goals and values were being met. Lesson: the situation matters.

### 1.16 Summarizing Management and Leadership

- Technology brought the “death of distance.” The economy went from agrarian to manufacturing and today is knowledge-based.
- Managers and leaders help overcome unclear goals, confused direction, bad hires, low motivation, and poor business development.
- **Exam note:** in 1.4 the Industrial Revolution begins in the late 18th century. The summary, in contrast, places that shift in the early 20th century.

## Capítulo 2 — Decision-Making (págs. 33–62)

**Central idea:** Deciding means choosing among alternatives to solve a problem. Good decisions come from critical thinking, which must overcome biases, misused heuristics, and group dysfunctions. The book proposes an eight-step process (Walker 2010) and warns about the limits of big data and artificial intelligence.

### 2.0 Starting concepts (introduction)

- **Decision-making:** choosing between two or more alternatives. In 2.5: the process of choosing among the available alternatives.
- **Problem:** an obstacle that makes it harder to achieve a goal or purpose.
- Thomas Schelling, Nobel laureate: no one can list what would never occur to them (cited in Carter 2018). What we know also limits what we decide.
- **The Three Little Pigs (Steel 1922) as a decision:** how did each pig choose? Did they understand the threat? Did early success blind the wolf to the third challenge? Lesson: a good outcome does not prove a good process. Luck is the residue of planning, preparing, and executing well.
- Thinking is often biased, distorted, incomplete, uninformed, or prejudiced. If it fails, the decision does not solve the problem.

### 2.1 Critical Thinking

- **Critical thinking:** using knowledge and intelligence to arrive at reasonable, justifiable positions on which to decide.
- Universal qualities (Foundation for Critical Thinking 2020): clarity, accuracy, consistency, relevance, depth, breadth, integrity, and fairness.
- It shows in fact-seeking, curiosity, open-mindedness, and systematic analysis. It is demanded more in positions of greater responsibility.
- **Example — premises liability litigation:** experts for both sides interpret the same facts differently without lying. The expert's credibility usually wins. One will say that what was “reasonable and ordinary” was done; the other, that it was not. No company must do everything possible to avoid liability.
- Two experts can reach reasonable but opposing positions: absolutes are not sought (Lok 2020).

**2.1.1 What Critical Thinking Is Not**

| It is not | It is |
| --- | --- |
| Looking for faults or being smarter | A neutral process for evaluating claims, opinions, and facts |
| Making everyone think alike | Somewhat subjective objectivity, according to values |
| A threat to individuality | A personal process for evaluating beliefs |
| Mere intellectual skill | The most intelligent are not necessarily the best |
| Eliminating emotions | They are acknowledged; without them there is no humility, empathy, courage, perseverance, or integrity |
| Fabricating irrefutable arguments | Explaining the probable, not everything possible |
| A linear process that guarantees truth | Generalizations that adapt to the evidence; it can err |
| Creative thinking | An analytic effort that narrows down the probable |

**Figure 2.1 Creative vs. Critical Thinking (Walker 2010)**

| Creative: producing ideas | Critical: producing a solution |
| --- | --- |
| Generative | Analytic |
| Divergent | Convergent |
| Possibility | Probability |
| Suspends judgment | Makes judgment |
| Diffused | Focused |
| Subjective | Objective |
| Novelty | Reasoning |
| “Yes, and...” | “Yes, but...” |

- It seeks empirical evidence with predictive and explanatory power. It questions what is based on faith (“it's always been done this way”), anecdotes, or doubtful data.
- **Occam's Razor / Law of Parsimony:** the simplest explanation is usually the best. It is associated with the KISS principle (Keep It Simple, Stupid) (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy 2016).

### 2.2 The Process of Critical Thinking

- It starts with an attitude of inquiry that admits not understanding, something hard for an expert.
- It calls for curiosity, pertinent questions, evaluating the source, and suspending judgment until relevant facts are in hand.

**Ten questions to get started (Kahneman, Lovallo, and Sibony 2011):**

1. Do ambiguity or vagueness keep you from understanding the argument?
2. What is the source?
3. How confident are you in its accuracy?
4. What assumptions led you to the conclusion?
5. Is it consistent with the data?
6. Are there alternative explanations?
7. Did you evaluate the relevance, fairness, completeness, importance, and sufficiency of the reasons?
8. Where could your reasoning have gone wrong?
9. Did you miss anything?
10. What does being wrong imply?

- You must change position in the face of new evidence and always reevaluate.
- Some biases are understandable (teams, children), but not in the professional realm.
- **Exam note:** the book spells it “Siboney”; the co-author's correct surname is Sibony.

### 2.3 Cognitive Biases

- **Cognitive biases:** systematic errors in thinking that alter perception and lead to bad decisions.
- They do not come from low intelligence. They are shaped by experiences, education, family, upbringing, and professional environment. They are unconscious: accepting this is the first step.

| Bias | What it is | Example from the book |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Attribution error | Overvaluing the personal and ignoring the situation when judging others | My success is talent and my failure is bad luck; with others, the reverse |
| Overconfidence bias | Unrealistic view of one's own performance | — |
| Anchoring effect | Fixating only on the initial information | — |
| Selective perception | Interpreting facts according to one's own perception to explain an incident | — |
| Confirmation bias | Seeking only what confirms prior ideas; the one that gets in the way most | Security investigator who, with indications of guilt, dismisses evidence of innocence |
| Recency effect | Overvaluing the recent | Lists of best athletes with almost only those from the last 20 years |
| Selectivity error | Measuring with a partial criterion | Judging teams by Super Bowls (since 1967), even though professional football has existed since 1892 and has had champions since 1920 |
| Escalation of commitment | Persisting despite increasingly worse results | Casino gamblers, investors; security professional who doubles down on their plan after failures |
| Framing effect | How the data is presented changes the decision | Pfeffer and Sutton 2006: with “80% survival” most accept surgery; with “20% death,” most refuse |
| Hindsight bias, in 2.5 | Expecting a decision never to go wrong | — |

Source for the first five: Wood 2015.

### 2.4 Intuition and Heuristics: The Good and Bad of Critical Thinking

- **Intuition:** a combination of factors that result from accumulated judgment. It is not a gut feeling or a gift.
- Factors (Wikipedia 2020): common sense; experience; education; training; knowledge; skills; subconscious data; emotions; values and ethics.
- It arises quickly, without awareness of the process. It is more reliable in veterans than in a new hire or recent graduate.
- **Heuristics:** rules of thumb that simplify decisions. They are not optimal, but they suffice when the optimal is impossible or impractical. They reduce cognitive load (Wikipedia 2020).
- Example: a medical diagnostician recognizes tens of thousands of symptoms through stored knowledge. If no answer is found, they analyze further.
- Advantage: speed, and rationality if they rest on experience. Disadvantage: biases, and non-optimal or inaccurate solutions if data are missing.
- Caution: first impressions must be reviewed. In investments or ethical decisions, check against reflection or an expert.
- Distinguishing a good shortcut from a bias is itself a decision.

### 2.5 Decision-Making

**Common thinking problems (7):** overgeneralizing (always or never); making it personal; false comparisons; confusing cause and effect (correlation is not causation); believing only experts; saying only what pleases; believing in self-evident truths.

- Expecting a decision never to fail is hindsight bias. The goal is the most informed decision at that moment.
- Deciding is a process with selective search for evidence, limited by time and resources.
- Everything begins when a gap is detected between the current situation and the desired one: that is the problem.

**Two types of problems (Walker 2011)**

| Aspect | Well-structured | Ill-structured |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Definition | Straightforward, familiar, easy to define | New or unusual; ambiguous or incomplete information |
| Detection | Audit, review, or checklist | Does not fit rules or guides |
| Where it appears | Lower levels; repetitive | Bulk of business and security problems |
| Decision | Programmatic: repetitive and routine | Nonprogrammed: unique, custom-made |
| Tools | Rules, policies, guides, training | Reasoning, judgment, and experience |
| Example | Access control violation | Which candidate to hire |

**Four styles for ill-structured problems**

| Style | Tolerance for ambiguity | Traits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Directive | Low | Efficient, logical, fast; short term |
| Analytical | Higher | More information and alternatives; long goals with intermediate decisions |
| Conceptual | High | Creative, intuitive; long term |
| Behavioral | Low | Less intuitive; avoids conflict; seeks acceptance |

No style works for everything.

**Three decision conditions (Walker 2010)**

| Condition | Definition | Traits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Certainty | All outcomes are known | The most rational; the least realistic |
| Risk | The probability of failure can be estimated | The most common; requires experience and critical thinking |
| Uncertainty | There are no probability estimates | The hardest; invites hunches that bias; requires critical thinking |

- Complete rationality is a myth: uncertainty is the norm.
- **Bounded rationality:** deciding rationally within limits, such as not gathering all the information in time. It is not a failure of the decision maker.
- **Exam note:** the book's text box defines “Unbounded rationality”; judging by the text, the correct concept is bounded rationality.
- **Satisficing:** accepting “good enough” solutions that address the most urgent matters.

**Benefits of a systematic process (7):** avoids errors of judgment; raises vital questions; gathers relevant information; reviews assumptions, implications, and consequences; reasons out conclusions; achieves commitment; solves the problem.

### 2.6 Eight Step Decision-Making Process (Walker 2010)

| Step | Key points |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Identify the problem | The most important: gap between current and desired; pressure to act and its origin (top management, competition, environment) |
| 2. Identify decision criteria | Information and resources; limits (time, cost, expertise, imagination); deadlines; expected results; final authority? |
| 3. Develop alternatives | Feasibility matters, not the number; people with training, experience, and imagination |
| 4. Assign weights | Are they equal? Are time and budget sufficient? Who weighs? A decision maker removed from execution can claim plausible deniability |
| 5. Analyze the alternatives | Tools and data; do they fit and solve? Is anything missing? |
| 6. Select | Review biases, power, and politics; if they barely differ, decide and move on |
| 7. Communicate and implement | Key formal step of the good manager. Without commitment, the decision is only a wish; badly executed, it fails |
| 8. Evaluate effectiveness | Did it solve it? If yes, celebrate. If not: what failed, was there new data, was there a better option? |

- It is iterative, not linear.
- **If it was not solved, review (12):** credible source? scarce information? accurate? enough alternatives? weights misplaced? objectives clear? risks identified? assumptions? bias? consistent with the data? other explanations? reasons for a new solution?
- If the process fails, ask whether enough people took part.

### 2.7 Group Decision-Making

- **Group decision-making:** a collective activity in which several people interact at the same time to solve a problem.
- Even if the boss decides, it is advisable to open up the process. More people is not always better.
- **Crowdsourcing:** obtaining work, information, or opinions from a large group; basic in marketing. Limit: not everyone contributes and they bring their biases.
- **Causes of dysfunction:** illusion of invulnerability and unanimity; underestimating the opposition; pressure for conformity or loyalty; fear that silences contrary data. Herd mentality advances through emotion.

**Groupthink:** the desire for harmony produces an irrational decision; agreement is sought without critical evaluation. The book's definition: a pattern of self-deception, forced consent, and conformity to the group's values and ethics.

| Symptom (8) | Typical motto |
| --- | --- |
| Illusion of invulnerability | We can't lose |
| Belief in the group's morality | We're the good guys |
| Collective rationalization | We only do the right thing |
| Stereotypes of outsiders | They don't know |
| Belittling of opponents | They don't understand |
| Lack of internal criticism | Unquestioned loyalty |
| Illusion of unanimity | Everyone agrees |
| Pressure on dissenters | Get on board |

Effect: the group overestimates its power, morality, and legitimacy, and does not see that the decision is mistaken.

**Abilene paradox:** the group decides something contrary to the preferences of many or all of its members. No one wants to “rock the boat”; it stems from poor communication. Cause: action anxiety and fear of exclusion. Example: Deepwater Horizon; there were doubts about cost overruns and redundancies, but everyone assumed others saw better and no one objected.

| Difference | Groupthink | Abilene |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Individual desire | They act according to their conscious wishes | They act against their preferences |
| Feeling | Satisfied with the decision | Silent out of anxiety |

**Common conditions (Walker 2011) (12):** unsatisfactory situation; incomplete search for alternatives; assuming nonexistent knowledge; poorly identified objectives; failing to examine risks; failing to reconsider discarded alternatives; poor information search; bias in processing it; fear of alienation, isolation, separation, or the unknown; not discussing alternatives; wanting to appear cohesive until “failure”; believing that any decision beats none.

**How to avoid it (Walker 2011)**

| Measure | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Question the process | Was it effective? How do you know? |
| Balance advocacy with inquiry | When proposing, ask what others think |
| Detect motives or personalities that hijack the process | — |
| Avoid the spiral of silence | Do not keep minority opinions quiet |
| Look for a devil's advocate | Someone who says the uncomfortable thing |
| Allow unpopular positions | No “with us or against us” |
| Recognize the weight of leadership | Without a leader, someone else fills the vacuum; the leader does not show a preference, to avoid a self-fulfilling prophecy |
| Turn to outside experts | They contribute what the team does not know |

- Consulting one-on-one yields more candid opinions; in a group, a few can dominate.
- Groups are useful for brainstorming. Choosing the path is more art than science.

### 2.8 Big Data and Decision-Making

- **Big data:** an enormous volume of quantifiable information analyzed with very sophisticated processing.
- Data grew exponentially in the last five years. Not all of it is equally valuable: even a multiple regression requires choosing independent variables.
- Criticism: another form of scientific management; it is a mistake to replace the decision maker. Its value depends on the problem and the analysis.
- It detects correlations, but does not explain why they matter (Lohr 2012).
- **Artificial intelligence (AI):** systems that perform tasks of human intelligence: visual perception, speech recognition, decision-making, and translation.
- Machine learning depends on the knowledge of whoever programs it. Self-driving car: hit the object or swerve and hit something else? A novice and a race driver would decide differently: who enters the data matters.
- Big data and AI have limits, like people.

### 2.9 Summary

**Figure 2.2 Factors involved in decision-making (Walker 2011):** decision-making approach; types of problems and decisions; biases and errors; decision maker's style; decision conditions; 8-step process.

- The higher the position, the riskier, less structured, and more highly valued the decisions.
- **Overconfidence:** the most frequent and potentially catastrophic judgment problem. We overestimate knowledge and control, and underestimate complexity and risk.

| Stated confidence | Actual accuracy (Campbell, Whitehead, and Finkelstein 2009) |
| --- | --- |
| 65–75% | Less than half the time |
| 100% | Around 80% |

- Fast and frugal is efficient; logical and reflective is usually more effective.
- Essential: focus on what is important and identify the vital questions. Then, open-mindedness, logic, and consistency in handling information, creating alternatives, and anticipating implications.
- **Two conclusions:** (1) critical thinking and a logical process are almost always needed. (2) Context changes: “on time and good” is worth more than “late and excellent.” Communicating and executing is crucial.
- There will be mistakes: listen and learn. The good human decision maker will always have value.

### Appendix 2A: Case Studies

- **Case study:** a compressed version of a real management problem. It trains analysis and decision-making; it may include fiction to protect anonymity. Every MBA (Master of Business Administration) uses them.
- This case, fictional but based on facts, closes the ASIS/IE Business School program “Effective Management for Security Professionals” (Walker 2010).

**Situation:** you are the new vice president and chief security officer (CSO) of a global pharmaceutical company. The position is new and reports to the chief executive officer (CEO), a woman. She asks you to evaluate security across the whole company and propose changes.

- The business lines reject centralizing and prefer to manage their own security.
- Corporate security carries staff, policies, systems, and programs. The lines fund and operate field security, with a “dotted-line” reporting relationship.
- The CEO blames inconsistent execution and a lack of time and resources. Legal warns: this is your first big test.
- You come from a smaller company and another sector: what worked before may not work here.

**Findings:**

- It grows through mergers and acquisitions, with a strong brand and leading products, but security did not keep pace with the increase in risks.
- Security vendors are inconsistent and expensive.
- Security does not take part in the due diligence of acquisitions.
- Research and development (R&D) fears espionage and theft of intellectual property.
- A competitor investigated an espionage case poorly: lawsuit, image disaster, stock drop, and replacement of the CEO and its CSO.
- Animal rights activists in the U.S. and Europe threaten violence over alleged mistreatment in R&D.
- Organized crime in Asia copies drugs: lower revenue, marketing and image problems, and more legal liability.
- Crisis and business continuity plans are incomplete and incompatible, despite four crises:

| Crisis | Place |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Bombing without fatalities at an R&D center | Eastern Europe |
| 2. Plant closure due to contamination of unknown origin, with impact on supply | Western Europe |
| 3. Workplace violence with injured employees | South America |
| 4. A former employee killed his supervisor; negligent security lawsuit | Distribution center in the U.S. |

- Previous attempts to coordinate failed for lack of support. The former boss clashed with human resources, legal, public affairs, finance, R&D, and operations. He preferred to give orders rather than build support.
- The staff (former military and former police) has a reputation for being comfortable, but their evaluations are brilliant and they receive merit raises.
- There is a credibility gap; in hard times, the value and resources of security will be questioned.
- The uncoordinated program threatens the company, its stakeholders, and your future.

**The book's analysis questions:** (1) What internal challenges does the CSO face? (2) And external ones? (3) What options does the CSO have? (4) What is possible and what plans would you recommend, and why?

## Capítulo 3 — What Strategy Is and Why It's Important (págs. 63–96)

**Central idea:** Strategy is a set of goal-oriented plans and actions for competing differently from rivals. It arises from a strategic management process that runs from vision to evaluation and adaptation. Security does not set corporate strategy, but it must align its own and not confuse it with tactics.

### 3.1 The Concept of Strategy

- **Stakeholders:** every party with an interest in the company: shareholders, employees, suppliers, communities, business associations, and local, state, and national governments.
- **Key questions:** 1) what the consumer wants and how to attract them; 2) how the industry will change; 3) what actions to take; 4) what resources are needed; 5) what advantages to gain and how sustainable they are; 6) above all, how to grow.
- **Strategic management process:** the process that answers those questions and generates the strategy.
- **Military origin:** analyzing the competitive situation and designing the strategic response.

| Source | Definition of strategy |
| --- | --- |
| Book | Goal-oriented plans and actions that allow better competition |
| Barney and Hesterly (2015) | A theory of how to achieve competitive advantage |
| Thompson, Peteraf, Gamble, and Strickland (2016) | Management actions to outperform the competition |
| Synthesis | A plan to reach the desired market position, attract customers, and meet goals |

| Strategy | Tactics |
| --- | --- |
| A plan to achieve a goal (“take the hill”) | Adaptive actions during the effort |
| Gives direction and coordination | Must align with the strategy, but is not it |

- **Workplace violence case:** proposing only a policy, a procedure, or an access control is offering tactics. Together they form a strategy if they integrate a coordinated plan that:
  1. Provides clarity of purpose and direction.
  2. Identifies the specific roles.
  3. Defines what to do and how.
  4. Makes it possible to make decisions.
  5. Supports the organization's goals.
- **Budgeting ≠ strategic planning.** It is strategic only if it allocates resources according to objectives and goals already set. Order: objectives and goals → strategies → budget.
- **The Economist (2012):** strategy “is king”; it is what makes or breaks the company.
- Luck explains short-lived successes. The long term requires a well-designed, well-executed, and evolving strategy.
- **Security's role:** the overall strategy belongs to top management. Security aligns its strategies, objectives, and goals with the company's.

**Figure 3.1 Strategy Quiz (Hiam 1990).** Are these true statements about strategy?

1. Top management defines the direction and communicates it to the whole organization.
2. Every manager knows the details of the overall strategy.
3. Managers communicate it in their areas to guide actions.
4. Employees know it is the basis for evaluating future opportunities.
5. It is developed independently of long-term planning.
6. It determines plans and guides resource allocation.
7. It is based on analysis and assumptions, not on plans.
8. Each function has its own strategies.
9. Functional strategies are consistent with the overall one.
10. Each function is evaluated on strategic and operational (or financial) performance.

The book's answer: all are true. Lesson: even senior executives confuse tactics with strategies.

- **International expansion case:** excluding security when choosing geographic areas makes it impossible to detect risks that can sink the business. The failure would be management's, not the risks'.
- **Choices for competing:** lower costs or superior products; narrow or broad offering; local or regional market, or national, multinational, or global.
- Better a good strategy well executed than an excellent one badly executed.

### 3.2 Strategy Is About Competing Differently

- Copying rivals rarely works: strategies are based on one's own capabilities that are hard to imitate.
- **Porter (1996):** compete in ways that rivals do not want or cannot match.
- Rivals can share the same generic strategy. The winner is the one who stands out to the customer and occupies a less crowded position.
- Knowing what not to do matters as much as what to do.

**Figure 3.2 Factors Related to Strategy** (patterns of action that define strategy):

1. Strengthen market position.
2. Manage key activities.
3. Create alliances or partnerships.
4. Win sales and share through quality.
5. Win sales and share through lower costs.
6. Enter new product or geographic markets.
7. Defend markets from external threats.
8. Acquire other companies or merge with them.
9. Build competitive resources and capabilities.

Success depends on the quality of the distinctive resources and capabilities.

### 3.3 Vision, Mission, Values, and the Strategic Management Process

- Choosing a strategy means deciding without certainty (see Chapter 2). It rests on assumptions about the market and rivals; if they fail, the strategy fails.
- **Definition:** a sequence of analyses and decisions about plans and actions that create the means (the strategy) to achieve a goal.

| Step | Linear strategic management process |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Establish vision, mission, and core values |
| 2 | Set objectives to measure performance and progress |
| 3 | Formulate the strategy |
| 4 | Execute it with efficiency (time and costs) and effectiveness (results) |
| 5 | Monitor, evaluate, and make corrective adjustments |

- Few employees know the vision, mission, and values. The security professional must master them to be a business partner.

**3.3.1 The Vision Statement**

- **Strategic vision:** management's aspirations for the company's future and the direction for achieving them. It must be compelling, specific to the business, and distinctive.
- **Organizational culture:** the values and behaviors that create the organization's own social and psychological environment.
- Examples: Johnson & Johnson (2020), employees who combine experiences for a healthier world; Amazon (2021), the most customer-centric company on Earth.
- Communicating it generates commitment and inspires stakeholders.
- **Why it matters (Thompson, Peteraf et al. 2016):**
  1. It crystallizes executives' idea of the long term.
  2. It reduces aimless decisions.
  3. It wins employee support.
  4. It orients middle managers on departmental objectives and strategies.
  5. It prepares the organization for the future.

**3.3.2 The Mission Statement**

- **Mission:** long-term purpose; it explains why the company exists. Making money is an objective, not a mission.

| Vision | Mission |
| --- | --- |
| Future aspirations: “where we are going” | Current scope and purpose: “who we are, what we do, and why we are here” |

- **A good mission:** 1) identifies products or services; 2) specifies the needs it satisfies; 3) identifies customers or markets; 4) defines how it satisfies the customer; 5) distinguishes it from rivals; 6) clarifies intentions to stakeholders.
- PepsiCo example (2020): leading international brand in convenient snacks and beverages, with honesty, value, and quality.
- Vision, mission, and values form the “three-legged stool” of the process.

**3.3.3 The Values Statement**

- **Core values:** beliefs, traits, and norms of conduct expected of personnel in pursuing the vision and mission.
- They start from universal beliefs (justice, fairness, trust, honesty), but each company sets its own. If they work, they hardly change.
- **Traits:** they are the expected conduct; they are integrated into the culture if top management backs them; they fit with vision, mission, and strategy.
- **Organizational culture (second definition):** expectations, experiences, philosophy, and values that guide internal and external conduct.
- **Walmart case (2020):** vision, to be the destination for saving; mission, to save people money so they can live better. Values: service to the customer, respect for the individual, strive for excellence, and act with integrity. They reflect Sam Walton and the slogan “Save money. Live better.” Lesson: total consistency.
- **Ben & Jerry's (B&J) case (Page and Katz 2012):** salaries by fixed ratio, with no ranges or adjustment for education or experience. It could not retain executives and tried to impose the model on its suppliers. Costs rose; it was sold to Unilever in 2001.
- Unilever kept the values, changed the compensation, removed management levels, and used its purchasing power. Profitability returned. Lesson: values can clash with economic reality.

### 3.4 Objectives and Goals

| Objective | Goal |
| --- | --- |
| A measure of progress toward the goal | Desired result |

- Vision, mission, and values are broad and subjective. Objectives are specific, measurable, and tied to the mission. Short term (quarterly) or long term (one to three years).
- Top-down process: top management → businesses, product lines, departments, and units.
- **Stretch objectives:** realistic, challenging, and attainable. They reveal valuable critical thinkers.
- **Security:** more training or programs for goals such as fewer crimes, fewer access control violations, or fewer workers' compensation claims.
- **The effort must:** 1) make everyone more inventive; 2) create urgency to improve finances and position; 3) make the company more intentional; 4) avoid inertia and complacency.
- Unrealistic objectives, or ones that ignore the capabilities of each function, generate conflict. Interdepartmental communication is vital.
- **Ex-convicts case:** security investigated every employee crime and saw physical and sexual assaults rise. With tax, human resources (HR), legal, and operations it found the cause.
- HR was hiring newly released ex-convicts in exchange for a tax incentive. Nearly all those investigated came from that program.
- Ex-convicts with violent crimes were excluded. At six months there were still assaults, but fewer.
- **Lessons:** 1) coordinate objectives across functions and with the values; 2) do not create contradictory objectives (tax savings versus legal liability and turnover); 3) the environment changes and you must adapt.

**Common business objectives:** 1) x% market share; 2) x increase in annual revenue; 3) lower costs than rivals; 4) outperform rivals in performance, quality, or service; 5) x% of revenue from products of the last five years; 6) broader or deeper technology; 7) broader product line; 8) better-known or more powerful brand; 9) better national or global sales and distribution; 10) launch products before rivals.

- Strategic objectives weigh more than financial ones. Financial ones are lagging indicators; strategic ones, leading indicators.
- **Example:** a milestone payment from a partner is nonrecurring revenue. It is not a basis for strategies or goals.
- Objectives are broken down by unit, including security. The right strategy aligns external challenges with internal capabilities.

### 3.5 The Role of External and Internal Analyses

| External analysis | Internal analysis |
| --- | --- |
| Opportunities and threats; how the competition will evolve and respond | Strengths and weaknesses; resources and capabilities; areas to improve |

- **SWOT (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats):** external and internal situational analysis. It depends on context; one from three or six months ago may not be useful.
- **Purposes:** 1) understand challenges and capacity to respond; 2) organize internal and external data; 3) provide a fresh perspective; 4) draw conclusions for the strategy.
- **Figure 3.3 Components of a SWOT Analysis (Walker 2013):** situational analysis → internal (strengths, weaknesses) and external (opportunities, threats).

| Component | Definition | Ten examples from the book |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strengths | Internal traits that give an advantage: tangible and intangible attributes and capabilities | Culture; core competencies; staff education and expertise; differentiated products; brand and reputation; cost advantage; solid balance sheet; geographic coverage; strong supply chain; alliances and joint ventures |
| Weaknesses | Controllable internal traits that put the company at a disadvantage; they hold back goals and growth | Dysfunction (turnover, low morale); resources misaligned with success factors; narrow line; weak brand; few customers; high costs; weak balance sheet; inferior intellectual capital; little management depth; idle plant capacity |
| Opportunities | Attractive, contextual external factors the company can exploit to become more profitable | Gaining share; growing demand; new geographies; new product markets; expanding lines; online sales; backward or forward integration; companies to acquire; alliance partners; emerging technologies |
| Threats | Uncontrollable, contextual external elements; they put profitability or survival at risk and, combined with weaknesses, increase vulnerability | More competition; slower growth; new entrants; substitutes; more buyer or supplier power; shifting preferences; adverse demographics (birth rate below replacement); restrictive trade; costly regulation (Sarbanes-Oxley); pandemics, terrorism, war |

| Benefits | Limitations |
| --- | --- |
| Understand the competitive environment | Subjectivity |
| Anticipate the future, not predict it | Omissions |
| Set goals and objectives | Snapshot of a moment (here and now) |
| Identify capabilities and competencies | Errors: overestimating strengths, minimizing weaknesses, not understanding opportunities, not imagining threats, overvaluing one area |
| Align strengths with challenges; provide evidence for adapting; create strategies and anticipate their tactics | — |

| Do | Don't |
| --- | --- |
| Be analytical; write down all ideas | Disguise weaknesses |
| Choose participants well | Forget that the external is uncontrollable and requires adapting |
| Think inside and outside the box | Exclude anyone who thinks differently |
| Recognize the context; assume everything changes | Ignore how choices affect plans; use it to assign blame |

- Its value: it is balanced, reveals hidden issues, gives a shared vision, and is simple.
- **Final realities:** strengths found the strategy; weaknesses that hold back opportunities are corrected; opportunities that match strengths are exploited; threats are addressed to reduce risk.
- **Confirmation bias:** dismissing data that contradict prior beliefs.
- **Security:** SWOT for functional strategies; mini-SWOT to decide, brainstorm, or assess a new position.

### 3.6 Crafting a Strategy

- A linear process with a clear beginning and end, for corporate, business, or functional strategy.
- **Strategic hows:** positioning; attracting customers; competing with rivals; achieving goals; seizing growth opportunities; responding to economic and market changes.
- Choosing is collaborative, at all levels. The strategy must make it possible to do things differently.

| Level | Definition | Content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Corporate-level strategy (Surbhi 2017) | Decisions that affect the whole organization and determine which industries to compete in | Several businesses and markets; growth, stability, and renewal |
| Business-level strategy (Surbhi 2017) | Method for executing actions that meet that business's goals | Cost leadership or differentiation |
| Functional strategy | Managing an activity to support the business strategy | Security, HR, finance, accounting, marketing |

- **Corporate strategy does one of three things:** 1) choose industries and mode of entry (new business, merger, acquisition, partnership, alliance); 2) share resources among businesses or brands; 3) improve joint performance by expanding or shrinking scope, often with mergers and acquisitions.

| Cost leadership | Differentiation |
| --- | --- |
| Spending at the lowest possible cost for the result | Takes on higher costs for unique, quality products |
| It is not making things cheap; it invests in what makes money | Charges a premium price |
| Southwest Airlines, McDonald's, Walmart | Apple, Nordstrom, BMW |

- **Security:** a support function (secondary activities of the value chain). It must align upward and downward. Otherwise, it receives no resources; if it gets in the way of operations, it is not followed.
- **Industrial plant case:** a non-employee crossed a poorly secured checkpoint and assaulted his ex-partner. Security proposed three measures together: improve the access system, new procedures, and change the officer staff.
- It obtained funds by showing greater operational effectiveness and less legal liability. Lesson: the strategy must be “sold” to top management.

| Competitive advantage | Comparative advantage |
| --- | --- |
| Only the whole business achieves it | A function such as security achieves it |
| Producing more efficiently (costs) or differently (differentiation) with higher margins | Better internal systems that reduce costs and raise profitability |

- A functional strategy is never the basis of competitive advantage. Security's can provide comparative advantage.

### 3.7 Strategy Implementation

Steps for management (Walker 2011):

1. Effective communication throughout the company.
2. Staff with the necessary skills.
3. Strengthen supporting resources and capabilities.
4. Sufficient resources for critical activities.
5. Policies and procedures that facilitate execution.
6. Organize the work according to best practices.
7. Information and operating systems.
8. Motivate and tie incentives to objectives.
9. A culture favorable to execution.
10. Internal leadership that drives implementation.

Then it is evaluated. If it works, celebrate; if not, look for causes and change the strategy or the execution. Always apply the lessons learned.

### 3.8 Strategy Evolves Over Time

- It must adapt to new rivals, products, and demands. Modifying it is usually enough.
- **Triggers:** market changes; new technology; unexpected moves by rivals; new buyer needs; emerging opportunities; new ideas.
- **Apple case:** highly differentiated and with little share in computers, it created its smartphone without abandoning differentiation. Pricing power shifted from the carriers to the manufacturers, previously mere commodities.
- Lesson: strategy is partly proactive and partly reactive, never static.
- **Exam note:** the book calls Apple's “the world's first smartphone.” There were smartphones before the iPhone; remember that it was the initial disruptor.

**3.8.1 The Importance of Adaptive Strategy**

- Besides crises (pandemics, natural disasters, terrorism, cyber threats), the number one threat is slow economic growth. It reflects bad strategy and puts pressure on profits.
- The greatest risk by which stakeholders judge management is sustaining growth with profits, not growing for growth's sake.
- That the final strategy differs from the one designed is a success of the process, not an error.

| Type | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Intended strategy | The one the company planned to follow |
| Emergent strategy | The one adapted to the situation |
| Realized strategy | The one it actually follows |

- **Johnson & Johnson (J&J) case:** it sold antiseptic gauze and plasters. Because of complaints of irritation it added talc, and customers asked for it alone: Johnson's Powder, later Johnson's Baby Powder.
- Because of lawsuits accusing talc of causing cancer, it replaced it with cornstarch. Later it created bandages that led to Band-Aid, its best-selling consumer product, and entered pharmaceuticals.
- Lesson: the realized strategy captures emergent opportunities.

### 3.9 What Makes a Strategy a Winner

| Test (Walker 2011) | Question | Requires |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fitness Test | Does it fit the situation? | External fit with the market and internal fit with resources and capabilities; dynamic |
| Competitiveness Test | Does it improve the competitive position? | Benefit over rivals through cost or novelty, even without competitive advantage; the greater and more durable, the better |
| Performance Test | Does it deliver superior performance? | Market position relative to rivals; profitability and financial strength |

- If any fails, it must be changed. Hand-in-glove analogy: internal capabilities with external challenges.
- **Exam note:** “Fitness Test” and “fit test” are the same test; the book uses both names.

### 3.10 Competitive Advantage

- **Definition:** the ability to give the consumer more value with lower prices or with more benefits that justify higher prices. It is the final goal of the process, although it is poorly understood.
- **Economic value or value proposition:** perceived benefits versus the cost to the customer.

| VRIN (Barney and Hesterly 2015) | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Valuable | Measured by financial value |
| Rare | Few companies have that capability |
| Inimitable | Others cannot replicate it |
| Non-substitutable | The customer has no alternative of equivalent value |

- Gathering and sustaining all four is hard. The advantage may not last because others invest in the market.
- **iPhone case:** it was the initial disruptor. Rivals offered lower prices or different features and today Samsung leads in share.
- Being first is not an advantage in itself, nor does being second imply not having one. The iPhone keeps its advantage because the customer perceives VRIN and pays a premium: price-value inducement.
- **Luxury cars case:** with equal prices, the perception of quality or service decides. Perception can be an advantage.

### 3.11 Summary: only what is new

- Outside the logo, achieving and sustaining competitive advantage is not always possible.
- The process seeks a good-enough strategy (satisficing) that improves competitiveness and profitability.
- Corporate strategies cited here: vertical integration, diversification, partnerships and alliances, mergers and acquisitions.
- Implementation requires resources for supporting structures, management control policies, and competitive compensation.
- **Economic value:** what is paid for a good or service above its cost of production. It changes with the price or quality of similar items. It combines tangible factors (quality) and intangible ones (brand, perception).
- **Exam note:** the summary says the mission neither improves nor worsens performance. In 3.3.3 it is said that vision and mission should improve it. What is measurable is the objectives.
- **Exam note:** 3.10 calls competitive advantage the final goal. The summary gives as the ultimate objective a better competitive position, “if not” competitive advantage.

## Capítulo 4 — Evaluating the External Environment (págs. 97–128)

**Central idea:** Before choosing a strategy you must diagnose the external environment: the macro environment and the industry with its competition. The tools are the six sectors of the macro environment, the five forces, strategic groups, competitor analysis, and key success factors. It all ends in a judgment about the attractiveness of the industry.

### 4.0 Introduction

- **Two facets of the situation (Thompson, Peteraf, Gamble, and Strickland 2016):** external environment (competition in the industry) and internal (resources and capabilities). A good diagnosis makes it possible to adjust both. The internal one is covered in the next chapter.
- **External business environment:** six sectors: economic, demographic, sociocultural, political-legal, technological, and international-global.
- **The security professional must be able to:**
  1. Recognize macro environment factors with strategic weight, such as risks (Chapter 2).
  2. Diagnose competition in the industry with analytical tools.
  3. Map the market positions of groups of rivals.
  4. Judge with several frameworks whether the industry offers attractive growth and profitability.
- **Industry:** a group of companies related by their main activity. Industries are grouped into sectors, which indicate what they sell.
- **Security is twofold:** a function inside companies (it reduces risks to the business, employees, and stakeholders) and a separate industry (companies that sell security products or services). Both must analyze opportunities and threats.

**Figure 4.1 From Thinking Strategically to Selection and Business Model:**

1. Think strategically about the external environment.
2. Think strategically about the internal environment.
3. Form a vision of where the company should go.
4. Identify promising strategic options.
5. Choose the best strategy and business model.

- **Opportunities:** positive external trends. **Threats:** negative external trends.
- A good analysis makes it possible to be more proactive than reactive, anticipate changes, and neutralize rivals.
- **Evidence (Barney and Hesterly 2015):** those who do formal external analysis have better financial performance, measured by return on assets or higher profit.

### 4.1 External Competition

- Very high external threats approach perfect competition; very low ones, monopoly.

| Structure | Firms | Products | Entry and exit | Examples | Performance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Perfect competition | Many | Homogeneous | Cheap | Crude oil | Competitive parity: price minus cost of exploration and production |
| Monopolistic competition | Many | Not homogeneous | Cheap | Toothpaste, shampoo, golf balls | Possible advantage through cost or differentiation |
| Oligopoly | Few | Homogeneous | Expensive | U.S. autos and steel in the 1950s; U.S. breakfast cereals today (4 companies, about 90%) | Possible advantage |
| Monopoly | One | — | Very expensive | Standard Oil; AT&T | Dominates the sector |

- **Monopoly:** one company and its products dominate a sector. Antitrust laws have largely prohibited them.
- **Cases:** Standard Oil, sued in 1906 and broken up into more than 30 companies. AT&T, a regulated natural monopoly, separated from the Bell System in 1984. Microsoft lost a monopoly trial, appealed, settled, and shared interfaces.
- **Competitive parity, according to the book:** optimal spending on brand and advertising to match competitors.
- **Exam note:** that definition is of a marketing budget. In the oil example it is used as performance equal to rivals, with no advantage.
- **Exam note:** the book calls monopoly the “extreme form of competition”; strictly it is almost its absence.
- **Competitive advantage (the chapter's definition):** a rare and often unsustainable situation that makes products more desirable than rivals'.
- **Environmental threat:** an external actor that competes and reduces performance: raises costs or lowers revenue.
- Two relevant factors: 1) macro environment; 2) immediate industry and competitive environment.

### 4.2 Macro Environment

- **Definition:** the industry's broad context, which the company does not control and must react to. Forecasts are not facts; the analysis must be flexible.
- Six sectors (Walker 2011), with positive or negative impact:

| Sector | What it covers | Elements or examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 4.2.1 Economic | Economic health of the country or region | Interest rates; exchange rate and value of the dollar; budget deficit or surplus; trade deficit or surplus; inflation; GDP and GNP; consumer confidence, spending, and debt; employment and unemployment |
| 4.2.2 Demographic | Distribution of the population; whom a product appeals to and how many customers there are | Gender; age; income; ethnic composition; education; family composition; location; birth rate; employment status |
| 4.2.3 Socio-cultural | What the culture sees as right, acceptable, or fashionable | Traditions; lifestyles; values; attitudes; beliefs; tastes; behavior patterns |
| 4.2.4 Political-legal | Laws, regulations, rulings, and political forces | Tax laws; industry regulation; trade treaties; patents; immigration; product labeling and safety |
| 4.2.5 Technological | Innovations that create opportunities and threats | Lasers; robotics; biotechnology; medicine; consumer electronics; telecommunications; artificial intelligence |
| 4.2.6 International-global | Worldwide influence | More global trade; exchange rates; emerging economies; treaties (NAFTA, EU, ASEAN); creation of the WTO |

Acronyms: NAFTA (North American Free Trade Agreement), EU (European Union), ASEAN (Association of Southeast Asian Nations), WTO (World Trade Organization).

- **Recession:** negative GDP growth for two or more consecutive quarters. **Depression:** a severe, multiyear recession.
- **Gross domestic product (GDP):** the market value of what is produced in a year by citizens and noncitizens, within the borders.
- **Gross national product (GNP):** adds the net activity of nationals abroad. The U.S. dropped it in 1991 in favor of GDP, which almost all countries and the International Monetary Fund (IMF) use.
- **Business cycle:** the natural rise and fall of growth in four stages: expansion, peak, contraction, and trough.
- Economic challenge: finding reliable data and interpreting it (what it means, whether it is good or bad, how it affects).
- **Demographic trends to interpret:** relevant population increasing; aging; urbanization; more women working; late families and fewer replacement births; more people over 30 than under; more education; more women than men with a college degree; more people with disabilities; Hispanic minority as the largest and growing; more obesity.
- **Sociocultural:** opinions and values reflect freedoms, work ethic, and equality of opportunity; they influence how people work and buy. Key when operating in several countries.
- **Political-legal:** the U.S. has rigorous antitrust and environmental regulation; in China companies operate at the government's will, with looser environmental standards.
- **Technological:** among the most dynamic sectors; it can create new industries. **Pending:** the book announces “two areas” affected, but names only research and product development (nanotechnology).
- **Global, risk events:** civil wars, coups, crime, terrorism, wars, famines, pandemics, recessions, and economic collapses.
- The immediate industry and its competition influence success more than the macro environment.

### 4.3 and 4.4 Immediate Industry and Competitive Environment; The Five Forces Framework

- **Five forces framework:** identifies the competitive forces that shape each industry. It reveals its structure and guides strategy; its strength varies by industry.

**Figure 4.2 Five Forces Framework for Analysis (Porter 1990):**

| Force | Actor |
| --- | --- |
| 1 Rivalry among existing firms | Competitors in the industry, at the center |
| 2 Threat of new entrants | Potential entrants |
| 3 Threat of substitute products or services | Substitutes |
| 4 Bargaining power of suppliers | Suppliers |
| 5 Bargaining power of buyers | Buyers |

- **Exam note:** the book cites Porter (1990), an article on the competitive advantage of nations. The model is earlier; the author is Michael Porter.
- **Three steps:** 1) identify the parties and pressure factors of each force; 2) rate each force as strong, moderate, or weak; 3) see how they support profitability.

#### 4.4.1 Force 1: Industry Rivalry

It is the strongest force. **It increases when:**

1. Demand grows slowly or falls: aggressive discounts, promotions, and marketing.
2. Switching brands costs little. Switching costs include money, time, inconvenience, and psychological cost. With industrial inputs (a sawmill that sells to a paper mill) the buyer avoids switching.
3. There is little differentiation: less loyalty and purchasing on price.
4. There is excess inventory or capacity, or the product is perishable (fresh food) or seasonal (summer clothing). High fixed or storage costs push discounts.
5. There are more competitors and of similar size; risk of a price war.
6. Rivals differ in objectives and strategies; some enter global markets with low prices even while losing money.
7. Exit barriers are high: assets hard to sell, labor protection, or owners with personal motives.

- **It decreases under the opposite conditions**, such as highly differentiated products (Vance 2016).
- **Levels:** strong, squeezed margins; moderate (the norm), healthy competition and acceptable margins; weak, no downward pressure.
- Weapons besides price: promotions, rebates, cheap credit, assortment, quality, features, new generations, and services.

#### 4.4.2 Force 2: Threat of New Entrants

- They add rivals and capacity and lower pricing power. The mere credible threat pushes prices down.
- It depends on the reaction of the incumbents and on barriers to entry. Their height depends on the entrant's resources and capabilities.
- The threat is low if incumbents can retaliate with discounts or if barriers are high:

| Barrier to entry | Key idea |
| --- | --- |
| Economies of scale | The entrant enters big or accepts higher costs |
| Other cost advantages | Patents, proprietary technology, exclusive suppliers, locations, depreciated plants; learning/experience curve: the steeper, the more advantage |
| Brand loyalty | Requires heavy advertising or discounts from the entrant |
| Patents and intellectual property | Example: pharmaceuticals |
| Network effects | Example: video game consoles |
| High capital | Plants, equipment, initial advertising, inventories, start-up |
| Access to distribution | Reluctant distributors; buying access (forward integration) raises costs |
| Restrictive regulation | Banking, nuclear, utilities, radio and TV, wine and liquor, food, pharmaceuticals, airlines, railroads |
| Restrictive trade | Tariffs and restrictions (The Strategic CFO 2020) |

- Barriers change over time. Companies with resources and brand can leap over them.
- **Samsung case:** with a brand in televisions, DVDs, and electronics, it entered smartphones and overtook Apple in leadership. It won more through effective pricing in a new market than through differentiation.

#### 4.4.3 Force 3: Substitute Products

- Products from similar industries that the buyer accepts as a replacement. Example: Dr. Pepper in place of Coca-Cola or Pepsi; for all soft drinks, tea, juice, or water.
- Soft drink makers launched their own substitutes to reach those who avoid calories.
- **Exam note:** Dr. Pepper versus Coca-Cola is more of a rivalry within the same industry; tea, juice, or water are the typical substitute.
- **The pressure is strong when:**
  1. Substitutes are available and cheap, which cap prices and profits.
  2. They look equal or better in quality, performance, or design.
  3. Switching costs little: switching Starbucks coffee is easy; switching car brands is not.
- Before evaluating: identify substitutes, their geography and distribution, and whether they meet the same needs or prices.

#### 4.4.4 Force 4: Supplier Bargaining Power

- They charge more and pass on costs. Example: manufacturers of famous brands impose price and shelf position on small retailers.
- **It is greater when:**
  1. Demand is high and the product is scarce.
  2. The product is differentiated; with commodities (precious metals, chocolate, sugar, coffee) the power disappears.
  3. Switching suppliers is expensive.
  4. A few large companies dominate the supplier sector.
  5. The buyer does not integrate backward: McDonald's, the world's largest buyer of beef, does not produce it; a pharmaceutical company could make its own ingredients.
  6. There are no good substitutes for the input.
  7. The buyer is not an important customer.
- If the input is essential, the supplier has pricing power.

#### 4.4.5 Force 5: Buyer Bargaining Power

- It depends on their price sensitivity and their dependence on the seller. Walmart, Best Buy, and Home Depot demand concessions for volume and access to customers.
- Supermarkets charge promotional support and slotting fees: fixed payments for placing a brand on the shelf or in the best spot.
- **Pending:** the book says the buyer is strong if the price “does not matter” to their production costs; the phrase is confusing.
- **It is greater when:**
  1. Demand falls and supply is in excess (housing in a crisis).
  2. Differentiation is weak.
  3. Switching sellers is cheap because there are substitutes.
  4. Buyers are large or few.
  5. They can integrate backward (brewers that make cans or bottles).
  6. They can postpone or cancel the purchase.
  7. They have low profits.
- A luxury clothing manufacturer has less power with Nordstrom, Macy's, or Bloomingdale's than with small boutiques.

### 4.5 Matching Strategy to Competitive Conditions

- The most intense force sets the downward pressure on profitability.

| Situation | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| All strong | Unacceptable profitability; causes exits |
| Three or more strong | Unattractive; examples: airlines, apparel, tires |
| Moderate or weak impact | Attractive; reasonable profits |
| All weak | Everyone earns a decent profit |

- **Exam note:** the book calls both all strong and all weak “the rarest.” It also says that with all weak the outlook can be bleak (buggy whips in the face of the automobile).
- The company must insulate itself from the pressures and shift the factors of the forces in its favor: 1) identify pressures; 2) understand the competition; 3) apply the right strategy.
- **Industry life cycle:** development, rapid growth, maturity, saturation, slow growth, stagnation, and decline. Technology and offshoring and outsourcing also change the industry.
- **Exam note:** the text box defines it with growth, peak, contraction, and trough, which are stages of the business cycle.

**Most influential driving forces (Thompson, Peteraf, Gamble, and Strickland 2016):**

1. Changes in long-term growth.
2. Increasing globalization.
3. Changes in buyer demographics.
4. New technological capabilities, such as artificial intelligence.
5. Innovation in manufacturing processes.
6. Diffusion of technical know-how.
7. Product and marketing innovation.
8. Entry or exit of major firms.
9. Changes in costs and efficiency.
10. Changes in uncertainty and risk.
11. Regulatory and public policy changes.
12. Changes in societal attitudes and lifestyles.

- The essential point: knowing whether the combined effect raises or lowers demand, competitive intensity, and profitability.

### 4.6 Strategic Group Analysis

- **Strategic group:** rivals with similar approaches and market positions (price, quality, channels, technology, geography, service).
- It is visualized with strategic group mapping. It helps find a competitive gap with no rivals.
- **Four steps (Thompson, Peteraf, Gamble, and Strickland 2016):**
  1. Identify characteristics: price or quality (high, medium, low); coverage (local, regional, national, global); line (broad, narrow); service (no frills, limited, full); channels (retail, wholesale, internet, several); vertical integration (none, partial, full); diversification (none, some, considerable).
  2. Place the firms on a two-variable map.
  3. Group those that occupy the same spot.
  4. Draw circles proportional to the group's share of sales.
- It shows close rivals, distant ones, and those you do not compete with.
- **Two reasons for different attractiveness:**
  1. The five forces vary by group (for casual dining, the substitute is fast food or a local option).
  2. Driving forces favor some and harm others.
- **COVID-19 case:** with no dining rooms, casual chains improvised delivery with no drive-through and with complex menus. Fast food with a drive-through won.

### 4.7 Competitor Analysis

- **Competitive intelligence:** “scouting the rival” with open sources, never illegal espionage. It looks for gaps caused by the rival's mistakes.

**Figure 4.3 Framework for Competitor Analysis** (four dimensions that explain moves, reactions, and results):

| Dimension | What to evaluate |
| --- | --- |
| 1 Current strategy | Position according to strategic groups; whether it has an advantage and its basis; what investments it makes |
| 2 Objectives | Financial and strategic (% of share); pressures. Successful rivals adjust little; weak ones innovate, sometimes successfully |
| 3 Resources and capabilities | Strengths and weaknesses; acquiring resources anticipates actions |
| 4 Assumptions | What its management believes about itself and the industry |

- **Sources:** press releases, website, annual and quarterly reports, 8-K reports (notices of material events in the U.S.), and calls with investors and analysts.
- Many companies have this function in business development.

### 4.8 Key Success Factors

- **Key success factors (KSF):** elements of strategy, product and service attributes, operating approaches, resources, and capabilities essential to survive and prosper (Weedmark 2019).
- How one's own KSFs compare with rivals' decides whether one can compete.
- **Questions:**
  1. Why do buyers choose among brands?
  2. What resources and capabilities does rivalry require?
  3. What shortcomings cause sure disadvantage?
- An industry usually has **three to five KSFs**. Without a strategy based on them, the company hardly survives.

### 4.9 The Industry Outlook for Profitability

- Attractiveness is not the same for all participants or entrants.
- **Questions (Thompson, Peteraf, Gamble, and Strickland 2016):**
  1. How does the macro environment affect it?
  2. Do the forces sink profitability?
  3. Do the driving forces favor or harm it?
  4. Does the position outperform that of rivals, and why?
  5. Can the competition attack it, and how?
  6. How well does the strategy meet the KSFs?

| Conclusion | Meaning | Typical response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Attractive | Opportunity for above-average profitability | Invest aggressively |
| Unattractive | Low profitability | The strong invest cautiously or look for another industry |

- Even in an attractive industry, a weak company may seek to be acquired.

### 4.10 Summary: what is new

- The summary organizes the analysis into seven questions: macro environment, forces, drivers, positions, rivals' moves, KSFs, and outlook.
- The **three or four** most important drivers of change are identified first.
- What is attractive for one company may not be for another. There is no single best external analysis.

## Capítulo 5 — The Internal Environment: Evaluating Resources, Capabilities, and Competitiveness (págs. 129–158)

**Central idea:** Internal analysis shows whether the strategy can deliver an advantage over rivals. It rests on resources, capabilities, the value chain, benchmarking, and assessment of competitive strength. For security, understanding the value chain is the way to demonstrate value and obtain resources.

### 5.1 Question 1: How well is the company's strategy working?

The chapter follows six questions:

| No. | Question |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | How well is the strategy working? |
| 2 | Which resources and capabilities are the most important and will give an advantage? |
| 3 | What strengths and weaknesses are there relative to opportunities and threats? |
| 4 | How does the value chain affect costs and the customer value proposition? |
| 5 | Is it stronger or weaker than its rivals? |
| 6 | What strategic issues require critical decisions? |

- Strategy: goal-oriented plans and actions that allow better competition.
- Recent moves are reviewed: prices, design, features, advertising, new areas, mergers or acquisitions.
- The question is whether it competes on price (costs) or on a better product (differentiation), and whether it targets a broad market or a niche.
- Each area has functional strategies: R&D (research and development), marketing, production, IT (information technology), HR (human resources), and security.
- Control questions: does it meet strategic and financial objectives? Does it exceed the industry average? Does it gain customers and share?
- Persistent failures indicate a weak strategy, poor execution, or both.
- Indicators: sales and profits, stock price, financial strength, customer retention, new customer acquisition, and process improvement (orders, deliveries, productivity).
- The worse the performance, the more the strategy should be questioned.

**Figure 5.1 — Strategy and related components.** Center: the business strategy, the action plan for managing a single business.

| Component | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Attracting customers | Better design, features, quality, assortment, or lower prices |
| Responding | Changes in the macro environment, the industry, or the competition |
| Competitive advantage | Lower costs, a better product, or better service to a niche |
| Geography | Expand or reduce coverage |
| Alliances | Strategic partnerships with other companies in the sector |
| Functional strategies | Finance; HR; IT; sales, marketing, and distribution; production; supply chain; R&D, technology, and design |

### 5.2 Question 2: What are the company's most important resources and capabilities, and will they give the company a competitive edge over rivals?

Resources and capabilities are the competitive assets. Two-step analysis: identify them and then see which ones sustain an advantage.

#### 5.2.1 Identifying the company's resources

- Resource: a productive input or asset that the company owns or controls.
- Pharmaceutical case: R&D teams are worth what they are for talent, therapeutic experience, and execution. Lesson: what counts is the application of talent.
- Security is a valuable resource if it improves the protection of assets and people. It reduces legal liability and worker compensation claims.
- Types: financial, physical, human, tangible, intangible, and structural-cultural.
- Financial: debt capacity, credit lines, available capital, cash reserves.
- Tangible: people, facilities.
- Intangible: behaviors, experiences, knowledge, judgment, skills, wisdom, relationships, beliefs, cultural norms.
- Intangibles are among the most important. They arise from learning and create institutional knowledge.
- Example: security uncovers an internal thief; whether to prosecute depends on the company's culture.

#### 5.2.2 Identifying the company's capabilities

- Capability: an aptitude learned over time to perform an internal activity competently.
- They are more complex than resources and arise from repetition. Almost all rest on tacit knowledge.
- Tacit knowledge: skills and experiences that are not codified and are hard to express. They are transferred through practice.
- They are routines that convert resources (inputs) into products (outputs). Example: purity tests at a drug manufacturer.
- Southwest Airlines: cost leadership, 30-minute aircraft turnaround, the pilot helps prepare the cabin. Lesson: culture sustains strategy.
- To find capabilities, start from resources. Biotech with oncologists: capability in cancer drugs.
- PepsiCo: capability to acquire and integrate companies serially.
- Resource bundles: linked competitive assets, centered on cross-functional capabilities.
- Nike: design, market research, sponsorships, brand, and management. It has led in athletic footwear and apparel for more than 20 years.

#### 5.2.3 Assessing the competitive power of a company's resources and capabilities

- Competitive advantage: a hard-to-imitate ability to give more value, with lower prices or more benefits that justify higher prices.
- VRIN test (valuable, rare, inimitable, nonsubstitutable): all four must be passed (Wernerfelt 1984).

| Test | Question | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Valuable | Is it relevant to the strategy? | iPhone, backed by the brand |
| Rare | Do few companies have it? | Cereal brands: it is the price of entry, not rarity |
| Inimitable | Is it hard or costly to copy? | Patents, unique location, complex process, unique culture, motivated staff |
| Nonsubstitutable | Does it resist substitutes of another kind? | iPhone versus cheap smartphones; Coca-Cola versus water, tea, or coffee |

- Most companies do not pass all four tests, and can still be profitable.
- The advantage is not permanent; it must keep being pursued.

Exam note: the book attributes the VRIN test to Wernerfelt (1984). Much of the literature attributes it to Barney (1991).

#### 5.2.4 Dynamic resources and capabilities

- Capabilities do not sustain themselves. Disruptions in technology, preferences, or channels can destroy them.
- They must be reinforced: hire for key positions, enter new geographic or product markets.
- Dynamic capability: the aptitude to modify, deepen, or expand resources and capabilities, and to create others.
- Incremental improvement: Toyota improves production, fuel, and cabin in Lexus. BMW builds capabilities in hybrid engines (Thompson, Peteraf, Gamble, and Strickland 2016).
- Capabilities decay through benign neglect; that is why new resources must also be added.
- Routes: alliances and acquisitions. Bristol Myers Squibb buys drugs to replace those that lose their patents.

### 5.3 Question 3: What are the company's strengths and weaknesses relative to market opportunities and external threats?

- Value question: does the resource exploit an opportunity or neutralize a threat? Yes: strength. No: weakness.
- The same resource can be a strength in one market and a weakness in another.
- Basis: the SWOT analysis (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats) from Chapter 3.
- Strength: something done well or an attribute that improves competitiveness.
- What passes VRIN are the greatest strengths. Other resources give temporary advantages, such as a good market study.
- Skill is measured in supply chain, R&D, production, distribution, sales and marketing, and service.

| Level | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Competence | An activity one has learned to do with ease, successfully, and at acceptable cost |
| Distinctive competence | Doing a set of activities well, often better than rivals |
| Core competence | An activity done with ease and central to strategy and success |

- Core competence is usually a growth engine.
- Procter & Gamble: brand management (Charmin, Tide, Crest, Pampers, Swiffer). Nike: innovative sports design and marketing.
- Security example: a novice investigator has the capability; with experience it becomes a distinctive competence and perhaps a core one.

Exam note: here the order is competence → distinctive → core. In 5.4.4 and in figures 5.4 and 5.5 it is competence → core → distinctive.

- Weakness: a competitive deficiency; something lacking, done worse, or that puts one at a disadvantage.
- Examples: inferior skills or intellectual capital; gaps in physical, organizational, or intangible assets; absent capabilities.
- Key: does it make the company vulnerable? It depends on whether strengths offset it.
- Opportunities: abundant or scarce, fleeting or lasting. In new markets you must anticipate with resources ready. In mature ones they arise sporadically.
- Do not pursue them all: the best fit the competitive assets.
- Threats: better or cheaper technologies, foreign or low-cost rivals, regulation, demographics, tastes, regime changes.
- Examples: the 2008–2009 recession and the pandemic with the 2020 recession. Lesson: have crisis and operating plans to adapt.
- SWOT is not just making lists: you must draw conclusions and turn them into actions.
- Base the strategy on strengths, correct weaknesses that create vulnerability, and pursue opportunities in line with resources.
- Defend against threats according to vulnerability and whether it is the best use of resources.

### 5.4 Question 4: How do a company's value chain activities impact its cost structure and customer value proposition?

- Value chain: primary and support activities that create value for the customer.
- It is not the supply chain; the latter exists within the value chain.
- It matters to security for two reasons: it shows understanding of the business and positions security as an informed partner. In addition, excessive costs can come from fraud, theft, or deception.
- Customer value: how well their needs are met for the price paid. More perceived value, less vulnerability.
- iPhone case: quality attracted customers even though it cost more. It is the price–value dilemma.

#### 5.4.1 The concept of a company value chain

| Category | Role | Activities |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary activities | Create, sell, deliver, and provide after-sales service; “make money” | Inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales |
| Support activities (secondary) | Facilitate the primary ones; seen as a cost | Procurement, HR, finance and accounting, security |

- Inbound logistics: receiving, storing, and distributing inputs. Poor plant or warehouse design raises costs.
- Operations: in manufacturing, plant technology and flow; in retail, store management, service, and inventory.
- Outbound logistics: storing and distributing to the buyer; fast shipping, without damage and with few steps.
- Marketing and sales: segmenting and creating purchase intent. Example: ski equipment targets people with income, athletes, and families.
- Security, as support, will be questioned when asking for budget or staff. It succeeds only if it demonstrates value to a primary activity.

Exam note: the book lists four primary activities, but its definition includes after-sales service.

**Figure 5.2 — Primary Value-Chain Activities**

| Retail | Engineering services |
| --- | --- |
| Supplier partnerships | Research and development |
| Merchandise purchasing | Engineering designs and solutions |
| Inventory management and distribution | Marketing and sales |
| Store operations | Service |
| Marketing and sales | (not applicable) |

- In engineering R&D is vital; in retail the suppliers do it.
- The analysis shows whether the emphasis is on costs or on differentiating, and how margins are achieved.
- Whole Foods case: it did not advertise and bet on higher-income customers. Walmart and Kroger offered cheaper organics. Amazon bought it in 2017 and changed its chain to improve price–value.
- The cost of each activity is compared with that of rivals; this usually requires accountants and analysts.
- Security must compare its activities with those of notable rivals to justify them.

#### 5.4.2 The value chain as a system

- The chain is embedded in a system with suppliers, distributors, and retailers.
- Helping suppliers lower costs or improve quality improves competitiveness. Automakers: auto parts near the plant for just-in-time deliveries.
- Distributors' costs and margins are part of the final price. Examples: car dealerships; kitchen cabinets.

**Figure 5.3 — Midsize coffee producer (costs per unit)**

| No. | Item | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Coffee from a growers' cooperative | $2.30 |
| 2 | Tariffs, storage, and freight | $0.73 |
| 3 | Roasting and packing labor | $0.83 |
| 4 | Labels and bag | $0.45 |
| 5 | Average overhead | $3.03 |
| 6 | Company's total cost | $7.34 |
| 7 | Retailer margin (operating profit) | $2.65 |
| 8 | Average consumer price | $9.99 |

- Check: 2.30 + 0.73 + 0.83 + 0.45 + 3.03 = 7.34; 7.34 + 2.65 = 9.99.
- Lesson: cost competitiveness also depends on suppliers and channels.
- For security: showing how it creates value gives influence and access to resources.

#### 5.4.3 Benchmarking

- Benchmarking: a tool for improving internal activities by learning how other companies, normally in the same sector, perform them.
- Best practices: a method that consistently delivers superior results; they can transcend industries.
- Purchasing, inventories, assembly, launches, order shipping, and even parking lot lighting are compared.
- Negligent security and premises liability lawsuits: courts compare with similar companies. They apply what is “reasonable and ordinary” in the sector, not best practice.
- Warning: with heavily stolen products, the best distribution is the safest, not just the cheapest.
- The hard part is accessing the information, not deciding to do it.
- Sources, without industrial espionage: industry, analyst, and annual reports; consultancies; voluntary company data; associations; observation.
- An independent group gives credibility, protects sensitive data, and reduces unethical conduct.
- McDonald's–Starbucks case: almost 70% of McDonald's sales come from the drive-through. Starbucks copied it and sped up its menu. Lesson: it creates new security risks.
- Security standards do not work equally for all companies; they must be adapted to the business.

Generic list for improving internal activities:

1. Apply best practices, especially in expensive activities.
2. Eliminate cost activities by redesigning products and the chain.
3. Relocate expensive activities to where they are cheaper.
4. Outsource what others do more cheaply.
5. Invest in cost-saving technology.
6. Bypass high-cost activities or line items.
7. Redesign products to make them faster and cheaper.

#### 5.4.4 Translating value chain and benchmarking activities into competitive advantage

- Managing the chain well delivers profitability in two ways (Thompson et al. 2016): 1) more efficiency and lower costs; 2) a basis for differentiating and charging a premium.
- Cost: continuous effort in every activity. Walmart, Dollar General, Nucor Steel, Southwest Airlines, T.J. Maxx.
- Differentiation: brand and reputation are built slowly. Cartier (status), American Express (service), FedEx (reliability), Gilead (patents), Coach (craftsmanship).
- Capability is the potential for action; the value-creating activity initiates the action (Bartlett and Ghoshal 2002).
- A resource's value is measured only when it is deployed. Value chain and benchmarking provide those data.
- Practice and investment turn an activity into a reliable competence (Teece, Pisano, and Shuen 1997). If it is a pillar of the strategy, it is core.

| Step | Figure 5.4: differentiation (Differentiation-Based Advantage) | Figure 5.5: cost (Cost-Based Advantage) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goal | Create more value for the customer | Perform activities more efficiently |
| 1 | Decide on activities that improve quality, features, and performance | Decide to perform activities at the lowest cost |
| 2 | Competences in quality and performance emerge | Competences emerge for lowering costs (production, inventory) |
| 3 | The skill rises to core competence | The skill rises to core competence |
| 4 | Evolves into distinctive competence | Evolves into distinctive competence |
| 5 | Advantage through superior differentiation | Advantage through lower costs |

### 5.5 Question 5: Is the company competitively stronger or weaker than key rivals?

- Quantitative strength ratings are used for the company and its rivals.
- They are subjective, like a risk assessment, without losing value. Finance usually does them; security must understand them.

Steps:

1. List key success factors and strength measures.
2. Assign a weight to each measure according to its importance.
3. Rate each competitor on each measure and multiply by the weight.
4. Add up the weighted ratings.
5. Conclude the net advantage or disadvantage and its areas.

- Strong where the rival is weak: offensive move. Weak where it is strong: defensive move.
- In a price war, it is better to lower the chain's costs first and then the price.
- The higher the weighted rating, the greater the competitiveness. Companies with a high score are good for benchmarking. The ratings guide the moves.

### 5.6 Question 6: What strategic issues and problems merit front-burner managerial attention?

Issues for management (Walker 2012):

1. Stopping new competitors.
2. Fighting rivals' discounts.
3. Reducing high internal costs.
4. Growing with decelerating demand.
5. Expanding products or services.
6. Buying a rival with the strengths that are missing.
7. Expanding abroad.
8. Repositioning by geography or products.
9. Responding to interest in substitutes.
10. Responding to demographic changes in the clientele.

- The list identifies problems; actions come later. If the issues are minor, the strategy is going well.
- You also need to know what to avoid: imitating the rival under pressure is not always advisable.

Exam note: the introduction speaks of issues that “require critical decisions”; the title of 5.6, of issues that “merit front-burner attention.” It is the same question.

Responses to Walmart's entry into groceries: ignore it (Safeway), adjust tactics with prepared food (Kroger), or change the strategy and remodel stores (Target).

Three reasons not to respond:

1. Protect one's own advantage.
2. Lack of resources to imitate by duplication or substitution.
3. Reduce rivalry, which avoids lowering prices and profits.

- Tacit cooperation is legal; colluding to fix prices or limit output is illegal.
- Agreeing on good practices and limiting unsafe products is legal, and raises barriers to entry.
- Tactics: specific actions to execute the strategy. They change more than strategies.
- Competitive parity: matching the performance of a competitor or of the industry average.
- Cheap tactical imitations: lemon-scented detergent, concentrated detergent, Coca-Cola's diet soda followed by Pepsi, airfares.
- Creative destruction: industrial mutation that revolutionizes the economy from within, destroying the old and creating the new.
- Changes in tastes, demographics, or laws can nullify a strategy. Waiting until forced can be disastrous.
- Microsoft case: its advantage in Office was threatened; it recovered it with the cloud, Azure.

### 5.7 Summary

- Valuable resources are those that exploit opportunities or neutralize threats; they raise revenue or lower costs.
- The value chain helps identify them; its differences explain differences in profitability.
- Valuable but common ones give parity, not advantage. Not investing in them creates a terminal disadvantage.
- Imitation (duplication or substitution) rarely gives an advantage.
- Imitating is costly because of unique historical circumstances, complexity, and intellectual capital such as patents.
- The company must be well organized: formal structure, formal and informal controls, and compensation that retains talent.

## Capítulo 6 — Business-Level Strategies (págs. 159–188)

**Central idea:** Business-level strategy defines how the company competes in a single market or industry. There are five generic strategies depending on whether one seeks low cost or differentiation, and a broad market or a niche. Security must understand which one its company chose in order to justify its resources.

### 6.1 Types of Generic Competitive Strategies

- Corporate-level strategies: actions to gain advantage by operating in several markets or industries at once (Porter 1996).
- Business-level strategies: a course of action, the product of management decisions, for competing in a single product market or industry.
- Every path requires giving more value than rivals by doing certain activities differently: a good and cheap product, a superior and more expensive one, or a better balance. One cannot count on inept or immobile rivals.
- Competitive strategy: a plan to position, please the customer, and repel threats.
- Two factors distinguish it: a broad or narrow target market, and advantage through low costs (cost leadership) or unique products (differentiation).

The five strategies (Walker 2018):

| No. | Strategy | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Broad low-cost provider | Lower costs than rivals on comparable products for many buyers; usually sells cheaper |
| 2 | Broad differentiation | Attributes that appeal to a wide spectrum of buyers |
| 3 | Focused low-cost | Serving a niche at lower cost than rivals |
| 4 | Focused differentiation | Serving a niche with attributes tailored to it, better than rivals |
| 5 | Best-cost provider | High-end attributes at lower cost; better value for money. It is a hybrid and is usually the hardest to execute profitably |

**Figure 6.1 — The five generic strategies.** Columns: type of competitive advantage being pursued. Rows: breadth of the target market.

| Target market | Lower cost | Differentiation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Broad spectrum of buyers | Overall low-cost provider | Broad differentiation |
| Narrow segment or niche | Focused low-cost | Focused differentiation |

The best-cost strategy sits at the center of the figure, among the four.

#### 6.1.1 Overall low-cost strategy

- Attractive when there are many price-sensitive buyers.
- Low-cost leadership: being the lowest-cost provider in the sector. It does not mean having the lowest price.
- It is not necessary to have the absolute lowest cost to be profitable.
- A product too poor in attributes offers little value even if it is cheap. The essentials must be maintained.
- Overall low-cost provider: seeks low costs across the entire system in creating and delivering a valued product.
- Two uses of the advantage: 1) sell cheaper and attract price-sensitive buyers; 2) keep price and share and earn more margin per unit.
- Selling cheaper can be seen as an attack and trigger a price war. If there is a risk of retaliation, the second option is preferable.

Two ways to have cumulative costs equal to or lower than rivals':

1. Perform the value chain activities more efficiently.
2. Redesign the chain to eliminate or bypass activities that generate cost.

- For security: no activity escapes cost scrutiny and all personnel must contribute savings ideas.

**The 10 cost drivers, according to Thompson et al. (2016) — Figure 6.2**

| No. | Driver | Idea and example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Economies of scale | More volume, lower average unit cost up to an optimal point. PepsiCo pays $5 million for a Super Bowl ad, spread over hundreds of millions of units |
| 2 | Learning and experience | Cost falls with accumulated repetitions. McDonald's knows what a site needs after thousands of locations |
| 3 | Capacity utilization | Operating near the maximum spreads fixed costs and depreciation over more units |
| 4 | Supply chain efficiencies | Streamlining orders, just-in-time inventory, shipments. Kimberly-Clark |
| 5 | Input costs | Cheaper inputs without losing quality. Timex |
| 6 | Bargaining power | Buying in volume to obtain discounts. Walmart |
| 7 | Communication systems and information technology | Sharing data with suppliers and enterprise resource planning. Amazon |
| 8 | Production technology and design | Computer-aided design and manufacturing, total quality management. Visteon, cockpit electronics for Ford, Nissan, Mazda, BMW, and Daimler |
| 9 | Outsourcing or vertical integration | Outsource if someone else does it cheaper; integrate if it lowers costs. Walmart's and Amazon's own fleets; Newell Brands grows by buying |
| 10 | Incentive systems and culture | Productivity, savings ideas, less turnover. Nucor Steel, Southwest Airlines, Walmart |

Learning curve:

- The cost of an activity falls according to the accumulated number of times it is performed.
- It was observed in World War II aircraft: labor per aircraft fell 20% each time cumulative production doubled (Boston Consulting Group 1970).
- Also in computers, semiconductors, and paper. It applies to any function, including the security generalist.
- Difference from economies of scale: it relates cumulative volume to unit cost, with no cost increase as it grows. Costs fall to the technically possible minimum (Hall and Howell 1986).
- As a graph, it shows how long it takes to acquire skills. Its slope is the rate at which learning becomes savings.
- Economies of scale: producing more units at greater scale with lower input costs.

| Type of cost | Definition | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed cost | Does not change with what is produced or sold; paid regardless of activity | Depreciation |
| Variable cost | Changes in proportion to production | Raw materials, packaging |

- The more capital and the greater the weight of fixed costs, the greater the penalty for operating below full capacity.

#### 6.1.2 Revamping the value chain system to lower costs

- Selling direct to the consumer without distributors or dealers. 30 to 50% of the final price is usually due to the distributor. One must see whether the extra structure generates more revenue.
- Examples: midsize pharmaceutical companies choose between their own sales force or a distributor; retailers, between more online sales or not.
- Eliminating work that adds little value. Walmart receives some products directly in store; supermarkets receive meat already cut and packaged.
- Asking suppliers to locate nearby to reduce freight, warehouses, and handling. Common with automakers' suppliers.

Cases (Walker 2013):

- Nucor: electric arc furnaces and continuous casting with recycled steel. Fewer steps, employees, and capital. It is among the cheapest steel producers in the world.
- Amazon: low-cost leader in e-commerce. It offers more than 230 million items in the United States, about 30 times more than Walmart. It sells more than the next five online retailers combined.
- Amazon uses its scale to negotiate with suppliers. Its own fleet spreads the fixed cost of automation and lowers its cost of capital per dollar sold compared with Walmart and Target.

#### 6.1.3 The keys to being a low-cost provider

- They are frugal, but invest heavily in what reduces costs.
- Vizio (large TVs): adopts technology after calculating the savings and sells at Costco and Walmart. It leads in low cost against Sony and Samsung.
- Other examples: Ryanair (airlines), Stride Rite (children's footwear), Poulan (chain saws).

When it works best (Thompson et al. 2016):

1. Price competition is intense.
2. Rival products are nearly identical.
3. It is hard to differentiate in a valuable way.
4. Most buyers use the product the same way.
5. Switching suppliers costs little (low switching costs).

Why large companies lower unit costs (Barney and Hesterly 2015):

1. More specialization and integrated technology; key in process manufacturing (chemicals, oil, paper).
2. Wholesale purchasing, volume advertising, and lower cost of capital through size and good credit (tied to the five forces model of Chapter 4).
3. Specialized workers who move along the learning curve. Small companies use multifunction employees and outsource accounting, taxes, HR (human resources), and security.

#### 6.1.4 Pitfalls of attempting to be a low-cost provider

Three basic pitfalls (Thompson et al. 2016):

1. Cutting prices aggressively: more sales and share do not mean more profit.
2. Using savings methods that are easy to copy: the advantage is short-lived.
3. Becoming obsessed with cost: features are degraded and interest in extras or new uses is ignored.

Numerical example:

- 1,000 units at $10, cost $9, margin $1: total profit $1,000.
- Price at $9.50 with the same cost: margin $0.50.
- To recover $1,000, 2,000 units are needed (1,000 ÷ 0.50), 100% more.
- The result depends on how much sales rise and how much costs fall with volume.
- Extra risk: a rival finds a cheaper chain or a cost-saving technology. It is worse if the company invested heavily in its current way of operating.

Exam note: the book announces “three basic pitfalls” and then adds this risk separately.

Functional alignment with cost leadership (Barney and Hesterly 2015):

| Function | Focus |
| --- | --- |
| Manufacturing | Lean, low-cost, and good quality |
| Marketing | Value, reliability, and price |
| Research and development | Product extensions and process improvements |
| Finance | Low-cost initiatives and stable structure |
| Accounting | Conservative accounting principles |
| Sales | Value, reliability, and low price |

- Controls: strict costs, quantitative goals, supervision of labor, raw materials, and inventory, and a culture with incentives for saving.

### 6.2 Broad Differentiation Strategies

- Attractive when needs are too diverse for a standard product.
- They require studying needs, behaviors, values, and willingness to pay. They work if many buyers see the offering as better and worth a higher price (Priem 2007).
- Differentiation strategy: distinguishing the product from the competition through unique design or features.
- It allows: charging a premium price; selling more units; creating loyalty and an emotional bond with the brand.
- It is not just marketing or service: there are opportunities throughout the value chain. The systematic approach is value drivers.

**The 8 value drivers (Thompson et al. 2016) — Figure 6.3**

| No. | Driver | Action and example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Product features and performance | Features, safety, environment, style, size and weight; innovation (Apple) |
| 2 | Customer service | Delivery, returns, repairs, assistance, training, credit, fast orders (Best Buy) |
| 3 | Production R&D | Custom products at efficient cost, variety, flexible manufacturing (Vistaprint) |
| 4 | Technology and innovation | Being first; with a patent, it gives an advantage (Pfizer) |
| 5 | Quality control processes | Fewer defects, longer life, longer warranties (Lexus) |
| 6 | Sales and marketing | The brand raises perceived value and the “cost” of switching (Coca-Cola versus Pepsi) |
| 7 | Input quality | Strict bean specifications (Starbucks) |
| 8 | Employee skill, training, experience | Intellectual capital drives innovation; incentives retain talent (Alphabet/Google) |

#### 6.2.1 Revamping the value chain to increase differentiation

Two approaches (Thompson et al. 2016):

1. Coordinate with channel allies (distributors, dealers, brokers, retailers): standards, point-of-sale templates, training, and joint promotions. Coca-Cola has taken over bottlers in trouble to improve them and then returned them.
2. Coordinate with suppliers: improves features and quality, especially in assemblers such as Dell and Harley-Davidson. It speeds development and gives supply priority in shortages.

#### 6.2.2 Delivering superior value with a broad differentiation strategy

Four routes (Thompson et al. 2016):

| Route | Content and example |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Lower the total cost of use | The least obvious and most overlooked route: less waste, just-in-time inventory, technical support, energy savings, less maintenance |
| 2. Tangible features | Features and style; safer, simpler, quieter, more portable, or more durable (smartphones) |
| 3. Intangible features | Image, prestige, status, reputation, and trust (Toyota Prius; Bentley, Coach, Cartier, Burberry, Ralph Lauren, Louis Vuitton) |
| 4. Signal the value | High price as a signal of quality, packaging, advertising, brochures, luxurious facilities (BMW or Mercedes dealerships) |

- Signaling value matters more with intangible features, first purchases, infrequent purchases, or unsophisticated buyers.
- Superior capabilities are needed in service, marketing, brand, and technology. Examples: M.D. Anderson, Mayo Clinic, and Cleveland Clinic; CNN or Fox News.
- The tangible is copied; the socially complex (reputation, relationships, image) is not.
- High switching costs tie the customer in, such as learning Microsoft Office.
- Most durable differentiation: established brand, patents, complex technical superiority, reputation for quality, relationship-based service, unique capabilities.

#### 6.2.3 When differentiation strategy works best

According to Barney and Hesterly (2015) and Thompson et al. (2016):

1. Diverse needs and uses: restaurants, cars, footwear, kitchen appliances.
2. Many valuable ways to differentiate: hotels. Raw materials offer few.
3. Few rivals with similar differentiation. If everyone uses the same attributes, the brand weakens and margins fall.
4. Rapid technological change and competition on new features: smartphones.

#### 6.2.4 Pitfalls to avoid in pursuing a differentiation strategy

| Pitfall | Why it fails |
| --- | --- |
| Attributes that are easy to copy | Rapid imitation leads to competitive parity |
| The buyer sees little value | They do not pay the premium and sales disappoint |
| Overspending | Erodes profit; the cost of differentiating must stay below the premium, or more units must be sold |
| Trivial improvements | They do not generate loyalty |
| Over-differentiating | Exceeds what the buyer needs and raises the price |
| Excessive price premium | The deadliest; the customer switches to cheaper offers |

### 6.3 Focused (Market Niche) Strategies

- They concentrate on a narrow part of the market: a geographic, customer, or product segment.
- Examples: Zipcar (urban rental), Tesla Motors (electric cars), Zappos (online footwear), Blue Nile (online jewelry), microbreweries, local bakeries, and boutiques.
- They tend to be smaller companies; they compete on cost or differentiation.

#### 6.3.1 A focused low-cost strategy

- Definition: it concentrates on a niche of price-conscious buyers, with lower cost and price than rivals.
- Same method as overall low cost; only the size of the group and its needs change.
- Private label: Perrigo leads over-the-counter health products for Walmart, CVS, Walgreens, Rite Aid, and Safeway.
- Budget lodging: Motel 6, Sleep Inn, Super 8.

#### 6.3.2 A focused differentiation strategy

- Definition: it concentrates on buyers in a narrow segment who seek quality.
- Examples from the top of the market: Rolls-Royce and Ritz-Carlton.
- Canada Goose case: founded in 1957. In 2001, with a new chief executive, it dropped private labels and bet on luxury winter wear.
- In a decade it reached about $600 million a year (more than 4,000% increase), in a $1.2 trillion fashion industry. In 2020, because of COVID-19, it laid off staff and closed stores.

#### 6.3.3 When a focused low-cost or differentiation strategy is attractive

1. The niche is profitable and growing well.
2. Industry leaders do not compete in it.
3. Multisegment competitors find it hard to serve the niche and their main clientele at the same time.
4. There are many niches to choose from, so one can pick the one that fits the resources.
5. Rivals have little interest in entering.

- Ideal for small and midsize companies. Examples: YouTube (short videos); Domino's and Papa John's (delivery and takeout, with a cost focus).

#### 6.3.4 The risks of a focused low-cost or differentiation strategy

1. Outside rivals match the niche's capabilities (Marriott and Hilton with multibrand strategies).
2. The niche's tastes converge with those of the main market and barriers fall.
3. The niche attracts so many rivals that they split the profits.
4. The niche's growth slows too much.

### 6.4 Best-Cost Provider Strategies

- Definition: a hybrid of low cost and differentiation. It delivers quality, performance, and service, and wins on price.
- Criticism: management can get confused between “cheaper or better.”
- It occupies the midpoint between cost and differentiation, and between broad market and niche.
- Target customer: the value-conscious buyer, who avoids the very cheap and the very expensive.
- It requires incorporating high-end attributes at lower cost (not price) than rivals.
- Lexus case: in 1989 Toyota entered luxury by matching Mercedes, BMW, Audi, Jaguar, Cadillac, and Lincoln, with a lower starting price.
- The price difference narrowed over time. Toyota created a separate dealer network with luxury service and ambience.

#### 6.4.1 When a best-cost provider strategy works best

- When differentiation is the norm and many buyers prefer the middle range.
- Position: medium quality at below-average price, or high quality at average or slightly higher price.
- It usually works in recessions, when more buyers seek value.

#### 6.4.2 The risks of a best-cost provider

- Without the capabilities to deliver high-end attributes at lower cost, it is not advisable.
- It can get stuck in the middle: low-cost players cut prices and differentiators attract with better attributes.
- Success is part luck (avoiding recessionary periods) and part capabilities.

Exam note: the book says this strategy works in recessions (6.4.1 and the summary), but in 6.4.2 it calls avoiding them luck. For the exam, associate it with success in recessions.

### 6.5 Summary

#### 6.5.1 The contrasting features of the five generic competitive strategies

- Choosing the generic strategy is perhaps the most important decision of top management.
- Each sets a central theme and limits of maneuver, and requires its own resources and capabilities.

| Strategy | Requirement | Works when | Fails when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Low cost | Lower costs; mastering cost drivers | Heavily contested price, identical products, little differentiation, price-sensitive buyers, cheap switching, low barriers to entry | Aggressive cuts, copyable methods, obsession with costs |
| Differentiation | Using value drivers better than rivals | Diverse preferences, few similar rivals, rapid technological change | Fast copying, buyer disinterest, excessive spending or price |
| Focus | Satisfying the niche outstandingly | Profitable and growing niche, hard for rivals, fit with resources, few specialists | Imitation, converging tastes, saturation, slowdown |
| Best cost | High-end attributes at lower cost | Recessions; many midrange buyers | Pressure from both sides |

Exam note: the summary adds two conditions to low cost, price-sensitive buyers and low barriers to entry. The list in 6.1.3 has five.

- In all cases, the advantage requires superior resources that fit the strategy. To be sustainable, rivals must not be able to match or substitute for it.

#### 6.5.2 A final point for security professionals on competitive strategies

- Competitive strategy affects the activities of all functions. To obtain resources, security must understand how the company competes.
- It is not about doing “the right thing,” which each function sees differently. Costs matter also to those who differentiate.
- Question for every leader: considering the costs, how does this help achieve the goals?
- Security must demonstrate that its proposal drives the strategy and the desired results.

## Capítulo 7 — Corporate-Level Strategies (págs. 189–224)

**Central idea:** Corporate strategy decides which businesses, industries, and markets a multibusiness company competes in and how to grow with that portfolio. Diversifying is justified only if it creates long-term economic value and passes three tests. Related diversification usually creates more value than unrelated diversification.

### 7.1 Diversification Strategy

- Corporate-level strategies: actions to achieve competitive advantage by operating in several markets or industries at once.
- Synergy: the combined value of two companies exceeds the sum of their parts. It is a term typical of mergers and acquisitions (M&A).
- Corporate strategy uses a portfolio approach: how the businesses fit together and affect each other and how the parent company is organized.

| Aspect | Business strategy | Corporate strategy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who designs it | Managers of each business | Top management of the corporation |
| Scope | A single industry | Portfolio across several industries |
| Horizon | Shorter | Longer |
| Key question | How to compete | Which businesses to be in and how to grow |

- For security: distinguishing both levels gives credibility to the professional without business training.
- Diversification strategy: adding markets, products, services, or stages of production to enter different lines of business.

Three facets, the responsibility of top management (Thompson, Peteraf, Gamble, and Strickland, 2016):

| Facet | Content |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Choose industries and mode of entry | Build from scratch, acquire, or form a joint venture (JV) or alliance |
| 2. Leverage relationships among value chains | Transfer resources, combine activities, share brand, collaborate |
| 3. Boost joint performance | Stay the course, expand, shrink by divesting, or restructure |

- Because of that workload, corporate management delegates business strategy to the head of each business.

#### 7.1.1 When to Consider Diversifying

- Signal: dwindling opportunities and stagnant sales in the core business.
- Risks: new technologies, substitutes, shifting preferences, more competition.
- Options: related or not; little or much; a few large businesses or several small ones; by acquisition, internal creation, or alliance/JV.
- It always requires a sound economic justification.

#### 7.1.2 Building Value: The Three Tests of Corporate Advantage

| Test | Requires |
| --- | --- |
| Industry attractiveness test | Attractive according to the Five Forces, compatible resources, good growth and return |
| Cost of entry test | The cost of entering does not exceed the potential profitability |
| Better-off test | The businesses perform better together than apart (synergy) |

- Paradox: the more attractive the industry, the more expensive the entry.
- All three tests must be passed; passing one or two is suspect (Barney and Hesterly, 2015).

### 7.2 Approaches to Diversification

| Mode | Advantages | Drawbacks | Best when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Acquisition | Fast; jumps barriers (know-how, suppliers, scale, brand, distribution) | Price, premium, due diligence, negotiation, and integration | Key resources are lacking or speed matters |
| Internal development (corporate venturing) | Avoids the risks of buying; more final benefit | Slow, uncertain; very high risk of failure; culture can hold it back | See five conditions below |
| Alliance/joint venture | Shares risk and saves entry cost | Conflicting objectives, management disagreements (the most common), cultural clashes; the least durable | High uncertainty or lack of competencies |

**Acquisition**

- Pharmaceutical case: bringing a drug to market can take more than 10 years; buying speeds it up.
- Average premium of the last decade: around 25% over valuation, depending on industry (Kengelbach et al., 2019).
- Business valuation: done by outside appraisers to set the objective economic value. It is not the stock price. Uses: purchase and sale, partners' stakes, taxation.
- Between 70% and 90% of M&A fail (Martin, 2016, Harvard Business Review).
- Causes: premium not recovered, poor integration, technology failures, cultural clashes, external factors (2020 pandemic).
- Expert acquirers: Amazon, Cisco Systems, Johnson & Johnson, PepsiCo, Pfizer, United Technologies.
- It also serves to enter foreign markets without local knowledge, distribution, or relationships.

**Internal development**

- Case: J&J Development Corporation funded internal projects with no budget or capex (capital expenditure, spending on physical assets) (Raynor, 2007).
- Obstacles: barriers to entry; investing in production, supply, personnel, distribution, and customers.
- Conditions: (1) the parent has almost all the resources; (2) there is time; (3) it costs less than buying; (4) it does not upset supply and demand; (5) incumbents will respond poorly or late.

**Alliance or JV**

- Definition: an agreement between independent companies with related goals and a specific purpose, often to reduce costs. It is usually co-managed with risk shared equally.
- Three situations (Thompson et al., 2016): (1) too complex or risky for one; (2) competencies are lacking (telecommunications, biotechnology, networked systems); (3) entering another country. Case: Starbucks and Tata Group, 50-50 JV in India.

#### 7.2.4 Choosing a Mode of Entry

| Question (Thompson et al., 2016) | Implication |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Do we have the resources? | Yes: internal. No, and they cannot be bought: acquisition or JV. Case: Bank of America bought Merrill Lynch for its investment banking |
| 2. What barriers are there? | Low, or high but surmountable: internal. Insurmountable: acquisition or JV |
| 3. Does speed matter? | Yes: acquisition. If it pays to wait for the market to clear up: JV or internal |
| 4. Which costs less? | Acquiring is worth it if the synergy offsets the premium. The JV has coordination and transaction costs |

### 7.3 Choosing the Diversification Path

| Type | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Related diversification | Businesses with valuable commonalities in value chain and resources |
| Unrelated diversification | Different value chains and resources, with no relevant commonalities |

- Types (Barney and Hesterly, 2015): product (several industries), geographic market (several regions), and product-market (both).
- Exam note: the book defines related diversification as operating in several industries or markets. That holds for any diversification; related diversification also requires commonalities in the value chain.
- Strategic fit: activities of different businesses so similar that they allow sharing or transferring resources and capabilities.

| Fit opportunity | Example from the book |
| --- | --- |
| Transfer expertise or know-how | A security investigator from one business investigates in the others |
| Share costs by combining activities | Same plant, warehouse, distribution, or sales force |
| Exploit a brand | Yamaha (motorcycles to WaveRunner); Apple (computers to phones and watches) |
| Share other resources | Disney uses Marvel in parks, stores, film, and video games |
| Collaborate to create capabilities | Defect-free products or faster time to market |

| Resources | Traits | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Specialized | Limited use; basis of related diversification | L'Oréal, Corning, drug discovery |
| General | Useful in any business | Information technology, human resources, accounting and taxes, budgeting and planning, security and risk |

- Security is the book's example of a general resource. Sharing only general resources is not enough for a related strategy.
- PepsiCo: only snacks and beverages; 23 brands of more than a billion dollars a year each.
- L'Oréal: more than 30 billion dollars; 23 research centers. It entered cosmetics, hair, skin, and fragrances; avoided food and transportation.
- Corning: 150 years in specialty glass and optics; it leads five related segments, although the book names only three.

#### 7.3.2 Strategic Fit Along the Value Chain

| Area | Benefit | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Supply chain | Common purchasing skill, logistics, and partners; volume discounts | Dell buys common components from the same partners |
| Research, development, and technology | Savings, less time to market, more innovation | Cable that offers internet and voice over IP (VoIP) telephony: DISH, XFINITY, Comcast |
| Manufacturing | Transfer quality and efficiency; fewer plants | Emerson Electric took its low cost to Beaird-Poulon chain saws; Smucker's combined production with Jif |
| Sales and marketing | One sales force, website, and ads; consolidated orders and billing | — |
| Distribution | Same centers, wholesalers, and retailers | Conair combined two centers after buying Allegro's business |
| Customer service | Single after-sales service and infrastructure; best practices that differentiate | Utility that adds gas, water, repairs, and home security using the same call centers |

- Low cost means an efficient value chain, not necessarily the lowest price.

#### 7.3.3 Economies of Scope

| Concept | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Economies of scope | Savings from operating in several businesses; only in related diversification |
| Economies of scale | Savings from operating at a larger size |

- Advantage in two ways: lower cost or differentiation with a higher price.
- The more relationship between businesses, the more opportunity for advantage.
- The benefits are not automatic: management must act to capture them.

### 7.4 Unrelated Diversification

- It enters any industry with good financial prospects; it does not seek fit.
- Conglomerates: Berkshire Hathaway, Siemens, General Electric (GE), United Technology. They almost always enter through acquisition.
- Purchase criteria: (1) meets profitability goals; (2) industry with good growth; (3) large enough to contribute.
- It must pass the better-off test: growing in profits, not just in revenue.

#### 7.4.1 Building Value

According to Campbell, Goold, and Alexander (1995), it depends on corporate parenting.

| Route | Content |
| --- | --- |
| General resources | Reputation, credit rating, access to financial markets, management training, ethics and governance, budgeting, accounting, and control systems; public relations, legal, human resources, risk, and security services |
| Expert oversight | Advice and tacit knowledge of veteran executives |
| Corporate brand | GE in appliances, medical products, and aircraft engines |
| Internal capital market | Funds what the external market would not fund |
| Restructuring | Buying weak companies cheaply and turning them around |

- Shared security: purchasing power in access control and surveillance; common investigations. But dedicated security may be preferable to understand each business.
- Eliminating duplicate support lowers overhead.
- Restructuring: rationalizing activities, selling idle assets, cutting expenses, and sending veteran managers.
- Newell Rubbermaid case (Sharpie, Levolor, Calphalon, Lenox): so good at rescues that it "Newell-izes" what it buys.

#### 7.4.2 The Path to Greater Value

| Action | Test |
| --- | --- |
| Enter industries with consistent returns | Attractiveness |
| Negotiate favorable prices | Cost of entry |
| Superior parenting (oversight, allocation, restructuring) | Better-off |

- Parenting advantage: creating more value than other diversified corporations.

#### 7.4.3 Drawbacks — Thompson et al. (2016)

1. High management demand: managing by the numbers fails in crises of unfamiliar businesses. One or two mistakes can sink profits and the stock.
2. Limited potential for advantage: it contributes only capital, general resources, and advice. General resources are worth little unless of exceptional quality (GE's management, Newell Rubbermaid's rescues).

#### 7.4.4 Misguided Reasons

| Reason | Why it fails |
| --- | --- |
| Reduce risk | The investor diversifies better with their own portfolio |
| Growth | Growing for growth's sake does not create value |
| Stabilize | There is no evidence of more stability in a recession |
| Managerial motives | Compensation grows with size |

- Real mixes (Barney and Hesterly, 2015): core business with 50–80% of revenue; narrow (2 to 5 businesses); broad; or unrelated areas with related businesses inside.

### 7.5 Evaluating the Strategy of a Diversified Company

| Step (Thompson et al., 2016) | Content |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Attractiveness of the industries |
| 2 | Competitive strength of each unit and attractiveness-strength matrix |
| 3 | Strategic fit among value chains |
| 4 | Resource fit |
| 5 | Ranking prospects and prioritizing resources |
| 6 | New strategic moves |

#### Step 1: Industry Attractiveness

| Measure | More attractive if... |
| --- | --- |
| Size and growth | Large and fast |
| Competitive intensity | Weak pressures |
| Opportunities and threats | Many opportunities, few threats |
| Fit among industries | Fits with the others; does not count in unrelated diversification |

- Each measure carries a weight; each industry is rated on each measure.

- Units in unattractive industries: candidates for divestiture, unless strong position or strategic value.

#### Step 2: Business Unit Competitive Strength

Measures: relative market share; costs versus rivals; key product attributes; brand and reputation; other valuable resources; fit with other units; bargaining power; profitability versus rivals.

- Relative share: own share ÷ share of the largest rival, in units, not in money.
- High cost is acceptable only if it funds a differentiation the customer pays for.

Example: A has 40% and its largest rival, B, 30%.

```latex
\text{Relative market share}_A = \frac{40\%}{30\%} = 1.33 \qquad \text{Relative market share}_B = \frac{30\%}{40\%} = 0.75
```

```latex
\text{Competitive strength} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( \text{weight}_i \times \text{rating}_i \right)
```

- Below 1.0, weak position. Attractiveness is scored with the same method.
- Pending: the book names the nine-cell matrix, but does not describe its cells.

#### Step 3: Strategic Fit

- It is omitted in unrelated diversification. Questions: do economies of scope give a cost advantage? how much is transferring skills worth? does the brand increase sales? does collaboration create capabilities?

#### Step 4: Resource Fit

- Definition: the businesses have compatible specialized resource requirements that allow them to be supported and add value.
- In unrelated diversification, there is fit if the parent knows how to exercise parenting or shares general resources.
- Financial fit: fund the businesses with internal cash, pay down debt, and not overstretch.

| Type of business | Traits |
| --- | --- |
| Cash hog | High growth; little cash, heavy investment |
| Star | Leader in a high-growth market; self-sufficient |
| Cash cow | Leader in a mature industry; surplus cash; just enough is invested to defend it |

- Ideal sequence: cash hog, then star, then cash cow.
- Financial questions: does any business absorb resources and contribute little? is the financial strength enough without hurting the credit rating?
- Nonfinancial questions: does the parent have the resources each business asks for? are they stretched too thin?
- If the resources do not fit the key success factors, divest or do not buy. If they fit another industry, consider acquiring there.
- Risk of overstretching: buying sprees or lack of depth to transfer skills.

#### Step 5: Ranking Business Units

- Priority: more profit and growth, good position in the matrix, and good fit.
- Consider past performance, although it does not always predict.
- Divesting the worst can free up funds for the best.

#### Step 6: Crafting New Strategic Moves

| Action (Thompson et al., 2016) | When |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Stay with the portfolio | Good prospects and fit |
| 2. Expand through acquisitions | To strengthen position and capabilities |
| 3. Divest and shrink (retrenching) | Portfolio too scattered |
| 4. Restructure | Eroding performance |

- For security: supporting the growth of the business strengthens its case for requesting resources.
- Exam note: the book announces four actions, but develops five. It adds geographic expansion of existing businesses.
- Spin-off: an independent company created by selling shares to the public (initial public offering, IPO) or distributing them to shareholders. Case: PepsiCo, 1997, Tricon Global Restaurants (today Yum! Brands).
- Case: pharmaceutical companies in cosmetics failed because of cultural clash and divested.
- Test: if we were not in this business, would we enter it today? If not, sell.
- Causes for restructuring: (1) misaligned resources; (2) many unattractive industries; (3) many weak businesses; (4) threatening technologies; (5) falling share; (6) excessive debt; (7) bad acquisitions.
- Special cases: financing a large purchase or complying with antitrust laws.
- VF Corporation case: returns more than five times higher than rivals. Since 2000, almost 5 billion dollars in 19 acquisitions; 2 billion for Timberland in 2011; 12 billion in 2015.

## Capítulo 8 — International Business (págs. 225–270)

**Central idea:** Going abroad provides customers, lower costs, and new resources, but complicates strategy. The chapter explains why and how to enter, which international approach to choose, and how to gain advantage. It closes with recent threats that broaden the role of security.

### Chapter introduction: attitudes toward the foreign

| Concept | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Parochialism | A narrow view that does not recognize that others live and work differently. It harms trust, development, and promotions. |
| Monolingualism | A sign of parochialism. Germans or Indonesians speak 3–4 languages; the U.S. and the UK cut back their teaching. |
| Ethnocentric attitude | Only the practices of the home country are valid; foreigners are not trusted. |
| Polycentric attitude | Headquarters believes the host country knows best. Risk: incoherent systems. |
| Geocentric attitude | Uses the best approach and the best people in the world. Typical of successful global companies. |

- Ethnocentrism: believing one's own nation, culture, or group to be superior, often with rejection of others.
- Recency effect: the most recent is remembered and weighs more. Typical error: believing that international trade was born in the 20th century (Robbins and Coulter 2018).
- World Economic Forum 2016: 10 global challenges that require public-private cooperation.
- To give advantage, strategy must exploit valuable, rare, and costly-to-imitate resources, with suitable organization.

### 8.1 Why Companies Decide to Enter Foreign Markets

Five reasons (Kokemuller 2020):

| # | Reason | Detail and example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | New customers | Grow if the local market is exhausted; lengthen the life cycle; recover research and development (R&D) investment sooner. |
| 2 | Lower costs through scale, experience, and purchasing power | Local volume is not enough; the learning curve is descended sooner. Michelin, BMW, Nestlé. |
| 3 | Cheap inputs | Natural resources (oil and gas, minerals, rubber, timber); cheap labor (China, India, Vietnam). |
| 4 | Exploit own competencies | Replicate the successful model (McDonald's, Starbucks). |
| 5 | Resources and capabilities abroad | Acquisitions or alliances to complement or learn: distribution, local management, technical knowledge. |

- Extra: suppliers follow their customers (auto parts makers; Newell-Rubbermaid after Walmart, Reynolds 2017).

### 8.2 Why Competing Across National Borders Makes Strategy More Complex

Five reasons (Barney and Hesterly 2015):

1. Home-country advantages that differ by industry.
2. Location advantages for each value chain activity.
3. Unequal political and economic conditions.
4. Risk of adverse exchange rate changes.
5. Different tastes: customize or standardize.

#### 8.2.1–8.2.5 Diamond of National Competitive Advantage

Examples: Chile stands out in mining, fruit, fish, chemicals, wine, pulp, and paper. Japan, in electronics, autos, semiconductors, and steel.

| Factor | What it includes | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Demand conditions | Size, growth, and sophistication of local buyers; sophistication drives innovation. | Luxury cars: more market in the U.S. and Germany than in Argentina, China, Mexico, or India. These exceed Great Britain, Canada, Denmark, or Japan in potential (O'Brien 2019). |
| Factor conditions | Labor, technical and managerial knowledge, land, capital, natural resources, and infrastructure. | India distributes well to 3 million retailers; China, only locally. |
| Related and supporting industries | Clusters of suppliers and complements; collaboration and innovation. | Ferrari, Maserati, and Ducati in the Italian “motor technology district.” |
| Firm strategy, structure, and rivalry | Own management styles; local rivalry that trains firms to compete abroad. | Alliances more common in Asia and Latin America than in North America. |

- All four factors must be favorable at the same time.
- Uses (Thompson, Peteraf, Gamble, and Strickland 2016):
  1. Predict where foreign entrants will come from and how strong they will be.
  2. Choose markets where rivals are weak.
  3. Locate value chain activities.
- Exam note: the book does not name the author; the diamond is Michael Porter's.

#### 8.2.6 Opportunities for Location-Based Advantages

- Wages, productivity, energy, and environmental regulation change the cost of manufacturing. Cheap countries are export bases.
- Examples of low wages: India, China, Pakistan, Cambodia, Vietnam, Mexico, Brazil, Guatemala, the Philippines, Africa, and Eastern Europe (textiles and apparel).
- Inputs are also outsourced, such as active pharmaceutical ingredients (API).
- China is the “manufacturing capital”; its wages and middle class are rising, but it limits foreign access.
- Tiffany & Co. bought mines in Canada for “conflict-free” diamonds. Call centers in India and Ireland.
- Upstream: searching for, extracting, and bringing raw materials to the plant. Downstream: distribution, transportation, after-sales, wholesalers, and retailers.

#### 8.2.7 The Impact of Government Policies and Economic Conditions in Host Countries

| Pro-business government | Government that discourages |
| --- | --- |
| Lower taxes, cheap loans, help with land, paid training (Ireland) | Costly environmental regulation: Coca-Cola and PepsiCo with water in India; they responded by investing in running water |
| Consults business leaders | Subsidies to local companies (China) |
| Softens new rules | Cumbersome customs, tariffs, or quotas |
| — | Minimum local content, prior approval of investments, limits on taking out funds, mandatory local partner, limits on exporting, price controls |

- Political risk: unstable or weak governments hostile to foreigners. It includes unrest, onerous laws, corruption, nationalization, war, terrorism, and cyber threats.
- Fact: only 7% of oil and gas reserves are open to international private firms; 65% belong to state-owned companies (Investopedia 2019). Mexico, Venezuela, Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, and Russia are cited.
- Economic risk: monetary and regulatory instability and little protection of property. It includes inflation, deficits, risky credit, piracy, exchange rates, and pandemics.

#### 8.2.8 The Risks of Adverse Exchange Rate Shifts

- The exchange rate can vary 20–40% a year, gradually or abruptly (Investopedia 2019).
- Two reasons for the risk: 1) it is hard to predict; 2) it makes uncertain which country is cheap to manufacture in and which rival wins.
- Example: a U.S. company manufactures in Brazil for 4 reals and exports to the European Union.

```latex
\text{Cost in euros} = \frac{\text{cost in reals}}{\text{reals per euro}}
```

| Exchange rate | Cost | Effect for the exporter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 4 reals per euro | 4 ÷ 4 = 1.00 euro | Baseline |
| 5 reals per euro (weak real) | 4 ÷ 5 = 0.80 euro | Favorable: cheaper and more exports |
| 3 reals per euro (strong real) | 4 ÷ 3 = 1.33 euros | Unfavorable |

- Exam note: the book writes “4 reals divided by 3 reals”; you divide by reals per euro.
- A weak dollar favors U.S. plants:
  1. It makes goods made by foreign rivals more expensive.
  2. It slows imports.
  3. It boosts exports.
  4. It raises foreign profits in dollars: 10 million euros are worth more dollars.
- A prolonged weak dollar attracts foreign plants to the U.S.; a strong one, the opposite. The same with the euro.

#### 8.2.9 Cross-Country Differences in Demographic, Cultural, and Market Conditions

- Cases: top-loading washing machines in France. Compact appliances in Hong Kong and large ones in Taiwan. Eel ice cream in Asia. Korean parents who avoid buying PCs.
- Customizing appeals to the local buyer, but raises costs (designs, short runs, logistics). Standardizing gives scale, learning curve, and lower unit cost.

### 8.3 Strategic Options for Entering International Markets

Five options (Thompson et al. 2016): exporting; licensing; franchising; subsidiary by acquisition or internal development; alliances or joint ventures (JV).

- Criteria: objectives, resources, country barriers, and transaction costs (hiring a partner and making sure it complies).
- More investment means more risk, but more ownership and control (Grosdanovska, Bojkofska, and Jankulovski 2017).

| Option | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 8.3.1 Export | Conservative first step; little capital; uses existing capacity. Delegates to foreign wholesalers or builds its own network. Common in Chinese, Korean, and Italian companies. | Vulnerable if: 1) producing at home costs more; 2) the exchange rate moves against it; 3) freight is expensive; 4) there are barriers. Requires competitive costs, local distribution, and currency hedging. |
| 8.3.2 Licensing | For those with technology, brand, or patent but without the resources to enter. Collects royalties; the licensee bears costs and risks. Used in software and pharmaceuticals. | Giving up know-how and losing control; hard to monitor. |
| 8.3.3 Franchising | Ideal in services and retail. The franchisee bears almost all the cost and risk; the franchisor recruits, trains, supports, and supervises. McDonald's, Yum! Brands (Pizza Hut, KFC, Taco Bell, WingStreet), UPS Store, 7-Eleven, Hilton. | Poor selection; low quality; fraud and embezzlement; dilemma of adapting. |
| 8.3.4 Acquisition | The fastest route; perhaps the least risky and most efficient in the face of barriers from channels, suppliers, and authorities. | Pay a premium for a good one or buy a weak one cheaply? |
| 8.3.4 Greenfield venture | Direct control of the entire value chain. | Expensive, risky, consumes resources; fails without solid institutions; the slowest. |

- Security in franchises: evaluating candidates, choosing locations, supplier due diligence, quality control, fraud and embezzlement.
- Adapting menus avoids ethnocentric thinking and improves profitability.
- Acquisition: with little local knowledge and a lot of capital, buy a capable company. With the know-how to transform it, a weak one can yield more.
- Greenfield venture or internal startup: a subsidiary created from scratch. It requires international experience and resources. It is advisable when:
  1. It is cheaper than acquiring.
  2. It does not upset the local supply-demand balance.
  3. It achieves good distribution, often through a well-known brand.
  4. It will have the size and costs to compete with locals.
- Reaching the market earlier increases the share captured; that is why its slowness weighs (Investopedia 2019).

### 8.4 International Strategy: The Three Main Approaches

- International strategy: competing in two or more countries at once. Tension: local responsiveness versus global efficiency (Kumar 2009).

| Trait | Multidomestic | Global | Transnational |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Motto | Think local, act local | Think global, act global | Think global, act local |
| Product | Varies by country | Same product, brand, and channels | Mass customization |
| Decisions | Decentralized | Centralized at headquarters | Combined |
| When | High local need, little savings from standardizing | Large savings from standardizing, homogeneous needs | High local need and high benefit from integrating |
| Example | Food with local brands; BP sells as BP and Arco in the U.S. and as Aral in Germany, Belgium, Poland, Hungary, and the Czech Republic | Ford and its global design | McDonald's, KFC, Starbucks, Unilever, Otis in China |

- Sources: Bartlett and Ghoshal 1998; Barney and Hesterly 2015.
- Multidomestic, disadvantages: 1) slows knowledge transfer; 2) raises costs; 3) does not create a single worldwide advantage.
- Global: few plants, shared practices, and strong central control. If differences between countries are small, it is preferable. It maintains a single focus: low cost, differentiation, best cost, or focused. Disadvantages: 1) serves local needs worse; 2) responds worse to local changes; 3) more transportation and tariffs; 4) more coordination.
- Transnational: the best for transferring capabilities among subsidiaries. Disadvantages: 1) the hardest to implement; 2) conflicting objectives; 3) expensive, slow, and uncertain.

### 8.5 International Operations and the Quest for Competitive Advantage

Three routes (Kumar 2009): location; sharing or transferring resources; coordination across countries.

#### 8.5.1–8.5.2 Using Location; When to Disperse Activities

| Concentrate when… | Disperse when… |
| --- | --- |
| Costs are much lower in certain places (footwear in China and Korea; PC boards in Taiwan) | They are buyer-related activities: distribution, marketing, after-sales |
| There are large economies of scale (LED televisions in Japan, South Korea, Taiwan) | High freight, diseconomies of size, or trade barriers |
| There are strong learning effects | The aim is to shorten deliveries |
| There are superior resources (Samsung in Silicon Valley) | Exchange rate, supply, and political risk are hedged |

- Cost leadership requires the largest worldwide share of manufacturing, not of brand, in a few giant plants.
- Regional distribution centers: North America, Latin America, Europe, Middle East, and Asia-Pacific.
- Dispersing: manufacturers of mining and drilling equipment, or large auditing firms, set up next to the customer.
- Risks that dispersing covers: strikes, natural disasters, pandemics, transport delays, and political changes.
- Just-in-time (JIT) or Toyota Production System (TPS): a method for reducing production and response times.
- Separable buyer activities go where there is advantage: parts in Mexico, research in Germany, development in the U.S., assembly in Brazil, Spain, or Taiwan.

#### 8.5.3 Sharing and Transferring Resources and Capabilities Across Borders

- Shared brands (Hermès, Prada, Gucci). Whirlpool transfers innovations among more than 70 plants.
- Sharing is cheaper than creating; it spreads fixed costs. Disney replicates parks; Honda took its motorcycle reputation to cars.
- One also learns from rivals, partners, and acquired companies.
- There is no guarantee: it depends on local rivalry and loyalty to local brands. Philips, present in more than 60 countries, failed to achieve a high-end image in North America and loses money there.

#### 8.5.4 Benefiting from Cross-Border Coordination

- Moving production because of exchange rate, components, wages, or energy; diverting shipments; pressuring governments; using idle capacity (Prahalad and Doz 1987).

### 8.6 Cross-Border Strategic Moves

## Capítulo 9 — Building an Organization Capable of Good Strategy Execution (págs. 271–298)

**Central idea:** Crafting strategy is analytical work; executing it is operational and revolves around people, processes, and structure. Strategies fail mostly because of poor execution. The chapter covers the first three of ten tasks: staffing, developing resources and capabilities, and designing a structure that supports the strategy.

### 9.1 A Framework for Executing Strategy

| Aspect | Crafting strategy | Executing strategy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nature | Analytical | Operational, action-oriented |
| Focus | Market, resources, and capabilities | People, business processes, and structure |
| Difficulty | Easier | Harder and slower |

- Executing requires: working with and through others; strengthening capabilities; the right structure; allocating resources; supporting policies, processes, and systems; discipline to achieve results.
- It tests the ability to lead change, improve daily operations, create a supportive culture, and meet goals (Thompson, Peteraf, Gamble, and Strickland, 2016).
- Study of 400 chief executive officers (CEOs) in the United States, Europe, and Asia: excellence in execution was their number one challenge (Sull, Homkes, and Sull, 2015).
- Announcing a strategy does not guarantee that the workforce will adopt it: its logic must be explained and commitment obtained.
- Implementing changes takes months to years. It is a task for the whole organization, although the CEO and top management (C-suite) are ultimately answerable.
- The approach depends on context (low cost or differentiation, new rival, financial crisis). There is no single recipe.

**The 10 managerial tasks of execution (Thompson et al., 2016)**

| No. | Task | Chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Staff with capable managers and employees | 9 |
| 2 | Acquire and develop resources and capabilities | 9 |
| 3 | Create a structure that supports the strategy | 9 |
| 4 | Allocate sufficient resources, financial and human | 10 |
| 5 | Implement policies and procedures that facilitate execution | 10 |
| 6 | Adopt, and adapt if needed, best practices and processes | 10 |
| 7 | Install information and operating systems | 10 |
| 8 | Tie rewards and incentives to strategic and financial goals | 10 |
| 9 | Instill a culture that favors execution | 11 (culture) |
| 10 | Exercise strong leadership toward operational excellence | 11 (the book does not expressly assign it) |

- Two best signs of good execution (Welch and Welch, 2005): meeting or exceeding performance targets, and performing the value chain with operational excellence across the company.
- Each manager must ask what needs to be done in their area to implement their part and how to achieve it on time (Bossidy and Charan, 2002).
- Large organization: top management communicates the change, builds consensus, places strong managers, directs resources, sets deadlines and measures, rewards milestones, and leads. It depends on operating managers.
- Small organization: top management deals directly with the front line, observes progress, and adjusts.

### 9.2 The First Three Key Actions

| Action | Content |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Staffing the organization | Strong management team; attract and retain employees with experience, technical skills, and intellectual capital |
| 2. Acquire, develop, and strengthen resources and capabilities | Accumulate resources; master critical value chain activities; update capabilities according to market and customer |
| 3. Structure the organization and the work | Organize activities and processes; set authority and reporting; decide how much authority to delegate |

- Even if the strategy does not change, resources must be renewed: strategic assets depreciate over time.
- A poorly fitted structure raises operating and management costs and breaks communication or coordination (AllenVision, 2020).

### 9.3 Staffing the Organization

#### 9.3.1 Putting Together a Strong Management Team

- It is the cornerstone: smart, clear people who know what to do, manage well, and deliver results (Menkes, 2005).
- Without that team: missed deadlines, wasted effort, and ineptitude. The weak cannot tell valuable ideas from mistaken ones.
- Good implementer (Collins, 2001): tough questions, knows the details, knows what resources are needed, gets results through others, and follows up.
- Strengthen by promoting from within or hiring from outside. Outside is the norm in turnarounds, rapid growth, or lack of know-how.
- Replace anyone who cannot make the changes on time and at reasonable cost.
- Goal: a critical mass of change agents. A team that collaborates achieves more than one or two isolated stars (Bartlett and Ghoshal, 2002).

#### 9.3.2 Recruiting, Training, Hiring, and Retaining Capable Employees

Evolution of human resources (Cappelli and Tavis, 2018):

| Era | Model |
| --- | --- |
| After World War II | Planning; lifetime employees; rotations; 5-, 10-, and 15-year plans; deliberate bureaucracy |
| 1990s | Lateral external hiring to gain flexibility; succession plans and annual reviews continue, much criticized |
| Today | Agile methods: rapid prototyping, iterative feedback, team decisions, and task-centered action |

- Apple, Goldman Sachs, Johnson & Johnson, McKinsey, PepsiCo, and Procter & Gamble seek "A players." They retain them with good compensation and promotions, and draw talent from rivals.
- For successful companies, broadening the talent base matters more than investing in capital projects.
- For security: this demand reaches all functions, security included, and creates performance expectations.

Eight practices of well-managed companies (Miller, Eisenstadt, and Foote, 2002):

1. Thoroughly screen and evaluate candidates: skills, energy, initiative, judgment, aptitude for learning, and cultural fit.
2. Offer internal and external training throughout the career.
3. Give challenging and interesting assignments.
4. Rotate across functions and geographies; in multinationals, with international experience.
5. Create a stimulating environment, a "great place to work."
6. Encourage questioning the established, innovating, and proposing products or businesses.
7. Retain the best with promotions, salary, flexible health insurance, and retirement.
8. Coach average performers and move out low performers.

### 9.4 Developing and Building Critical Resources and Capabilities

- Sustainable advantage depends on the quality of resources (Chapter 5).
- If the strategy brings new elements, it may be necessary to acquire resources or build competencies. If it does not change, resources still have to be updated.
- Building competencies is a multistage process that takes months or years.

#### 9.4.1 Three Approaches to Building and Strengthening Capabilities

- According to Helfat and Peteraf (2003), building and refining competencies is slow and demanding. Copying others' best practices is easier said than done.
- Making capability building routine generates dynamic capabilities for managing change.

| Approach | Advantage | Difficulty |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Internal development | Easier if it updates or recombines existing capabilities | Capabilities are bundles of skills embedded in cross-functional routines |
| 2. Mergers and acquisitions | Speed; necessary if the capability cannot be built in-house and the environment changes fast | Tacit knowledge does not transfer easily; incompatibilities; the purchase can destroy the capability |
| 3. Collaborative partnerships with suppliers, competitors, or other experts | Access to others' resources | They depend on trust and fit |

**Internal development**

- Cross-functional capabilities: getting products out fast (research and development, engineering and design, purchasing, production, marketing, distribution); superior service (call center, shipping, billing and collections, after-sales).
- It starts with setting a goal, ideally a stretch objective: it demands extra effort, but it must be achievable or it demotivates.
- With repetition, the skill becomes a proven competence: learning by doing (the experience curve applied to people). It is accelerated by deliberate learning and incentives.
- Williams-Sonoma case: catalog in the 1970s, purchase of Pottery Barn, and e-commerce. This sustained it in 2020 and against Amazon.
- Two forms of managerial action (Ridderstrale, 2003): (1) strengthen skills, knowledge, and experience, at any level; (2) coordinate and integrate groups and departments, best done from top management.

**Acquisition**

- Buying capabilities can be as worthwhile as buying products.
- Facebook and Oculus VR case: integrating virtual reality into the rest of Facebook was difficult because of incompatible systems and processes.
- Risk: a different culture and decision-making style reduce the productivity of what was acquired (Thompson et al., 2016).

**Collaborative partnerships (Barney and Hesterly, 2015)**

1. Outsource the weak function to a key supplier. It saves resources and provides focus; useful for small companies.
2. Collaborate in a JV (joint venture), alliance, or other partnership with complementary resources. Choose the partner for style, culture, and goals, not only for resources.
3. Partner to learn from the partner and internalize its methods. It works if both learn; if the benefit is one-sided, trust breaks down.

- Starbucks and Kraft case: Starbucks entered supermarkets with Kraft, learned, and broke the agreement. After three years and an arbitration, it paid 2.75 billion dollars to Mondelez, which was spun off from Kraft in 2012.
- Lesson: it still came out ahead. The agreement limited it to pods for Kraft's Tassimo machines, while Keurig's (Green Mountain) K-Cups were growing.
- Once free, it sold billions of K-Cups and surpassed 20% share in pods (Mandel, 2013).
- Exam note: the book presents the third approach as "developing" capabilities through partnerships, but the subtitle says "Assessing". From context, it is about obtaining or accessing capabilities.

#### 9.4.2 The Strategic Role of Employee Training

- Key when moving to a strategy with new skills and where technical know-how changes very fast. It must be well funded and effective.
- More than 4 thousand companies have internal "universities" and spend more than 370 billion dollars a year (Mazareanu, 2020).
- Practices: orientation of new hires, competency programs, tuition reimbursement, outside courses, professional certifications, and online courses at all hours.
- Shift in focus: before, only people with potential were trained; today everyone is, and development is the employee's own responsibility.
- For security: companies want business knowledge, not only technical expertise. Use ASIS International (formerly American Society for Industrial Security) for specialized content and agree with the supervisor on how to gain business skills.

#### 9.4.3 Strategy Execution Capabilities and Competitive Advantage

- Executing well is learned and becomes a capability. It is a direct source of advantage: it lowers costs, delivers more value, and makes it possible to reach the market sooner.
- It is socially complex, slow to build, hard to imitate, and has no substitute.
- If rivals easily copy the strategy, the only advantage is to out-execute them.

### 9.5 Matching Organizational Structure to the Strategy

- Universal rule: the structure must fit what implementing the strategy demands.
- Every organization chart reflects prior patterns, internal circumstances, criteria on reporting lines, and the politics of assignments. That is why it is unique.

#### 9.5.1 Deciding Which Value Chain Activities to Perform Internally and Which to Outsource

- An outsider with expertise may do it better or cheaper, and the company concentrates on its core.
- Cases: E.J. Gallo Winery outsources 95% of its grapes and the growers bear the weather risk. Other companies produce in China, India, and Vietnam and keep design, marketing, and sales (Quinn, 1992).
- Advantages: lower costs, more focus, less bureaucracy, faster decisions, and better capabilities.
- Where work is outsourced also matters: the pandemic broke supply chains.

Three benefits (Thompson et al., 2016):

| Benefit | Detail and examples |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Turning a competence into a distinctive competency | Focusing on a few critical activities lowers costs or improves quality. Whirlpool, Hugo Boss, ING Insurance, and Chevron outsourced data processing. Today: Microsoft Azure, Amazon Web Services, and Google Cloud Platform |
| 2. Streamlining internal operations | Less bureaucracy, flatter structure, quick decisions, and less complexity. Automakers that work with suppliers shorten the new-model cycle |
| 3. Adding capabilities with partners | Soft drink makers and brewers with bottlers and distributors; Wendy's and Burger King with their franchisees versus McDonald's |

- Cloud: advantages, simplicity, upfront cost, and full availability; disadvantages, sustainability, privacy, and security. Cybersecurity incidents are almost daily.
- Risks: the 2011 earthquake and tsunami (Fukushima) cut supplies to automakers; the 2020 pandemic, pharmaceutical ingredients.
- Do not overdo it: keep what is essential for the long term in-house.
- Except for key parts, components, and ingredients, what is outsourced tends to be less strategic. It includes customer inquiries, technical support, payroll, benefits and training, data processing, and risk and security (Pisano and Shih, 2009).

#### 9.5.2 Aligning the Firm's Organizational Structure with Its Strategy

- Organizational structure: the formal and informal arrangement of tasks, responsibilities, authority, reporting lines, and communication through which the company is managed.
- It conditions coordination and control (Olsen, Slater, and Hult, 2005). Without it there is confusion, internal politics, and bureaucratic inertia.
- A good design improves coordination and capabilities, lowers costs, and speeds up decisions.
- Critical activities are the core building blocks, with resources and decision-making power. Case: ski apparel (Sport Obermeyer, Arc'teryx, Spyder) revolves around design, cheap manufacturing, distribution, and marketing.
- The type of structure depends mostly on size, less on strategy, and evolves as the company grows.

Four basic types (Mintzberg, 1979):

| Type | Features | Advantages | Drawbacks and use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Simple structure | One central executive decides with little staff; flat; little specialization, few rules, informal dealings, little training and planning | Low administrative cost, easy coordination, flexibility, fast decisions; fosters creativity and accountability | Small companies and startups; it is the most common. It falls short as the company grows |
| Functional structure (departmental) | Units by value chain function: research and development, engineering and design, manufacturing, sales and marketing, logistics and security | Specialization, learning, economies of scale, and productivity | Turf wars that slow information and coordination; central control becomes a burden as the company diversifies |
| Multidivisional structure | Decentralized; divisions by market, product, or geography; headquarters that oversees, allocates resources, provides support (such as security), and controls | Less overhead; decisions close to the customer; lower coordination cost; more control; aligns incentives | Hinders the collaboration and synergies that related diversification requires |
| Matrix structure | Two or more dimensions at once (business, geography, value chain); multiple bosses | More communication and coordination; more balanced and collaborative oversight | Another management layer; more cost and slowness if there is confusion; divided loyalty and politicization |

**Functional**

- It is the best fit in a specific type of business. Case: Walmart (purchasing, warehouses, distribution, stores, advertising, customer service, and online sales).
- Also in high volume with related products and little vertical integration. Case: General Motors, all its brands in one functional structure.
- For security: security, risk, and safety usually end up inside finance, legal, human resources, or administration, as management decides.

**Multidivisional**

- Each division is a strategic business unit (SBU) and a profit center.
- The division runs its operation and strategy; the corporation monitors performance and sets the corporate strategy, which rules. Case: with a corporate low-cost strategy, an SBU cannot choose differentiation.
- Common in diversification or international strategy. Unrelated: one division per industry. Related: by industry, customer, product, region, or technology.
- In vertical integration: divisions by stage (production, assembly, wholesale, stores).
- For security: the budget is always 100%, never 150%. The functions that generate value and growth take it.
- For security: it is a support activity, not a primary one (Chapter 5). It must protect those who create value without hindering them with intrusive or unproductive processes.
- Exam note: in the functional structure the book places security among the "main components" of the value chain. Later it calls it a support activity. For the exam, security is a support activity.

**Matrix**

- Security case: a field security manager reports to his or her division, where the budget and daily work are. He or she also reports to the corporate chief security officer (CSO).
- Problem: conflicting direction, especially if the division controls the budget.
- In the 1960s it was formal, inefficient, and bureaucratic. The modern version is flexible: direction stays with the corporate head of the function, even if the budget remains in the field. Communication is key.
- For security: leading security involves organizational power and can set off power struggles. There is no guide; knowing they happen is half the battle. Choosing sides is a matter of deciding well (Chapter 2).

#### 9.5.3 Determining How Much Authority to Delegate

- It must be decided how much authority top management retains and how much it delegates to divisions, departments, plants, and employees.
- There are two approaches with opposite principles, each with pros and cons. Management decides; everyone else works within that decision.
- Pending: the book does not develop the two approaches or their pros and cons.

#### 9.5.4 Further Perspectives on Structuring the Work Effort

- Steps: choose a basic design; adapt it; add coordination mechanisms (cross-functional task forces, special project teams, self-managed teams); create communication networks.
- Building the organization is consciously joining the efforts of people and groups. Top management must be deeply involved.

## Capítulo 10 — Managing Internal Operations: Promoting Good Strategy Execution (págs. 299–328)

**Central idea:** Executing strategy well requires managing five internal levers: resources, policies, best practices and processes, information systems, and rewards. All must be aligned with the strategy and rest on objective data. The chapter closes with lessons for managing during and after the COVID-19 pandemic.

It covers tasks 4 through 8 of the 10 that drive execution: 4) allocate financial and human resources; 5) institute policies and procedures; 6) adopt and adapt best practices and processes; 7) install information and operating systems; 8) tie rewards and incentives to strategic and financial goals.

### 10.1 Allocating Resources to the Strategy Execution Effort

- At the outset, management decides what resources are needed and how to distribute them among units.
- It screens requests for staff and equipment: it approves those that contribute to execution and rejects the rest.
- If internal cash flow is not enough, it is financed with debt or the sale of new shares.
- It must be decided with critical thinking and objective data, not political criteria (Thompson, Peteraf, Gamble, and Strickland 2016).

| Error | Consequence |
| --- | --- |
| Underfunding | Slows progress and makes success harder |
| Overfunding units, often operating units that generate cash | Wastes resources, lowers financial performance, and sends the wrong message to other managers |

- A change of strategy almost always requires reallocating the budget: units with a smaller role are reduced and those with a bigger role are reinforced.
- New initiatives must be funded well and projects that are no longer justified must be canceled.
- Reallocating visibly signals commitment, speeds up implementation, and gives it credibility.
- Fine-tuning the current strategy calls for few changes; new initiatives require reviewing budgets line by line.
- If efficiency is the goal, all functions, including security, must demonstrate their value with data.
- Data are more than numbers (Albert 2018). Faced with the first results: what do they mean? How valid are they? What actions do they call for? What priorities should be set? Are we measuring the right thing?
- Answering requires judgment, intuition, prudence, and common sense, and must be repeated as conditions change.

### 10.2 Instituting Policies and Procedures that Facilitate Strategy Execution

When changes occur, it must be checked whether current policies still serve, or they must be revised or eliminated. They help in three ways (The Art of Service 2020):

| Way | How it helps |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Top-down guidance | They say how to do tasks and solve problems; they align behaviors and counter resistance to change |
| 2. Consistency in critical activities | They standardize quality and service, key with geographically dispersed units |
| 3. Favorable work climate | They set "how we do things around here"; changing policies is a lever for changing the culture |

- Example: McDonald's manual details the procedures of each unit; so do hospitals, hotels, and day care centers.
- Too much policy gets in the way and confuses.
- **Goldilocks Moment:** enough policies, applied consistently, to give direction and boundaries, empowering people to act within them.
- It is advisable to give freedom when creativity and initiative matter more than strict conformity.
- **Role of security:** review, draft, establish, execute, and enforce policies that protect the company, its employees, and its stakeholders.
- Without effective policies there can be injuries and deaths, with major legal liability, extra costs, and missed financial goals.

### 10.3 Adopting and Adapting Best Practices and Employing Process Management Tools

Performance is compared against three benchmarks (Bogan and English 1994): best-in-industry, best-in-world, and best-in-company, that is, internal units that do the same task elsewhere. They serve to set standards to match or exceed.

#### 10.3.1 How the Process of Identifying and Incorporating Best Practices Works

- **Benchmarking:** a tool for improving internal activities by learning how other companies in the same industry do them. It is the basis of the process (see Chapter 5).
- **Best practice:** a way of doing an activity that consistently delivers superior results; it can transcend industries.
- Exam note: the definition limits benchmarking to the same industry, but the chapter also applies it against "best-in-world" benchmarks from other sectors.
- Imitating is not enough: each practice is born of years of its own trial and error. One must **adapt, not adopt**.
- The true change agents are frontline employees (Ungan 2004).
- **Application to security:** ASIS International has created best practices; professionals believe they reduce legal liability.
- Serious error: applying them without considering strategy, structure, culture, and competence. Managers distrust what is "not-invented-here".
- The committee will ask where the practice comes from and how it fits the strategy. Showing that it is adapted gives security credibility.
- The more units use adapted practices, the closer operational excellence is.

#### 10.3.2 Business Process Improvement

- Problem: an activity spread across departments without an overall owner. Security is a typical case, with an inconsistent message and disorderly execution.
- **Business process reengineering:** radical redesign of an activity to achieve quantum improvements (Hammer and Champy 1993). It is carried out by a cross-functional team.

| Case | What it did | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hallmark | Mixed teams (artists, writers, lithographers, merchants, administrative staff) by card theme | Development 24 months faster, more efficiency and satisfaction |
| Northwest Water (British public utility) | Eliminated 45 depots; crews work from vehicles with terminals | No depots or bureaucracy; better scheduling (Hall, Rosenthal, and Wade 1993) |
| Unnamed company | Ideas by email; if management does not respond in 30 days, they are implemented | More than 6,000 ideas in the first year, with no financial incentives (Walker 2018) |

- Lesson from the third case: people get involved if they believe their ideas are valued.
- Pending: the book does not identify the company or the initial inefficiency in the third case; text seems to be missing. It later calls it a "manufacturing business".
- Criticism: reengineering is sometimes an excuse to cut staff.
- **Total Quality Management (TQM):** a culture of quality at all levels to continuously improve every activity in the value chain.
- **Six Sigma:** advanced statistical methods to reduce defects and variability in processes.

| Tool | Who applies it | Type of improvement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Best practices and reengineering | A manager or functional leader on his or her own | Big leaps |
| TQM and Six Sigma | They require a company-wide philosophy; an isolated manager would meet rejection | Incremental, after reengineering |

- Every tool needs top management's acceptance and a link to strategic priorities.
- Steps to take advantage of best practices and reengineering (Olian and Rynes 1991):
  1. Set visible, measurable objectives.
  2. Move employees toward behaviors that support them.
  3. Empower everyone to improve as part of their job.
  4. Spread the practices through online systems and exchange ideas.
  5. Insist on improving: rivals do not rest and customers want more.
- This is how a culture of operational excellence is born, the basis of competitive advantage. Tools are copied; culture is not.

### 10.4 Information and Operating Systems as a Means for Improving Strategy Execution

| Company | System and link to strategy |
| --- | --- |
| FedEx | Coordinates more than 100,000 vehicles and 11 million shipments daily to more than 220 countries and territories. One controller directs up to 200 of 650 aircraft. Online tools for customers. Competes with UPS, DHL, and the US Postal Service (UK Essays 2017) |
| Amazon | More than 130 warehouses in the US, China, and Europe; algorithms order the picking; it measures line speed, productivity, and weights. Supports its low cost (Uenlue 2018) |
| Siemens Healthcare | Cloud activity monitoring in more than 190 countries, for customer satisfaction (Siemens Healthcare 2020) |
| Caesar's Entertainment | Customer database; free upgrades according to spending (Caesar's Entertainment 2012) |

- Good systems create capabilities and advantage: superior quality control (differentiation), unmatched benchmarking (low cost), the ability to recruit and train en masse (fast growth).

#### 10.4.1 Instituting Adequate Information Systems, Performance Tracking, and Controls

- Real-time data: daily sales by store, production by shift, transport arrivals on screens.
- Five areas the systems must cover (Picincu 2018): 1) customers; 2) operations; 3) employees; 4) suppliers or strategic partners; 5) financial performance.
- Key performance indicators (KPIs) are reported in real time if possible. Cycle: track, gather information, diagnose, and correct.
- Three sources: statistics (the feel of the numbers), meetings (news), and personal contacts (the human dimension).

**Monitoring Employee Performance**

- Before 2020, supervision by proximity was already declining. At the pandemic peak, about 35% of US workers went remote.
- Data (Sutton 2020): remote meetings +10% (shorter) and after-hours messages +52%. Without body language, meetings leave results less clear and generate stress.
- It is hard to micromanage remotely; command and control is heading toward extinction (Culbert 2020). The focus shifts to results and removing obstacles.
- **Security example:** the new head of investigations does not take the case even if he or she knows more; he or she provides resources and directs the team.

### 10.5 Using Rewards and Incentives to Promote Better Strategy Execution

- A good reward structure is the most powerful tool for mobilizing commitment.
- It focuses attention, aligns personal motives with goals, and acts as a control mechanism that reduces supervision.
- One must understand what motivates each person and use monetary and nonmonetary incentives (Fawcett, Rhodes, and Burnah 2004).

#### 10.5.1 Incentives and Motivational Practices that Facilitate Good Strategy Execution

Monetary: base salary raises, bonuses, profit-sharing, stock, contributions to 401(k) or retirement plans, and piecework pay for laborers.

Nonmonetary (Brownlee 2019):

| Practice | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Attractive benefits | Health insurance, wellness, tuition, vacations, day care and gym, cafeteria, parental and family leave, scholarships, relocation. Telework went from a perk to a necessity in 2020 |
| Awards and recognition | They create esprit de corps, shape behaviors, and stimulate healthy competition |
| Promotion from within | Binds company and employee; evaluates bosses on developing people, sometimes in their bonus |
| Asking for and applying suggestions | Many improvements come from employees |
| Mutual respect and care | A "family" climate; collaboration and innovation |
| Inspiring vision | A "noble purpose" raises productivity and lowers turnover |
| Sharing information | Finances, strategy, market, and rivals; shows trust |
| Attractive environment | Physical and emotional comfort; in remote work, emotional support |

#### 10.5.2 Striking the Right Balance Between Rewards and Punishment

- **Up-or-out:** whoever does not deserve promotion leaves.
- **Forced ranking:** e.g., ranking 10 employees from 1 to 10 even if they differ little; those at the bottom go.
- There is no evidence that a pressure-free environment achieves better execution. But excess stress or fear is counterproductive.
- Rule: more positive elements than negative ones (Christensen, Marx, and Stevenson 2006).

#### 10.5.3 Linking Rewards to Achieving the Right Outcomes

- Reward what is achieved, not what is done (Kerr 1995). Low cost: reward savings. Differentiation: fewer defects and complaints, better delivery.
- Top management: revenue, profits, stock price, return on investment (ROI), creditworthiness, and market share. Managers and teams: metrics of their area.
- **Security example:** a retail security director with incentives for reducing shrink. Indicators: investigations, responsible parties identified, recovery or restitution, and new policies.

Tips for focusing on results (Kruse 2019; not exhaustive):

1. Comprehensive strategy as simple as possible.
2. Clear measurement of results.
3. Backing from all stakeholders.
4. Ambitious but achievable goals with each member.
5. Confirm that everyone understands the objectives.
6. Positive, enthusiastic environment.
7. Involve every employee in the mission.
8. Empower people to solve problems creatively.
9. Monitor productivity and tackle persistent poor performance.
10. Encourage reporting of problems.
11. Timely feedback.
12. Reinforce guidelines and end goals.

Guidelines for incentive compensation (Thompson et al. 2016; companies such as Cisco Systems, Salesforce, American Express, Hilton Hotels, and Edward Jones):

| Guideline | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Pay for performance as the larger part | Bonuses of at least 10–15% of base salary; 20% or more of total compensation draws a lot of attention; less than 5% motivates little. High performance is paid clearly more than average |
| 2. For everyone, not only the top | Inequity demotivates and causes turnover; percentages may vary by level |
| 3. Objectivity and fairness | Unrealistic goals or poorly documented evaluations cancel the benefit |
| 4. Goals the person can influence | Measuring what he or she does not control does not guide behavior |
| 5. Short time between achievement and reward | Bonuses are usually annual; a portion is usually tied to overall profitability |
| 6. Reward results, not effort | "No Excuses" approach; blaming the environment is admitting poor planning |

- Everyone must be told how each incentive is calculated. No incentive is one if it is guaranteed.
- **Security example:** a field manager focused on the bonus-linked objectives and ignored his or her daily tasks.
- Without an attractive reward the system breaks down and all that remains are orders and goodwill.

## Capítulo 11 — Corporate Culture and Its Impact on Strategy Execution (págs. 329–358)

**Central idea:** Corporate culture can be the best ally or the biggest obstacle to executing strategy. The manager must identify it, align it with the strategy, and change it if it is harmful. In addition, the manager must lead execution in person: monitor, apply constructive pressure, and correct.

Review of the 10 management tasks: Chapter 9 covered task 1 (staffing the organization with capable people), task 2 (acquiring and developing resources and capabilities), and task 3 (a structure that supports the strategy). Chapter 10 covered tasks 4 through 8. This chapter covers task 9 (instilling a culture conducive to good execution) and task 10 (strong leadership to drive execution and achieve operational excellence as soon as possible).

### 11.1 Instilling a Corporate Culture Conducive to Good Strategy Execution

- **Corporate culture:** shared values, deeply rooted attitudes, core beliefs, and traditions that determine norms of conduct, accepted practices, and operating style.
- It is summed up as "how we do things around here." It defines what is ethically acceptable and what is expected of the worker.
- It is the organizational DNA and shapes individual performance (Reid and Hubbell 2005).
- Peter Drucker, father of modern management: "culture eats strategy for breakfast."
- Many studies link healthy culture and results, but most companies barely work on their culture (Rick 2020). They take it for granted: every organization, even a bad one, has a culture and mechanisms that perpetuate it.

| Company | Features of its culture and lesson |
| --- | --- |
| Walmart | Obsession with low costs and frugality, work ethic, ritual meetings at headquarters, managers who visit stores and listen (Lombardo 2019) |
| Apple | Shaped by Steve Jobs: customer-focused, secretive, with pressure for performance and protective of its technology; it collaborates, but with secrecy even from family. **Application to security:** different codes for parts of the same project compartmentalize leaks, protect intellectual property, and help find those responsible (Pratap 2019) |
| W.L. Gore & Associates (GORE-TEX) | Teams and personal initiative: no organization charts, no formal chain of command or fixed channels; leaders emerge and change by project. It went from niche to diversified multinational (W.L. Gore & Associates 2020) |
| Nordstrom | Exceptional service and a motto of handling "unreasonable" requests; it promotes those who repeat acts of service. It agreed to take back a pair of shoes that were not its own (Person 2015) |

#### 11.1.1 Identifying Key Features of a Company's Corporate Culture

Where to look for the culture (Thompson et al. 2016):

1. Values, principles, and ethical standards that management preaches and practices.
2. People management and official policies, procedures, and practices.
3. Atmosphere of the work climate, in person or virtual: competitive or cooperative, innovative or change-averse, collegial or politicized, serious or fun.
4. Interaction between managers and employees: individual or collaborative, fluid or scarce communication, first-name dealings, socializing outside work.
5. Strength of peer pressure to comply with norms.
6. Behaviors that management rewards and those it disapproves of.
7. Traditions and repeated stories of "heroic acts."
8. Treatment of external stakeholders: suppliers as partners or a distant relationship; real commitment to corporate citizenship and sustainability.

- Origin: it usually arises from the founder or from strong leaders, from exemplary actions by staff, and from a consensus that evolves. Over time it becomes entrenched.

**The Role of Core Values and Ethics**

- The basis of almost every culture is core values and the ethical bar; that is why value statements and codes of ethics exist (Thompson et al. 2016).
- If they are merely cosmetic, when discovered employees leave and reputation suffers. Well rooted, they endure and recover after poor management.
- Managers promote them because they improve execution, reputation, and performance (Badaracco 1997), and reduce ethical lapses.
- Enron and WorldCom case: crimes, indictment of top executives, and collapse. Collateral damage: bankruptcy of the auditor Arthur Andersen, jobs, reputations, and retirement savings lost (Giroux 2008).
- Exam note: the book writes "Arthur Anderson"; the correct name is Arthur Andersen.
- Dual role of values and ethics: 1) they create shared convictions about how to do business; 2) they guide which behaviors are acceptable, as a yardstick for doing things right and doing the right thing.

**Embedding Behavioral Norms in the Organization and Perpetuating the Culture** — eight ways (Carasco and Singh 2003):

1. Hire candidates who fit the culture.
2. Include the culture in induction and training.
3. Have top management repeat the values at events and in communications.
4. Managers at every level as cultural role models.
5. Use cultural behavior in evaluations, raises, and promotions.
6. Have managers, down to frontline supervisors, explain the desired traits and why.
7. Encourage peer pressure to comply with norms.
8. Periodic ceremonies to honor those who embody the values.

- Companies with a tradition use word of mouth. To root values, strict norms and little tolerance for deviations are applied.
- **Cultural norms:** behaviors and beliefs shared by the members of a society, organization, or group.

**The Role of Stories** — they teach which behavior is revered and increase peer pressure:

- Zappos: a customer returned shoes that did not relieve her mother's pain and received flowers and free express shipping from then on.
- Trader Joe's: going beyond duty to make the customer's life easier.
- Apple: an iPad 2 was returned because the wife said no; Apple refunded the money, returned the device, and replied that Apple said yes (Conradt 2016).

**Forces That Cause a Company's Culture to Evolve**

Culture evolves like strategy and structure. Triggers: new market challenges; revolutionary technologies; crises, a change in top management, or a new strategic direction; a new chief executive officer (CEO), a major disruptor because he or she changes the key team; a massive influx of employees; diversification; geographic expansion; merger or acquisition.

#### 11.1.2 Strong Versus Weak Cultures

| Aspect | Strong culture | Weak culture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Feature | Deeply rooted values and norms that regulate behavior and climate | Shared values and norms are lacking |
| Leadership | Explains and repeats the values and walks the talk (Thompson et al. 2016) | No committed, stable leadership; little pressure to follow the company's way |
| Loyalty | To the company and its traditions | Only to the department, colleagues, or boss; the company is "just a job" (Kotter and Heskett 1992) |
| Effect on execution | Great support: values and bonds serve as leverage | Little or no help: there are no levers |

- Two results of demanding behavior consistent with the values (Deal and Kennedy 1999): 1) the values are shared, because whoever does not fit leaves; 2) strong peer pressure.
- Two historical factors: a strong founder or leader whose values are associated with success, and a sincere, lasting commitment to those traditions.
- Also helpful: continuity of leadership, low turnover, geographic concentration, and considerable success (Sathe 1985). They take years of deliberate work.
- They withstand leadership changes, but erode if a new CEO stops tending them or changes them aggressively.
- Their norms change little as strategy evolves: either the culture limits the options, or its traits are nearly neutral and compatible.

#### 11.1.3 Why Corporate Culture Matters to the Strategy Execution Process

- A strong culture may or may not be compatible with the strategy.
- Exam note: strong does not mean healthy; the book's summary clarifies that it can have a positive or negative impact.
- Cost leadership requires frugality and thrift. Differentiation and innovation require initiative, creativity, risk-taking, and openness to change.

Three ways an aligned culture helps (Thompson et al. 2016):

1. It focuses attention and guides decisions; it aligns the entire company and reduces direct supervision.
2. It generates peer pressure: group norms can shape behavior more than financial incentives.
3. It energizes staff, deepens commitment, and increases productivity.

- If it clashes with the strategy, it is an obstacle: the employee hesitates between tradition and the new behaviors (Kotter and Heskett 1992).
- The better the culture fits, the less need there is for policies, rules, procedures, and supervision.

#### 11.1.4 Healthy Cultures that Aid Good Strategy Execution

A strong culture that fits the strategy is healthy. Two other healthy types:

| Type | Features | How it is achieved or sustained |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High-performance culture | A "can-do" spirit, pride, accountability without excuses, results orientation, and stretch objectives; clear goals by company, unit, and employee; problems dealt with immediately (Thompson et al. 2016) | Inspire loyalty, reward the best also in public, eliminate bad habits, match talent to task, and learn from mistakes instead of punishing them (Reid and Hubbell 2005) |
| Adaptive culture | Willingness to adopt new strategies in the face of environmental change; receptiveness to risk, experimentation, and innovation; initiative is rewarded and prudent risks are taken (Thompson et al. 2016) | Two traits (Kanter 2008): 1) changes do not compromise core values; 2) they satisfy the legitimate interests of customers, employees, shareholders, suppliers, and communities |

- Change is accepted more readily if jobs are not at risk. Examples: technology, software, and internet companies; Apple created an industry with the iPhone.
- Apple, Alphabet, and Facebook hire flexible people and practice entrepreneurship and innovation (Patel 2015).

#### 11.1.5 Unhealthy Cultures that Impede Good Strategy Execution

They show counterproductive traits and clashes between management and workers or between groups. Five types:

| Type | Features | Examples and consequences |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Change-resistant | Fear of change; reward for not making mistakes; the status quo is protected; whoever proposes changes is "troublesome" | Bureaucracies with past success: Sears, Borders Books, J.C. Penney, Xerox, IBM, and General Motors lost share (Thompson et al. 2016) |
| 2. Politicized | Internal struggles; "empire-building" managers with fiefdoms; distrust of collaborating; decisions made by coalitions with vested interests | Frustrates those who seek the good of the company |
| 3. Insular, inwardly focused | They believe they have all the answers; ignore the customer; arrogance; "must-be invented here" mentality; they do not hire outside managers | They overestimate their capabilities and underestimate rivals |
| 4. Unethical and greedy | Ego, greed, and "the end justifies the means"; lapses are tolerated if they generate revenue; doctored accounting | Turing Pharmaceuticals, Enron, BP, AIG, Three Oceans Shipping, Countrywide Financial, WorldCom, Tyco, and Global Crossing (Eichenwald 2005) |
| 5. Clashing subcultures | Subcultures by department, location, division, or function with opposing philosophies vying for dominance | No consensus; fragmented execution (Booz & Company 2014) |

- Outcomes: IBM, Xerox, and General Motors rebuilt their culture. Borders liquidated under Chapter 7 of the US bankruptcy law; Sears and J.C. Penney filed under Chapter 11 to restructure debt.
- Subcultures are not a problem if they are compatible with the overall culture.
- Exam note: the book recommends promotion from within (Ch. 10), but criticizes insular cultures that never hire from outside; the best combine both for the fresh ideas.

#### 11.1.6 Changing a Problem Culture

- If a strong culture is harmful or does not fit, it must be changed as soon as possible. It is one of the hardest tasks: people cling to what is familiar.
- The most visible success factor is capable leadership at the top; only top management, especially the CEO, has enough power (Hout and Carter 1995).
- The entire management team takes part: middle managers and supervisors implement the practices and win over the rank and file.

Steps (Bossidy and Charan 2002):

1. Identify the dysfunctional traits.
2. Define the new desired behaviors and traits.
3. Convince people of why the current culture harms and how the new one will improve performance.
4. The most important: visible, forceful actions, both substantive and symbolic. Those who resist in key positions can be replaced.

**Making a Compelling Case for Cultural Change** — communicate (Bossidy and Charan 2002): 1) why certain current behaviors get in the way; 2) how the new ones will deliver better results, with stories and examples; 3) if the strategy changes, why and how the new culture will help execute it.

- The CEO speaks to the whole company. Frontline supervisors and opinion leaders must be won over.
- As long as the great majority does not accept it, the change is only an aspiration (Thompson et al. 2016).

**Substantive Culture Changing Actions** (Thompson et al. 2016):

1. Replace key executives who obstruct the change.
2. Promote those who lead it and serve as models.
3. Appoint outsiders with the desired attributes to visible positions.
4. Hire only candidates who fit.
5. Mandatory cultural training for everyone.
6. Incentives that pay more to those who show the desired behaviors.
7. Publicize that raises were given to those who supported the change with results.
8. Review policies and procedures.

- Launch enough actions at the start to leave no doubt, and sustain the effort until the new way becomes "the standard."

**Symbolic Culture Changing Actions**

- The most important: top management leads by example. With a low-cost strategy, managers must be frugal.
- **Anonymous case:** a low-cost company with austere offices but a luxurious executive wing, private bathrooms, and an exclusive dining room. There were clashes between management and the rank and file and it nearly went bankrupt after COVID-19.
- **Walmart:** simple executive offices and jealous cost control (Glassdoor 2016).
- **Nucor Steel:** managers in economy class and taxis, not limousines; every proposal is evaluated for its efficiency (ICMR 2003).
- Ceremonies to honor examples and celebrate progress; presence at training sessions; every meeting reinforces values. Analogy: military ribbons and medals.

**How Long Does It Take to Change a Problem Culture?** There is no fixed timeline. The larger the organization, the longer it takes. In a large company it is harder to reform an entrenched culture than to create one from scratch in a new organization.

### 11.2 Leading the Strategy Execution Process

- Top management leads and sets the pace: it goes out into the field, spots obstacles, and uses its influence to clear them, with constructive and constant pressure.
- Sequence: understand the requirements, diagnose the organization's readiness, and decide how to proceed.
- Three actions for the manager in charge, whatever the position (Montgomery 2008): 1) monitor progress; 2) exert constructive pressure to achieve operational excellence; 3) initiate corrective actions.

#### 11.2.1 Staying on Top of How Well Things Are Going

- Sources (Thompson et al. 2016): key subordinates, latest results, rivals' reactions, visits to customers and suppliers, visits to facilities, and conversations with staff at all levels.
- **Management by walking around:** listen to those who are close to the customer. In-depth conversations, not a staged dog and pony show.
- It makes it possible to see progress, problems, and needs, and also to encourage and show commitment (Bossidy and Charan 2002).

#### 11.2.2 Mobilizing the Effort for Excellence in Strategy Execution

Practices for a results-oriented climate (Kanazawa and Mikes 2008):

| Practice | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Employees as valued partners | Training, good pay, promotion from within, career, and job stability |
| 2. Esprit de corps | Morale, pride, teamwork, and emotional commitment |
| 3. Empowerment | Delegate to middle and lower managers; let the rank and file act on their own initiative |
| 4. Stretch objectives | Goals beyond what is expected; they raise effort, morale, and pride |
| 5. Proven tools | Benchmarking, best practices, reengineering |
| 6. Motivation and incentives | Innovation is cultivated with autonomy; a failure is not punished, or risk aversion arises |
| 7. Celebrating successes | Individual, group, and company |

- Poor performance has proportionate consequences, without creating fear of failure.

#### 11.2.3 Leading the Process of Making Corrective Adjustments

- If there are no results, the leader assesses whether the strategy or its execution is failing. Success requires analysis, good judgment, and good implementation.
- Whoever does not show measurable progress on time is a candidate for replacement.
- In a crisis, quick action (even while reviewing data and options). If there is room, incremental commitment to a course of action (Thompson et al. 2016).
- Process, the same for proactive and reactive decisions: 1) understand the situation; 2) gather information and create options; 3) explore pros and cons; 4) propose actions; 5) seek consensus; 6) formally adopt the agreed course.
- The timeline depends on complexity, time, and resources. There is no generic template: critical thinking is required.
- Three tests of good strategic leadership (Thompson et al. 2016): 1) a good strategy consistent with the business model; 2) competent execution; 3) goals met or exceeded.

## Capítulo 12 — Key Business Measurements and Metrics: A Relevant Discussion for Security Professionals (págs. 359–392)

**Central idea:** The security professional does not need to be a financier, but does need to speak the “language of business” to compete for resources. The chapter reviews budgets, financial statements, key financial ratios, and two nonfinancial measures. Whoever argues best with numbers gets more budget.

### 12.1 Financial Planning and Budgeting

- Financial planning is the basis of the budget. No strategy is executed without funding the functions of the value chain.
- Case: a security director took three years to obtain resources that had already been agreed. He or she lacked the ability to argue from business reality; after learning metrics, he or she succeeded.
- Long-term planning has limits because of uncertainty. Example: no one foresaw the pandemic; Boeing and the airlines suffered from it.
- McKinsey surveyed 127 chief financial officers (CFOs) (Agrawal, Maloney, Seth, and Birshan 2020).
- Result: 43% want to simplify the budget to react quickly; 65% expect to use rolling forecasts after 2021.
- Common factor after the COVID-19 (coronavirus disease of 2019) pandemic: more speed and cost control.
- Companies had to decide faster, raise productivity, use technology and data in new ways, and accelerate innovation.

Pandemic achievements, stepping outside the prescribed budget (De Smet, Pacthod, Relyea, and Sternfels 2020):

| Achievement | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Redeploying talent | Global telecommunications company: 1,000 store employees moved to inside sales, retrained in three weeks |
| New business model | US retailer: curbside delivery in two days, versus 18 months planned |
| Productivity | Factory at more than 90% capacity with 40% of the workforce |
| New products | Engineering: ventilators designed and built in one week |
| Changing operations | Shipyard: from three shifts to two, coordinating thousands of employees with local authorities |

- The typical budget, bottom up or top down, gets bogged down negotiating details.
- Redesigned plans: multiple scenarios; budget by key areas and cost categories; central pools of flexible funds; monthly reviews focused on value.
- Security is a support function and weighs less on profitability.
- The budget is a 100% pie: whoever argues best wins the bigger slice.
- Two consequences for security: more budget scrutiny and more use of zero-based budgeting.

### 12.2 Key Measurements

Exam note: the book numbers “12.1.1 Budgeting” inside 12.2; this is a numbering error.

**Costs.** Budgeting is listing monthly costs and allocating funds to goals.

| Aspect | Fixed costs (overhead) | Variable costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nature | Do not depend on output; tied to time; hard to reduce | Fluctuate; the manager is answerable for them, even in his or her bonus |
| Examples | Rent or mortgage; loan payments; insurance; vehicle lease or credit; equipment (machinery, tools, computers); payroll; landline telephone and internet | Commission-based salaries or contractors; electricity, gas, and water according to activity; raw materials; shipping; repair and maintenance; credit card fees |

**Types of budget**

| Type | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Master budget | Projection for the entire company, normally for the fiscal year. It projects the income statement, balance sheet, and cash flow: revenue, expenses, operating costs, sales, and capital expenditures. Basis of all operating budgets (functions, departments, divisions, units) |
| Operating budget | Revenue and expenses of daily operations, by day or month. In a crisis it is reviewed every month and unused funds are “swept”: leftover travel expenses go back to the master budget, with no carryover to the next month |

**Traditional Budgeting.** It adjusts the prior year's budget, almost always with an increase for inflation or growth. Only new expenses are analyzed in depth; existing line items receive at least a token increase. Its benefit is simplicity.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Solid, easy-to-control framework, with the prior year as a reference | More human error from many spreadsheets |
| Decentralizes; top management devotes itself to the strategic | Time-consuming; adjustments add uncertainty |
| It becomes part of the culture and perpetuates itself; changing it is risky | Does not foster innovation |
| — | Doubtful alignment between spending and strategy |
| — | Inaccurate or obsolete predictions, as in 2020 (Wallstreetmojo 2020) |

- It works in small companies, with few departments and no global complexity. In large ones it presents challenges.
- Trend: more and more companies, regardless of size or revenue, are migrating to zero-based.

**Zero-Based Budgeting (ZBB).** Every expense is justified and approved in each period, from zero. It emerged in the 1970s and is usually annual. It rejects the perpetual “cost-plus” approach; nothing is untouchable. Goal: costs that enable innovation and value.

Steps (The Street 2020):

1. Forecast available revenue or loans.
2. Set goals for the period (e.g., raise revenue by a percentage).
3. From the revenue goal (top-line), work backward: what resources are needed.
4. Cost those resources and see whether they help the goal.
5. Evaluate all expenses to see whether they make sense.
6. Implement the budget.
7. Repeat the next period (generally annual).

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Detailed annual review; what supports the goals is funded | Favors primary activities (Chapter 5) with tangible revenue or return |
| Detects costs that rise every cycle without adding value | Support services (security, human resources, accounting) get less budget |
| Avoids increases due to inflation or inertia | Encourages short-termism; cutting research and development or technology damages the future |
| — | Consumes many resources; requires commitment from employees and management |

- Argument for security: it does not generate direct revenue, but poor security harms growth through litigation or extra operating expenses.

### 12.3 Key Metrics

- Profitability ratios: profit relative to sales, costs, assets, and equity. Two groups: margin ratios and return ratios.
- Example: gross margin higher in the first quarter than in the fourth, even though the fourth sold more. Probable cause: higher operating costs. Earning more is not the same as being healthy.
- Exam note: the book puts liquidity and leverage ratios under “profitability”; it itself calls the current ratio a liquidity ratio.

**Liquidity**

| Metric | Formula | What it indicates | Reading |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cash ratio (or cash asset ratio), 12.3.1 | (Cash + equivalents) ÷ current liabilities | Paying debts of 12 months or less with cash alone | Greater than 1.0: covers everything with some left over; ample liquidity for creditors |
| Operating cash flow ratio, 12.3.2 | Operating cash flow ÷ current liabilities | Times operations cover the liabilities; allows for accruals | 1.5 to 2.0: covers 1.5 to 2 times. Less than 1.0: insufficient. Higher, greater safety margin. Used by lenders |
| Current ratio (or working capital ratio) | Current assets ÷ current liabilities | Paying obligations due within a year | Greater than 1.0: pays its liabilities for the year. Uses current figures, not totals |

- Equivalents: money market, Treasury bills, anything convertible into cash.
- Current liabilities: accrued, short-term debt, and accounts payable within a year.
- In the current ratio, add salaries, taxes payable, and the short-term portion of long-term debt.
- Current assets: cash, accounts receivable, inventory, and anything convertible in less than a year.
- Operating cash flow = net income + noncash expenses + changes in working capital.
- Cited irony: banks lend more to those who need it least.
- Exam note: the book says a current ratio of 2.5 pays liabilities “for two and a half years.” That is incorrect: current assets equal 2.5 times current liabilities.

```latex
\text{Cash ratio} = \frac{25{,}000 + 100{,}000}{30{,}000 + 25{,}000} = \frac{125{,}000}{55{,}000} = 2.27
```

Cash $25,000 and equivalents $100,000; accounts payable $30,000 and short-term debt $25,000. It covers its liabilities 2.27 times; arithmetic correct.

**Margin**

| Metric | Formula | What it indicates | Reading |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gross profit margin | (Gross profit ÷ sales) × 100; gross profit = sales − COGS | Product profitability | High is good, but its monthly or quarterly stability matters |
| Operating profit margin | (Operating profit ÷ sales) × 100 | Current earning power and efficiency before taxes | A rise is healthy; the best ratio for comparing with the industry; more objective than net |
| Net profit margin (or profit margin) | (Net income ÷ sales) × 100 | Overall view, as a percentage or decimal | Compare with industry standards |

- COGS includes direct materials, direct labor, inventory, and indirect costs of the product.
- Gross margin only varies sharply because of industry-wide changes: supply disruption or supplier price increases.
- Operating margin stagnates if expenses rise. Managers compare the percentages of two or more quarters side by side.
- High net margin: financial services, pharmaceutical, medical, and real estate. Consumer products: more conservative.

**Return and investment**

| Metric | Formula | What it indicates | Reading |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Return on assets (ROA) | (Net income before taxes ÷ total assets) × 100 | Asset efficiency | Higher, more efficient; compare across periods and with the sector |
| Return on investment (ROI) | (Current value − cost) ÷ cost | “Is it worth it?” | Positive, probably worth it; choose the highest. Weak over the long term and in capital budgeting |
| Internal rate of return (IRR) | No formula in the book; use Excel's IRR function | Growth with the time value of money | Greater than the hurdle rate: accept; lower: reject |
| Hurdle rate | No simple formula | Minimum required return | More risk, higher rate; subjective and different by company and industry |
| Return on equity (ROE) | Net income ÷ shareholders' equity | Return on net assets; management effectiveness | Around 14% (average of the Standard & Poor's 500 index) acceptable; less than 10% poor |

```latex
ROI = \frac{\text{Current value of investment} - \text{Cost of investment}}{\text{Cost of investment}}
```

- Exam note: the book prints the ROI without parentheses; read it as (current value − cost) ÷ cost.
- Exam note: the book's ROA uses “net income before taxes,” even though it defines net income as after taxes. Memorize the book's version.
- ROI: versatile and simple. Examples: a stock, expanding a factory, a property.
- ROI is more common because IRR is confusing. In capital budgeting both are used.
- ROI overstates profitability, is the same over the whole life of the project, and ignores the time value of money. IRR corrects this and controls for the assumed reinvestment rate.
- IRR in Excel: combine the initial outlay and the later inflows; Excel finds the discount rate. Better, ask finance for support.
- Hurdle rate: management weighs risks, cost of capital, and the return of other options. It serves to decide among internal opportunities.
- Exam note: the book also defines it as net gain as a percentage of the initial cost, which is a rate of return. Stick with “minimum required return.”
- ROE: equity = assets − debt. It uses the profit of the trailing 12 months.
- ROE best practice: use the average equity of the period, because of the lag between statements.

**Leverage**

| Metric | Formula | What it indicates | Reading |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Debt ratio | Total debt ÷ total assets | Proportion of assets financed with debt | Greater than 1.0 (100%): more debt than assets. Lower: good. Utilities and energy pipelines, higher than technology or services |
| Debt-to-equity (D/E) | Total liabilities ÷ shareholders' equity | Operation financed with debt versus own funds | Higher, more risk. Automotive, more than 1.0; technology companies, around 0.5; compare only within the industry (e.g., biotechnology) |

- Exam note: the debt ratio uses “total debt”; the D/E uses “total liabilities.”
- With loan covenants tied to revenue, downturns such as those of 2008–2009 and the pandemic lead to default.
- Possible outcome: Chapter 7 (liquidation) or Chapter 11 (restructuring).
- For the D/E, only long-term debt is usually counted: short-term debt threatens viability; long-term debt allows renegotiating the rate.
- Leverage: using borrowed money or financial instruments to raise return; or the debt that finances assets. It avoids issuing shares that dilute.
- “Highly leveraged” = more debt than equity. Types: operating, financial, and combined. It multiplies downside risk.

**EBITDA: Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation and Amortization**

It measures operating performance before capital structure, leverage, and noncash items. It is used as a proxy for cash flow and, sometimes, in place of net income.

```latex
EBITDA = \text{Net income} + \text{Interest} + \text{Taxes} + \text{Depreciation} + \text{Amortization}
```

| Element | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Interest | Paid on loans that finance the business |
| Taxes | Vary by jurisdiction; reduced via depreciation or amortization |
| Depreciation | The actual decline in value of a tangible asset through use, and the accounting allocation of its cost to the periods of use, down to zero |
| Amortization | Charging the cost of an intangible as an expense over its useful life to lower the taxable base. Examples: milestone payments, patents, and loans |

- Book's warning: do not confuse depreciation with the ROI calculation; it damages credibility.
- Pending: the example of extraction from the income statement (Corporate Finance Institute 2020) is an image with no legible text.
- It is not mandatory in the United States under Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP), but it is calculated from the statements.
- It compares profitability across companies and industries by eliminating the effects of financing and accounting decisions. It measures the ability to pay debt.
- Banks set a debt/EBITDA cap in covenants; breaching it makes the loan callable. Rating agencies use it to estimate default and downgrade ratings.
- Exam note: the book calls that cap a “revenue level,” but it is a leverage ratio. Also, a loan is not an intangible asset.

### 12.4 Non-Financial Measurements

**12.4.1 SWOT Analysis: strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats.** Seen in Chapter 3. It depends on context and timing; one from 3 to 6 months ago is already problematic.

Purposes:

1. Understand the challenges and the capacity to face them.
2. Organize internal and external data.
3. Provide a fresh, current perspective.
4. Draw conclusions to create strategy.

It is balanced (positive and negative, internal and external) and reveals non-obvious issues. It requires time, an open mind, and commitment to the facts.

**12.4.2 Environmental Scanning.** Seen in Chapter 4. It filters information to detect external or competitive trends and changes, and thus be proactive. It uses open sources, not data theft.

- Questions: Who are the competitors? What have they done? What are they doing? What could they do? How would it affect us?
- It produces competitive intelligence, the basis of benchmarking and best practices, without espionage.
- Commercial sources: advertisements, promotional material, press releases, news, the internet, trade magazines, trade shows, and databases.
- Other sources: government reports, such as the quarterly and annual filings with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), and professional relationships.
- Many companies buy rival products to analyze their quality and service.
- The only barrier: curiosity and analytical ability.

### 12.5 Final Thoughts

1. Lean on finance and accounting to evaluate projects and show their value. It improves the proposal and builds relationships.
2. Avoid paralysis by analysis. It occurs when:
   - the options overwhelm;
   - the urge to analyze everything complicates the simple;
   - one seeks the “perfect” when there is something “satisfactory” (satisficing);
   - there is fear of erring or the organization punishes mistakes (risk aversion).

- Business is not rocket science, where a minimal error costs missions and lives. Even so, management rejects analyses it does not understand or that are poorly designed.
- Security, as a support function, must back the activities that make money: minimize risks and reduce costs.
- Metrics do not solve everything, but they make it possible to defend the business impact of security recommendations.

## Apéndice — Standards in Security (págs. 393–396)

**Central idea:** Standards can be voluntary, statutory, or mixed and help protect assets and build resilience. Management system standards revolve around the Plan-Do-Check-Act cycle.

### A.1 What Is a Standard

- Standard: a set of criteria, guidelines, and good practices that improves the quality and reliability of products, services, or processes.
- They allow parts, processes, and systems to work regardless of who creates or uses them or where.
- They arise from a market need or from a gap in existing standards.
- They are coordinated by accredited standards developing organizations (SDOs), which bring together private and public actors.
- They are the basis of innovation and a common language for trade.
- They are developed on four principles: consensus, openness, due process, and transparency.
- In security they help nations, communities, societies, organizations, and individuals face natural and human threats.

### A.2 Voluntary, Statutory or Regulatory, Mixed Standards

| Type | Feature | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Voluntary standards | Not mandatory but widely adopted; some already integrated into the regulation of several countries. | International Organization for Standardization (ISO) and national institutes. In the US, associations such as ASIS International. |
| Statutory or regulatory standards | Binding by law; enforced by formal authorities. | No examples in the book. |
| Mixed standards | Voluntary ones incorporated into laws, or voluntary in theory but mandatory in practice. | Fire, construction, and life safety. Insurers that set coverage and price according to approved materials or practices. Contracts that require standards. |

- ISO topics useful in asset protection:
  - Facility management.
  - Health, environment, and safety.
  - Risk management.
  - Security and resilience.
  - Information technology (IT) and information security.
- Other standards issuers:
  - National Fire Protection Association (NFPA): not all are voluntary; some pass into regulation, such as building codes.
  - Underwriters Laboratories (UL): security equipment such as locks, alarms, and access control.
  - Illuminating Engineering Society: lighting standards and practices.
  - Electronic Industries Association: electronic components and products.
- ASIS International maintains a broad library of security management standards and guidelines.
- The adoption of standards is increasingly customer-driven.

### A.3 Benefits of Standards

A standard can do one or all of these things:

1. Codify good practices and processes, and share lessons learned.
2. Provide tools to assess threats, risks, vulnerabilities, criticalities, and impacts.
3. Define measurement methods.
4. Document equipment performance requirements for effectiveness and safety.
5. Set design requirements so that devices, systems, and infrastructure withstand threats.
6. Define effective methods to identify people.
7. Increase knowledge and competence.
8. Improve information sharing and interoperability across jurisdictions.
9. Give consistency to services.

### A.4 Management System Standards

- They help improve how services are delivered and processes are executed; widely used in many fields.
- The most prominent: ISO 9001 (quality management systems) and ISO 14001 (environmental management systems).
- They analyze the requirements of the organization and of stakeholders and define processes for success.
- They provide a framework for continual improvement toward strategic, operational, tactical, and reputational objectives.

### A.5 Plan-Do-Check-Act Cycle

It is the operating principle of management system standards. It solves problems in a structured way, oriented to continual improvement.

| Phase | According to the text | According to Figure A.1 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plan | Identify and analyze problems and assets; rank them by importance. | Define and analyze an issue and its context. |
| Do | Prioritize steps, prepare a detailed action plan, and execute it. | Devise a solution, prepare the action plan, and apply it systematically. |
| Check | Verify whether the solutions deliver results consistent with the plan. | Confirm results against the plan; detect deviations and problems. |
| Act | If they work, standardize them across the organization, review the list of problems, and define new ones. The cycle starts over. | Standardize the solution; review and define the next issues. |

Figure A.1 — taken from ASIS ORM.1-2017, “Security and Resilience in Organizations and their Supply Chains – Requirements with Guidance”. Elements:

- Stakeholders and Interested Parties with their Requirements and Expectations.
- Organizational Resilience Management Systems, which apply the PDCA model.
- Stakeholders with Managed Risk as the result.

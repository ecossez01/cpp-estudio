## Capítulo 1 — Crisis, Emergency, and Business Continuity Management (págs. 1–8)

**Central idea:** Emergency management, crisis management, and business continuity are three distinct disciplines that are often used as synonyms. The first addresses the immediate impact; the second is strategic and protects viability and reputation; the third returns operations to normal. Together they form the organization's resilience.

### Chapter Introduction (unnumbered in the book)

- The three terms (emergency management, crisis management, business continuity management) describe the response to disruptive events and the management of their impacts.
- The chapter focuses on planning and activities for high-impact events.

**Evolution of industry terminology**

| Stage | Terms used | Focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Early days of the industry | contingency planning · information technology (IT) recovery | Contingency and recovery plans for information technology |
| Middle stage | disaster planning · disaster preparedness · emergency response | Distinguishing the different aspects of preparation |
| Today | crisis management · business continuity | Including crises that are not natural disasters |
| Current umbrella term | resilience | Groups the risk management, security management, and crisis management programs |

**The three areas of the chapter**

| Area | What it is | Time horizon · example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Emergency management/response | Planning and activity to detect, contain, and address the immediate impact of the event | Immediate · putting out the fire in a blaze · locking down the network in a cyber breach |
| Crisis management | Strategic process that supports emergency response and continuity. At the same time it addresses the issues that threaten long-term viability. | Long term · aftermath of the destructive event · effects on reputation and other nonoperational aspects |
| Business continuity | Processes and procedures to move from functioning during the crisis to operating normally after a business interruption | Transition back to normal |

- The three areas together make it possible to fulfill the mission while the company recovers from the event.
- **Key point:** emergency = immediate and tactical; crisis = strategic and long term; continuity = returning to normal operations.

### 1.1 Emergency Management and Response: An Overview

- **Emergency management (definition):** any formal process that an organization uses to prepare for and respond to disasters and emergencies.
- Many methodologies divide it into a cycle of four elements: mitigation, preparedness, response, and recovery.
- The cycle originated for United States (U.S.) government agencies.
- It is applied almost universally to governments and businesses, with variations in terminology and implementation.

#### The Four Phases of the Cycle

| Phase | Definition | Examples · source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mitigation | Protective measures to reduce the likelihood of a disaster, or its impact if it occurs. It reduces damage before the event. | Flood barriers · fire-rated structural elements · bolting cabinets to the wall in seismic zones |
| Preparedness | Any activity, program, or system created and implemented before the incident. It supports and reinforces mitigation, response, and recovery from disruptions, disasters, or emergencies. | Source: ANSI/ASIS PAP.1-2021 (Physical Asset Protection) |
| Response | Executing the plan and providing services to preserve and protect life and property, and to care for the surviving population. In continuity, the response must support the mission, consider the interests of stakeholders, and assess the impact of the disruption. | Source: ASIS BCM 2021 |
| Recovery | Restoring processes, resources, and capabilities to meet ongoing operational requirements. It may include major organizational improvements, even reorienting strategic or operational objectives. | Source: ASIS BCM 2021 |

- Acronyms: ANSI = American National Standards Institute (information from outside the book). ASIS BCM 2021 = ASIS International's business continuity management (BCM) guideline, 2021.

#### Emergency Management Is Largely Generic

- Every emergency requires certain common responses.
- Even so, the character of each situation must be understood in order to respond optimally.
- Specific training and equipment are needed for each type of emergency likely in each industry and facility.

### 1.2 Crisis Management: An Overview

- **Definition (ASIS BCM 2021):** a holistic management process that identifies potential impacts that threaten the organization.
  - It provides a framework for building resilience and responding effectively.
  - That response protects the interests of key stakeholders, reputation, brand, and value-creating activities.
  - It also effectively restores operational capabilities.
- It is part of the event response and recovery program.
- **When it starts:** upon notification of any disruptive event, whether it is:
  - a physical emergency;
  - a nonphysical event: product recall or regulatory, legal, or financial problems.
- It is holistic: all of the organization's functions take part in the response.
- Impacts on brand and reputation are an important part. They require coordination by executive management and the public-facing communications teams.
- **Ultimate objective:** to protect core assets from the greatest possible harm caused by an event that interrupts the business:
  - reputation;
  - brand;
  - financial well-being;
  - trust;
  - physical and intellectual property;
  - key relationships.
- It must look at how to sustain the mission while the organization recovers, once the continuity and recovery phase has begun.
- **A healthy crisis management program:**
  - prepares all functions to support all affected levels;
  - prevents one functional area from dominating the program.

#### 1.2.1 Crisis Management Principles

- Management usually responds with limited information and little time.
- For that reason, every disruptive event must be communicated to the crisis management team (CMT) as soon as it occurs, so that the team can assess it.
- Crisis planning sets criteria to distinguish two cases:

| Type of event | Characteristics | Who manages it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Crisis | Requires support from other functions of the organization | The crisis management team, with support from other functions |
| Routine incident | Probably does not require support from other functions | The incident commander or another senior person in charge of the response |

- Assessing and responding to a crisis is a complex management decision. It requires procedures, planning, training, and rehearsal.
- All employees must know how to report and escalate an event as soon as they discover it.

### 1.3 Business Continuity Management: An Overview

| Source | Definition of business continuity management (BCM) |
| --- | --- |
| ASIS BCM 2021 | A proactive set of planning, preparation, and related activities. It seeks to restore critical business functions to predetermined levels. That way the organization operates despite serious disruptions and quickly returns to an operational state. |
| DRI International 2016 | A management process that identifies risks, threats, and vulnerabilities that could affect the continuity of operations. It provides a framework for building organizational resilience and effective response capability. |

- DRI = Disaster Recovery Institute (information from outside the book; the book does not spell out the acronym).
- **Key point:** DRI's definition of BCM closely resembles ASIS's definition of crisis management (identify threats, resilience framework, effective response). ASIS's definition for BCM focuses on restoring critical functions to predetermined levels.

**Continuity strategies**

| Strategy | What it consists of |
| --- | --- |
| Resumption and recovery in place | Recovering the functions at the same location |
| Contracting out | Outsourcing selected functions |
| Relocation | Moving critical functions and personnel to one or more sites, owned or contracted from third parties |

- The choice depends on the nature and needs of the business.
- **Objective, whatever the strategy:**
  1. resume critical functions as soon as possible;
  2. return the business to its pre-emergency condition and location;
  3. if that is not possible, take it to a new location or a new level of operation.
- There is no single formula for structuring a BCM plan. Good practices and frameworks adapt to:
  - the culture;
  - the available resources;
  - the industry;
  - the complexity of the business;
  - the company's level of maturity.

## Capítulo 2 — General Event Response and Management (págs. 9–16)

**Central idea:** Every response begins with a plan that defines the crisis, creates teams, provides for resources, manages entry into and exit from crisis mode, and is tested. The chapter explains the emergency response structure (coordinator, committee, alternates, incident command), coordination with external actors, and how to set up the crisis management team.

### Chapter Introduction (unnumbered in the book)

- In a disruptive event, decisions must be made while the event is still under way and without knowing its true scope.
- Some decisions affect the organization's health for years. Others, its immediate ability to survive.
- An emergency or crisis can overwhelm those who did not plan or prepare.
- **Logical starting point:** develop a plan that fulfills five functions (ASIS BCM 2021, ASIS International's business continuity management guideline):
  1. Define the term and scope of crisis or emergency in terms relevant to the organization.
  2. Create a group or team with specific tasks before, during, and after the disruptive event.
  3. Establish a method for using available resources and obtaining additional resources during the event.
  4. Provide a means to shift normal operations into crisis mode and back out of it.
  5. Provide a plan and a framework for ongoing testing and maintenance of the plan and response capabilities.
- Each phase of the disruptive event must have its own plans and teams.

### 2.1 Emergency and Response Teams

- Response personnel must be ready for every type of emergency, even those not foreseen when the plan was written.
- The main training focuses on:
  - the most likely situations;
  - the location, construction, size, and function of each site.
- In large organizations with several sites, two levels of plan are needed:
  - a general emergency operations plan (EOP) to coordinate all sites;
  - site-specific plans, adapted to their particular exposures.
- Response requires organization and structure. Defining teams and structures in advance allows each member to:
  - understand his or her role;
  - train for it;
  - be reminded of it by consulting the plan during the event.

#### 2.1.1 Structure

**Emergency coordinator**

- A single operating executive is designated as the organization's emergency coordinator.
- Functions: to take responsibility for the plan and to keep responsibilities and boundaries between departments from slowing the response.
- Profile: someone who routinely manages emergencies, such as the head of security or of engineering.
- He or she must deal effectively with executives and employees at all levels.
- The appointment is documented in two places:
  - the emergency operations plan;
  - a general policy or program statement that sets out his or her responsibilities and authority.
- Senior management must fully support the program and the person chosen.
- The person responsible provides coordination and overall direction within the organization.

**Committee to support the coordinator**

- A committee of representatives from critical departments or divisions helps the coordinator organize the plan.
- At a minimum it must include:
  - legal;
  - personnel;
  - medical;
  - transportation;
  - public relations;
  - facility engineering;
  - information technology;
  - security.
- **A new organization is not created** for emergencies. They are handled by the existing organization, temporarily reconfigured, together with the executives who run the day-to-day operation (ASIS BCM 2021; ASIS 2017; Broder 2012).

**Alternate Designations**

- It is one of the most important considerations when designing the structure.
- Alternates are named for the principal decision maker and for every person with a responsibility in the plan, by name or by position.
- If possible, more than one alternate for each primary.
- Equally important: informing, training, and testing primaries and alternates in their roles.
- They are among the most significant aspects of planning and among the hardest to fulfill, because:
  - training and testing consume a lot of time and resources;
  - management often does not give them priority.
- Even so, training and testing must be done before the incident (ASIS BCM 2021; Broder 2012).

#### 2.1.2 Incident Command

- **Incident Command System (ICS):** command and control mechanism used by many public safety agencies around the world.
- Each country has its own way of managing and coordinating the groups that respond to public emergencies or disasters.

| Country | System or guide | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| United Kingdom | 2013 Emergency Response and Recovery guidance | Sets good practices based on lessons from responses and recoveries in the United Kingdom and in other countries |
| United States | National Incident Management System (NIMS) of the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) | Guides all levels of government, nongovernmental organizations, and the private sector to prevent, protect, mitigate, respond, and recover. Includes training and tools. |
| Canada | Incident Command System (ICS) · ICS Canada publishes the Curriculum and Training Standards | Integrates facilities, equipment, personnel, procedures, and communications into a common organizational structure, for effective and efficient management |
| Australia | Australia’s Emergency Management function | Prevents, prepares for, responds to, and recovers from disasters and emergencies · offers recovery assistance, response plans, and a catalog of resources |

- **Five common features of incident command systems:** command · operations · planning · logistics · administrative/finance.
- Most name a single incident commander, for a consistent and coordinated response.
- Many provide for special staff positions that report directly to the commander when the emergency warrants it:
  - public or government affairs;
  - safety;
  - interagency liaisons.
- If the incident requires a public safety response, the commander is usually the highest-ranking member of the responding agency.

| Situation | Type of command | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One agency and one jurisdiction | Single-incident command, if the size makes it necessary to formalize the system | Command by a single agency |
| Several agencies or jurisdictions | “Single command,” according to the book | Collaborative decision making · a single incident command post · the agencies take part with direct representation or through a liaison |

- **Exam note:** for several agencies or jurisdictions the book says “single command.” In ICS and NIMS that model is called unified command (information from outside the book). Do not confuse it with single-incident command.

**Incident command in the private sector**

- ICS is used mainly by public agencies, but the concept also serves the private sector.
- According to MSU, the private sector must understand and practice incident command. The public sector must know the private response procedures, which vary between companies.
- **Exam note:** chapter 2 uses the acronym MSU without spelling it out or including it in its references. The references of chapters 4 and 5 clarify it: Michigan State University (2000, *Critical Incident Protocol*).
- **Role of the security professional:** build relationships with public safety services and with supply chain partners. Hold joint drills and other exercises with them. That way, in a real incident, everyone knows each other.
- Every organization must have an internal incident management system, because not every emergency requires a public safety response.
  - Example: a weather emergency may activate the emergency operations plan and the internal or interdepartmental system. It probably does not involve a public response.

**Level of response according to the nature and scope of the emergency**

| Emergency | Who responds |
| --- | --- |
| Limited | The emergency coordinator or the facility manager, without a larger operation |
| More extensive | The organization’s incident management system, supported by the crisis management team (CMT) |
| Requires a commander or manager on the scene | The incident management system is activated · the commander or manager and staff lead on the scene · the CMT provides the necessary support |
| Requires a public safety response | The CMT and the incident structure cooperate with the agencies and their commander · they provide information about the facility and its personnel |

- **Key point:** the CMT plays a strategic role, not a tactical one. For that reason, if a commander is needed on the scene, the incident management system is activated.

### 2.2 Emergency Response Liaison and Coordination

- Coordinating groups and departments with different responsibilities is a main function of the response.
- Who interacts with whom, and how, is documented in the liaison roles of the emergency operating procedures.

#### 2.2.1 Planning Liaison

When drafting the plan, a wide range of agencies and people must be taken into account. Examples from the book (10):

- emergency response personnel: law enforcement, firefighters, emergency medical services, explosive ordnance disposal, and emergency management agencies;
- members of the cyber response team;
- senior management and department heads of the organizations that use the plan;
- other employees;
- victims, survivors, and their families;
- elected officials;
- other public officials;
- the media;
- neighboring organizations, private and public, and community groups;
- protesters or activists.

What this liaison is for:

- The information from these entities is of great value in developing and implementing the plan.
- Contacts in those groups help obtain assistance and coordinate plans.

### 2.3 Crisis Management Teams

- The CMT is perhaps the most critical piece of crisis management.
- First step of an overall crisis program: name the critical personnel who will handle any event that interrupts or affects the business.

#### 2.3.1 Setting Up a Crisis Management Team

Members of the crisis team (8):

- executives;
- human resources;
- public affairs/communications;
- safety/security;
- information technology (IT);
- legal;
- finance or another shared service;
- critical operating departments.

Composition rules:

- In large organizations, the senior leadership team is usually separate from the CMT.
  - Senior leadership focuses on decisions that affect strategic objectives.
  - It then hands those decisions down to the CMT to implement.
- The necessary members vary by organization. They must reflect the groups most critical to continued operation.
- Good planning includes all the areas that may be activated if needed.
- Members are identified by position (primary and alternates). If the primary is not available, the team knows who can cover.
- **Exam note:** in 2.1.2 the CMT has a “strategic, not tactical” role. In 2.3.1, in large organizations, strategy is decided by senior leadership and the CMT implements it. In addition, the list of CMT members includes executives.

#### 2.3.2 Roles on the Crisis Team

The team list must indicate for each person:

- his or her role in the incident;
- critical contact information;
- alternate chains of command.

**Crisis team requirements**

- The crisis team must be listed in the crisis plan.
- Its members must know the plan.
- They must take part in the exercises of the overall resilience program.

**Types of Crisis Team**

- In some organizations a single team is enough for all aspects of the crisis.
- In large ones, teams may be needed at different levels:

| Type | Scope | Example or level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Operational | Only one functional segment of the operation · focuses on recovering that area | Manufacturing crisis team · delivery and logistics crisis team |
| Tactical | Closer to the level of the crisis · carries out the tactical recovery instructions | At the geographic location where the crisis occurred |
| Strategic | Overall strategic command of the response and recovery | Almost always at the management or executive level |

## Capítulo 3 — Business Continuity Management Planning (págs. 17–34)

**Central idea:** Continuity plans document how each team recovers its part of the business after a disruption. They rest on risk assessment and on the Business Impact Analysis (BIA). They must be tested with exercises and always updated: planning never ends.

### Opening: The Continuity Plan (unnumbered in the book, pp. 17–18)

- A business continuity plan documents the instructions that the business continuity management (BCM) team will follow to recover its area.

**Goals of continuity planning** (ASIS BCM 2021):

1. Save lives and reduce the likelihood of further injuries or deaths.
2. Protect assets.
3. Restore critical business processes and systems.
4. Shorten the duration of the interruption.
5. Protect against reputational damage.
6. Control media coverage (local, regional, national, or global).
7. Maintain the relationship with customers.

**What plans must include (9 elements):**

- Team members.
- Alternate locations or transportation needs.
- Checklists for preparing for a disruption.
- Checklists for responding during the disruption.
- Checklists for returning to normal operation afterward.
- Lists of needed resources.
- Communication paths and templates.
- Financial considerations.
- Plans for specific disruptions that require a special response.

### 3.1 Business Continuity Teams

- They are a critical piece of the process. They execute the plan to recover activities after a high-impact event.
- The continuity team is treated **separately** from the crisis management team. It is a **support** for it; the crisis team is the overall team above it.
- According to the ASIS BCM Guideline (ASIS BCM 2021):
  - Response teams prepare plans for different aspects of the crisis: damage assessment · site restoration · payroll · human resources · information technology · administrative support.
  - Those response plans must be consistent with the overall Business Continuity Plan (BCP) and be included in it.
  - Members are recruited by three criteria: skills · level of commitment · personal interest in the outcome (vested interest).

**Requirements for each team participant:**

| # | Requirement |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Be formally named to the team |
| 2 | Contribute to the development of the plans |
| 3 | Know his or her role in the plan |
| 4 | Have real capacity to respond when needed |
| 5 | Know when and where to report for recovery |
| 6 | Be evaluated with plan exercises periodically |

### 3.2 Developing Plans and Procedures

- Developing a plan is part of the broader risk management process.
- Plans are written in the context of the organization. They are always tested and updated and go through the planning cycle.
- According to the ASIS BCM Guideline, the organization must establish, implement, and maintain continuity plans and procedures. They are based on the **recovery objectives identified in the BIA**.
- It must document them, with the necessary arrangements, to ensure continuity of activities and management of the event.

**Plans and procedures must:**

1. Set the notification and communication protocol, internal and external.
2. Be specific about the immediate steps during the disruption.
3. Be flexible in the face of unforeseen threats and changing internal and external conditions.
4. Focus on the impact of events that could interrupt operations.
5. Be based on explicit assumptions and an analysis of interdependencies.
6. Minimize consequences by applying appropriate mitigation strategies.

- They serve so that the whole organization knows how to respond, especially those with roles in the BCM program.
- The continuity response must support the mission, consider the interests of stakeholders, and assess the impact of the disruption.
- The BCM program’s activities make it possible to use available resources to manage the impact on critical processes.
- Plans must include arrangements for three things: safety and support of people · continuity of activities · management of the disruptive event. (ASIS BCM 2021)

**Risk assessment as the basis of the plan:**

- It serves to identify, evaluate, and prioritize threats and vulnerabilities, and how they would affect the organization. This focuses continuity initiatives.
- Before starting any part of the risk cycle, you must identify: mission · goals · critical functions and assets · stakeholders who decide on risk. You cannot protect what you do not understand.
- To choose prevention and response measures, two things are estimated:

| Element | How it is estimated |
| --- | --- |
| Likelihood of the incident | By assessing the organization’s changing profile and the security measures already in place |
| Consequences | According to knowledge of the assets at risk |

- It must be done with enough time to develop and practice procedures before they are needed.
- It is reviewed when the environment changes and as security plans and site surveys are developed.
- Each risk usually requires several preventive measures. Each measure can cover several risks.
- To assess threats it is advisable to create a **threat evaluation team**. It evaluates all the threats the organization receives.

#### 3.2.1 Establishing Objectives

- It is essential to document continuity objectives consistent with: expectations about activities · relationships with third parties · stakeholder requirements.
- Objectives and targets must be measured qualitatively and/or quantitatively. They must be consistent with the continuity policy (BC policy; BC = business continuity).

| Concept | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Continuity objectives | They take precedence over considerations such as rapid restoration of operations |
| Targets | Concrete metrics for restoring operations |

- **Exam note:** the book’s statement that objectives “take precedence over” rapid restoration is confusing. It clashes with the plan goal of shortening the interruption. Hold on to the difference: objective = purpose; target = concrete metric.
- They must be appropriate to the organization and based on the risk assessment and the BIA.
- They reflect what the organization does, how well it does it, and what it wants to achieve.
- They are set to meet the goals and commitments of the continuity policy. They turn that policy into action plans within the continuity strategies.
- They must be specific and measurable to track progress and see how the organization’s preparedness improves.
- They are defined by the appropriate levels of management. They are reviewed and adjusted periodically.
- Afterward it is advisable to set **key performance indicators (KPIs)** aligned with them.
- KPIs are the basis of a continuity performance evaluation system. They report on the BCM program and its mitigation, response, and recovery strategies. (ASIS BCM 2021)

#### 3.2.2 Writing the Business Continuity Plan

**Establish the context:**

- Identify operational priorities and the internal and external influences of the environment.
- In a program modeled on Enterprise Security Risk Management (ESRM), the context is defined in the first steps of the whole security program. BCM is just one component of that program.
- With the context, the scope of BCM is defined within the set of risk mitigation plans and tactics. A fit-for-purpose framework is designed.
- It must meet the objectives, needs, and concerns of internal and external stakeholders.
- When starting the program, analyze existing operations to set the context and the limits of the scope. (ASIS BCM 2021)

**Telecommunications at emergency operating sites:** radios (including citizens’ band) · cell phones · satellite phone · availability of internet/network.

**Vital records:**

- Definition: records needed to ensure the survival of the business.
- The safekeeping of their backup copies must be integrated into business continuity and Continuity of Operations Planning (COOP).
- Purpose: to avoid so-called “organizational amnesia.” The records essential to resuming operations can be kept at the emergency headquarters.
- Each organization adapts the list to its needs.
- The medium of the record matters less than having enough equipment to recover it, including for electronic records.
- With distributed data processing, much of the vital data may already be in several locations.

**Communicating the Plan:**

- Developing and implementing an emergency plan takes a lot of time.
- Software greatly reduces the work of capturing the information. Emergency management consultants help make the plan complete.
- Neither software nor consultants replace management’s responsible participation in preparing the plan.
- Management must directly identify and evaluate the organization’s assets. That is how the key assets to protect emerge.
- Day-to-day managers make risk analysis more manageable.
- Purpose of an emergency plan, like any part of ESRM: to show the problems that decision makers will face. It thus forces them to think in advance about how to react. (ASIS 2005, 2009; NFPA 2010)
- The planning process is critical and often misunderstood. Many plans are filed away and forgotten.
- For a plan to be effective:
  - it must reflect the requirements of your organization;
  - everyone responsible must understand their tasks and be trained to carry them out;
  - it must be tested with practice and revised based on those tests.
- An exercise or an actual activation may require revising the plan, reassigning responsibilities, or retraining personnel. Afterward it must be tested again.
- **The most important thing about planning:** it is a continuous process. It never ends as long as the plan exists. (ASIS 2005, 2009; FEMA 1993; NFPA 2010)

#### 3.2.3 Testing and Exercising

Plans, teams, and resources must be validated with exercises and reviews, and kept up to date.

**The organization must (8 points):**

1. Have a program approved by senior management. Exercises are held at planned intervals and when there are major internal or external changes.
2. Design exercises consistent with the scope of the BCM program.
3. Define the objectives and targets of each exercise.
4. Plan the exercises so that they do not themselves cause a disruption.
5. Exercise plans, teams, and facilities to verify that they meet requirements and expectations.
6. Hold several types of exercise that, together, validate all the continuity arrangements.
7. Do a post-exercise review: achievement of objectives and targets · lessons learned · opportunities for improvement.
8. Deliver a written report to senior management: exercise · results · feedback · recommended corrective and preventive actions.

**What exercises measure and provide:**

- The personnel’s ability to respond, recover, and continue their activities in specific scenarios.
- That technology works as planned and that personnel know how to use and operate it.
- They keep teams effective, clarify roles, and identify improvements in the program, plans, and procedures.
- Commitment to exercises gives credibility and authority to the BCM program.

**Changes to take into account when exercising (7):** primary or alternate facilities · reorganization of the organization · assigned personnel · relationships with partners · support systems · scope of operations · recovery objectives.

**What they must be like:** realistic · assess BCM capabilities and capacities · ensure the protection of the people and assets involved.

- Scope and detail mature with the organization’s experience, resources, and capabilities.
- The first tests can be checklists, simple exercises, and small components of the program.

**Types of exercise, from lowest to highest maturity:**

| Level | Type | What it consists of |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Orientation | Introductory, overview, or educational session |
| 2 | Tabletop | Practical or simulated exercise presented in narrative form |
| 3 | Functional | Simulates a scenario as realistic as possible in a controlled environment |
| 4 | Full scale | Live exercise that simulates a real scenario in real time · includes agencies such as police, emergency management, fire, and life safety |

**Rules of execution:**

- All participants must understand their role in the exercise.
- All members of the organization defined by the scope take part. If appropriate, external stakeholders as well.
- A review is scheduled with everyone to discuss problems and lessons learned. It is documented and the plan is updated.
- Lessons from exercises, tests, and actual incidents are incorporated into future exercises and tests. (ASIS BCM 2021)

#### 3.3.2 Identifying and Prioritizing Risks

- Identifying and prioritizing the risks to critical assets is usually called **risk assessment**.
- ASIS International and RIMS published a risk assessment standard in 2015: **ANSI/ASIS/RIMS RA.1-2015 Risk Assessments** (ANSI = American National Standards Institute; information from outside the book).
- **Exam note:** the book spells out RIMS as “Risk and Insurance Management Institute.” Its real name is Risk and Insurance Management Society (information from outside the book).

**Definitions from RA.1-2015, in process order:**

| Term | Definition (paraphrased) |
| --- | --- |
| Risk Assessment | Overall, systematic process for evaluating the effects of uncertainty on the achievement of objectives |
| Risk Identification | Process to determine which risks are anticipated · their characteristics, time dependencies, frequencies, duration, and possible outcomes |
| Risk Analysis | Process to characterize and understand the nature of the risk and define its level |
| Risk Evaluation | Process of comparing the results of the analysis with the risk criteria · decides whether the level is tolerable or is a potential opportunity |
| Risk Tolerance | Amount of uncertainty the organization accepts · in total or in a bounded way: by business unit, by risk category, or by specific initiative |
| Risk Criteria | Terms of reference for measuring and evaluating the significance and effects of the risk |
| Risk Register | Compilation of all the risks identified, analyzed, and evaluated in the process |

**The overall process (risk assessment) is divided into 4 steps:**

| Step | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Risk identification | Determines sources and nature of the risk and the effect of uncertainty on objectives · can be done with qualitative or quantitative analyses · is part of a good business and risk strategy · SWOT analysis (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats) is a key input |
| 2. Risk analysis | Understands the nature and level of the risk in order to know its importance · evaluates the information from identification in the context of the operation and the risk criteria · assesses likelihood and consequence to set the risk level and prioritize treatments |
| 3. Risk evaluation | Uses the risk criteria and the results of steps 1 and 2 · decides which risks are acceptable with current treatments and which need further treatment · the level before and after treatment, together with value driver analysis, indicates whether the residual risk stays within the acceptable level set by the criteria |
| 4. Risk assessment reports | Communicates the results to the client and the organization · leaves a complete and concise record · is delivered to the risk manager as soon as possible after the post-assessment meeting · is reviewed before being distributed |

(Source: ASIS/RIMS ANSI Risk Assessment Standard)

- Key difference: **risk analysis** sets the risk level; **risk evaluation** compares that level with the criteria and decides whether it is accepted or treated.

### 3.4 Business Impact Analysis

- The BIA is an important part of BCM planning. It helps identify and evaluate the potential impact of a disruptive event on operations.

**The BIA must address:**

1. The potential impacts over time (e.g., financial, legal, reputational) of disruptions from uncontrolled and nonspecific events.
2. The legal, regulatory, and contractual requirements of activities and resources.
3. The maximum tolerable downtime without losing viability. This makes it possible to set the recovery time objectives.
4. The requirements for resources, activities, and external interdependencies to resume within the recovery time frames.

- The organization defines the scope of the BIA. It must have a formal, documented process and methodology to do it.
- That process assesses and prioritizes critical activities, assets, and resources for delivering products and services. It includes interdependencies and variations by date, time, location, and event.

**Typical BIA activities (7):**

1. Confirm the scope of the BIA with senior management.
2. Identify sources of information.
3. Decide on data collection methods.
4. Collect data through interviews, questionnaires, or documentation.
5. Analyze the information on impact, timing, and interrelationships.
6. Present recommendations and their rationale to management for evaluation.
7. Prepare the information for developing the BCM strategy.

- The BIA results are the basis for setting the recovery time objectives. The time frame is considered after which not resuming an activity would have unacceptable impacts. (ASIS BCM 2021)

#### 3.4.1 Identifying and Prioritizing Assets

- After understanding the business and the general risk environment, the **first step of the risk life cycle** is to identify and prioritize the assets to protect.
- Knowing the critical pieces (those with the greatest impact if lost) makes it possible to direct resources first to the most critical risks on the most critical assets.
- One of the most effective ways to prioritize assets is to do a BIA.

**Types of asset and valuation methods:**

| Method | Type of asset | How it values |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cost method | Tangible: can be seen, touched, or held | By its purchase price |
| Market value method | Tangible | By its current market price: what it would cost to replace it or what would be obtained by selling it |
| Market method | Intangible: nonphysical, e.g., brand reputation | Looks for whether a similar asset was recently bought or sold in the public market, and for how much |
| Income method | Intangible | If the asset generates income or contributes greatly to generating it, takes the estimated future income as its value |

- The cost and market value methods identify high-value tangible assets. Those values may already exist in the finance area.
- Valuing intangibles requires more questions. There are generally accepted practices that help.
- **Exam note:** do not confuse the *market value method* (tangibles, current price) with the *market method* (intangibles, recent transactions of similar assets).
- Once they are identified with their asset owners and valued, a **BIA questionnaire** is applied to measure the impact. It is also applied to the critical functions.
- The questionnaire establishes the recovery priority of the processes according to criticality criteria. In this step the time and recovery point targets are set.

**Recovery metrics:**

| Metric | What it measures | Detail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| RPO: recovery point objective | Maximum amount of data that can be lost during recovery | Measured in hours · it is the tolerable loss of information |
| RTO: recovery time objective | Maximum tolerable time to restore critical systems | Usually measured in hours · must be met within the maximum tolerable downtime |
| Maximum allowable time · also maximum tolerable period of disruption | Point at which the organization’s viability is in danger if each product or service is not resumed | Source: ASIS 2021 |

- Key relationship: the RTO always falls within the maximum tolerable time.

## Capítulo 4 — Emergency Response Planning (págs. 35–52)

**Central idea:** A written plan is key to preparing for emergencies, even if it does not cover every case. Putting it together and training the team gives the organization a framework for responding systematically. The chapter covers the plan’s format, its priorities, its typical sections, and how to train on it and exercise it.

### 4.1 All-Hazards Emergency Operations Plan

- The format of the plan depends on the nature of the organization and its policy.
- If the organization is exposed to many types of threats and hazards, the all-hazards approach is the best option.
- Structure of the all-hazards approach:
  - A basic emergency operations plan (EOP). It has sections that serve for several emergencies, such as the team and contact lists.
  - Hazard-specific checklists for the most likely events. Examples: hurricanes in coastal areas · floods in flood-prone areas.
- Why it works: planning requirements are usually similar, whether the incident is a natural threat, a human threat, or an accident.
- Example: the evacuation plan is almost the same for fires, bomb incidents, utility shutdowns, and hazardous materials (HAZMAT) spills.

### 4.2 Threat-Specific Emergency Operations Plan

- Some organizations, because of their type of business, need very specific plans for each emergency situation.
- Example: a health center needs different plans for the effects of an earthquake and for an outbreak of a high-level contagion.
- Each organization decides the format that serves it best.
- Rule for any format:
  - Write the plan as simply as possible.
  - Describe the specific responsibilities of those assigned to respond.
  - This ensures an effective response to any extraordinary situation.

| Aspect | All-hazards (4.1) | Threat-specific (4.2) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| For whom | Organizations exposed to many types of threats and hazards | Organizations whose business requires very different plans depending on the event |
| Structure | Common basic EOP · checklists by hazard | Specific plans by type of threat |
| Why | Requirements are similar across natural, human, and accidental threats | The response changes greatly depending on the event |
| Example from the book | Same evacuation plan for fire, bomb, utility shutdown, and HAZMAT | Health center: earthquake vs. outbreak of a high-level contagion |
| Common rule | Plan as simple as possible · specific responsibilities | Same |

### 4.3 Considerations for Planning

The response plan must address these points:

| Consideration | What the plan must provide for |
| --- | --- |
| Shelter | Procedure to keep people inside (hold-in-place or shelter-in-place) if the threat is external · access to water, restrooms, food, and communications |
| Data search | Take the access control and visitor records to the assembly area · they serve to verify everyone recorded as being on site · it can be done by remote access to the information technology (IT) systems |
| Assembly areas | Places where personnel are sheltered from the weather · preferably with amenities |
| Special item removal | It may be necessary to remove information, equipment, laboratory samples, or other items from the site |
| Alerting of neighbors | If evacuating, alert the neighbors · done by the organization or the emergency services · a good threat evaluation capability protects against claims: that they were bothered unnecessarily by evacuating · or that they were put at risk by not evacuating |
| Shutdown procedures | Not shutting down certain processes when evacuating can be expensive and dangerous · petrochemical: evacuating the main control room can create more danger than the bomb itself · kitchens: turn off cooking fires and gas lines |
| Security | At large sites, lock the areas unrelated to the location of the device during the evacuation |
| Emergency rendezvous point (ERV) | Chosen in advance · the responding services are briefed there · accurate site plans are brought, showing the location of secondary hazards |

- Who meets at the ERV:
  - The witness.
  - A senior manager.
  - The threat evaluation team leader.
  - The chief warden.
  - Preferably, the site engineer.
- Giving quick and accurate information at the ERV can help the emergency services save the facility.

| Point | What to provide for |
| --- | --- |
| Decision | The emergency services can advise · the final decision usually rests with the organization’s management |
| Search | It may be advisable to inspect the site or certain areas before returning · objective: a safe physical environment |
| Clients and customers | They may have to stay in the assembly areas · depending on the time, it may be advisable for personnel to leave and return later or the next day · for that a means of notification is needed: communication system, message center number, website, or other · ensure safe transfer to their vehicles · in remote areas, perhaps buses to public transportation |

#### 4.3.1 Priorities

- When building the EOP, the relative importance of each type of activity must be set.
- Without priorities set in advance, resources are wasted.
- Each organization sets its own priorities.
- In the ESRM (Enterprise Security Risk Management) process, that task belongs to the business risk owners.
- It covers asset protection priorities and risk response planning.
- A possible list, in order, with the principles the book associates with each priority:

| # | Priority | How it is applied |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Protect human life | Proven principles: evacuation and shelter (take to a safe place everyone not needed in the disaster operations) · personal protection (protect as much as possible those who must remain in the threatened area) · rescue and relief (have ready a means of support and assistance for the injured) |
| 2 | Prevent or minimize personal injuries | Design safety: eliminate in advance conditions that increase the likelihood of injuries, depending on the emergency and the type of organization or facility · training: prepare those who will handle the disaster so that they do not expose themselves further through ignorance or ineptitude |
| 3 | Reduce the exposure of assets | Do not concentrate valuable materials or equipment unnecessarily in high-risk places · with enough warning, relocate them if a relocation site and transportation are already planned |
| 4 | Optimize loss control for assets whose exposure cannot be reduced | Emergency response systems (people, equipment, and procedures) that deploy quickly to threatened places and have operational support |

### 4.4 Topic-Specific Emergency Operations Plan

- With the priorities and assumptions defined, the remaining sections of the plan are completed.
- The content of those sections depends on each organization and on the priorities of the plan.
- The book does not cover all possible sections; it presents the most common ones in the following subsections.

#### 4.4.1 Emergency Evacuation and Shelter-in-Place

- In emergencies with partial or total evacuation (fire, HAZMAT spill, bomb incident), it is not enough to sound the alarm and send people to the nearest exit.
- Time frames used by the chapter:
  - Short-term: less than 1 hour.
  - Extended: more than 1 hour.
  - Around 1 hour, or sooner, people at the assembly point become restless and look for a solution. This happens above all if the point is outdoors.
- Questions the plan must answer (list from David H. Gilmore, CPP, Certified Protection Professional):
  1. Where are the main exits?
  2. Where are the alternate exits?
  3. Will employees use the same exits in emergencies other than fires?
  4. How will the employer tell occupants to evacuate in an emergency other than a fire?
  5. How will it evacuate people with disabilities who need assistance?
  6. Where will it evacuate to in a short-term emergency (less than 1 hour)?
  7. What are the alternate short-term evacuation areas?
  8. What will it do if the evacuation is extended (more than 1 hour)?
  9. What are the alternate extended evacuation areas?
  10. What will it do in bad weather, in a short or extended evacuation?
  11. How will it count employees, contractors, vendors, and visitors after the evacuation?
  12. Who will keep evacuees informed of the status of the incident?
  13. How will it move occupants to a temporary area that is not easily reached on foot?
  14. What will it do if personal or company vehicles are left inside the security perimeter and cannot be used?

**Practicing the alternatives**

- The plan must provide for alternate exits, routes, and assembly points, and practice them regularly.
- Technique: in each fire drill, block the main exit on a different floor or zone.
  - With an “exit blocked due to smoke” sign.
  - Or with a monitor at the exit who announces that it is unavailable.
  - This forces people to use the alternate exits.
- Only with practice do people know what to do if the main exit is blocked.
- In multistory buildings where everyone uses elevators, going down the fire stairs will be new to some. Another reason to hold regular drills.
- Walk the real distance: if in a fire you have to cross the street, go to another block, or go to the far end of the parking lot, in the drill you do the same.
- The drill continues until everyone reaches the designated assembly points.
- Bomb or HAZMAT plans: in all drills the real evacuation distances are respected, which are usually considerably greater than in a fire.

**Shelter-in-place**

- It is used when evacuating is not possible or not advisable. Examples: HAZMAT release · tornado · active assailant · earthquake.
- When choosing shelter areas, consider: space · sanitary facilities · food · water · accommodation.
- Consult local authorities on whether community shelters already exist.
- The plan must take into account personnel with visual, hearing, mental function, and mobility impairments.

| Aspect | Evacuation | Shelter-in-place |
| --- | --- | --- |
| When | Fire · HAZMAT spill · bomb incident | Evacuating is not possible or not advisable: HAZMAT release · tornado · active assailant · earthquake |
| What to plan | Main and alternate exits, routes, and assembly points · short or extended time frame · headcount · transportation · bad weather | Space · restrooms · food · water · accommodation · existing community shelters |

Sources cited: ASIS 2003, 2004, 2009, 2021.

### 4.5 Emergency Communications

- According to ASIS International’s Emergency Planning Handbook, communication is one of the most important ingredients for managing an emergency well.

| Concept | What it focuses on |
| --- | --- |
| Emergency communications | The technical ability to communicate and to spread information about the emergency |
| Crisis communications | The messages and the public response to the incident |

**Exam note:** the book says that crisis communications is covered “later in this chapter.” However, chapter 4 does not develop it.

#### 4.5.1 Notification

- The plan must provide a method for warning occupants that there is an emergency.
- It must alert as quickly as possible so that they act; for example, by evacuating an area.
- Many local building, fire, and safety codes require alert and communication systems in high rises and other structures.
- When designing the alert, take into account ambient noise and distances.
- Both indoor and outdoor alerts are needed, because there may be people outside:
  - Indoor: existing communication systems can be used.
  - Outdoor: bells, whistles, sirens, or public address systems.
  - Visual signal, such as a flashing light: indoors, and outdoors where noise prevents hearing the alert.
- In the U.S., if the organization is subject to the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA), the alert may have requirements for location, visibility, and audibility.
- All occupants must know the signals and what to do on receiving them.
- Test the system periodically with employees present, so that they become familiar with it.
- Electronic mass notification systems speed up the warning and the location of employees through their devices.

Sources cited: ASIS 2003, 2004, 2005, 2009, 2021.

#### 4.5.2 Emergency Contacts and Communications

- **Interoperability:** the ability of different agencies and jurisdictions to communicate with each other by wireless means. It is an important problem in the public safety sector.
- Always keep at hand the details and phone numbers of the police, fire department, and other emergency services, so as not to delay the call.
- In an emergency, the emergency services’ lines can become saturated with calls from others. For that reason it is advisable to obtain private-line numbers.
- If possible, program those numbers into the phones of all members of the crisis management team (CMT), with speed dial.
- Many people call at once from and to the affected place. It is advisable to give the response team individual cell phones or additional lines.
- Some organizations may consider satellite phones.
- If the network becomes saturated and calls fail:
  - Use text messages: they may work when calls do not. They also free up bandwidth on the cellular network.
  - Wait 10 seconds before redialing. It avoids saturating the network and lets the data between the phone and the tower clear.
  - Keep batteries charged or have car chargers.

Sources cited: ASIS 2003, 2004, 2005, 2009, 2021.

### 4.6 Emergency Shutdown and Restoration

**Shutdown**

- The plan must assign specific people responsible for shutting down equipment. They must be people who know the process.
- Shutting down some equipment can take several hours. A flood, fire, explosion, or earthquake may not allow that margin.
- Shutdown crews may be the last to leave, or may even have to stay.
- For that reason maximum protection is planned for them, including a shelter with a good guarantee of survival.
- Crews must be as small as possible and trained in rapid shutdown.
- A careful shutdown greatly speeds up recovery.

**Restoration**

- The plan must also include restoration procedures. If planned, recovery is easier and more efficient.
- A list of key recovery items simplifies emergency operations.
- Do not spend effort on areas or equipment that cannot be saved. Better to concentrate and save something than to scatter and lose everything.
- It is a painful decision, but it has to be made.
- A pre-emergency review with managers and supervisors of the assets in each area helps set a salvage priority scheme.
- Two costs of key assets must be evaluated:
  - The purchase or replacement cost.
  - The cost impact of not having them when operations are restored.
- After the emergency, the priority is the structure of the facility:
  - The engineering crew inspects the building and the grounds, especially the structure.
  - At least one member must have the technical ability to recognize structural weaknesses.

### 4.7 Emergency Personnel

- The plan must take into account and include both internal (onsite) responders and local ones.

#### 4.7.1 Emergency Operations Centers and Command Posts

- The organization has two options:
  - Define a place as an emergency operations center (EOC) or crisis management center (CMC), from which to direct the response.
  - Or establish a virtual command post.
- The size, nature, and location of the organization determine where the EOC/CMC goes and its scope.

| Organization | Where the emergency is managed |
| --- | --- |
| Small | Building manager’s office · plant manager’s office · someone else’s usual workplace |
| Larger | Boardroom · or dedicated EOC/CMC, used only in emergencies |
| Many large organizations | The EOC and CMC may be far from each other: CMC at corporate headquarters · EOC at a remote facility |
| Any size | Can set up a virtual command center |

- **Note:** the book uses “virtual command post” and “virtual command center” as synonyms.
- Control access to the EOC/CMC, physically or remotely. Those directing the response need total concentration.
- Designate one or more alternate locations. At least one must be in a different building from the main EOC/CMC, in case it is damaged, destroyed, or becomes inaccessible.
- Requirements for the primary and alternate locations:
  - Space for the CMT.
  - Redundant communications.
  - Backup power.
  - Independent supply of drinking water.
  - Sanitary facilities that do not depend on the normal water system.
  - In long emergencies, or if the CMT cannot leave: lodging and food for the CMT and support staff.
- Document in the plan the location of the EOC, its setup, how to get there, the backup site, and other details. Also, disseminate it to the CMT members.

#### 4.7.2 Command Management/Succession

- An emergency can occur at any time. The plan must ensure that a senior management representative is always available to take command.
- Outside business hours there may be no telephone or electricity, and no way to arrive by road.
- For that reason, the start of the plan and control of the facility may fall to the highest-ranking executive present on site.
- **Security role:** that person may be a security manager, a security supervisor, or a security officer.
- The plan must define responsibilities and how the person on site assumes command until a senior executive arrives.

**Emergency telephone list of key personnel**

- It is essential. It goes in the published plan and is updated regularly.
- The plan with that list is distributed only to the control centers and to executives with responsibilities in the plan.
- This protects the privacy of those who have unlisted numbers or want limited distribution.

**Succession lists, distinct from emergency contact lists**

- In some organizations they are kept sealed in a safe place and opened only in a major emergency.
- In others they are in the written plan, and those involved always know who is in command.
- Some board resolutions make the chief executive officer (CEO) responsible for preparing and reviewing the succession lists.
- Emergency succession need not coincide with the normal planned succession.

**Board of directors**

- Some boards have a management succession list for the corporate level and another for subsidiaries. They can be adopted as resolutions.
- If board alternates are named who were not formally elected by the shareholders, a lawyer must review the plan. This ensures that what is done in the emergency is legal.
- Quorum: some companies ensure that the board can function even if a disaster incapacitates so many members that there is no quorum.
  - Their bylaws provide for how to return the board to its full number.
  - One method: the remaining members fill the vacancies.
- Some states have passed special laws to facilitate emergency planning by companies. If they exist, it is advisable to use them.

**By type of organization**

- Government: each member must know his or her chain of command and supervision. Also the name or position of who would succeed his or her boss.
- Nongovernmental organizations: management succession must be defined and within reach of key personnel in an emergency.

Sources cited: ASIS 2003, 2004, 2005, 2009, 2021.

#### 4.7.3 Emergency Response Agencies

For each agency that may come to the facility, answer:

1. Where is it?
2. What support can it give?
3. Under what conditions does it respond?
4. How fast can it respond?
5. What does it need from the organization?
6. How is it contacted?
7. Is there a written agreement between the organization and the agency?
8. Have its representatives visited the facility?
9. Have they taken part in drills at this location?
10. Have they responded to previous emergencies at this location?

- The last question touches on one of the keys to coordination: agencies must know the layout and particularities of the facilities to which they may respond.
- Sources of that idea: FEMA (Federal Emergency Management Agency, the U.S. federal emergency management agency) 1993; MSU (Michigan State University) 2000.
- Some public agencies offer free site assessments and security reviews.
- The local police usually agree to review a site. At a minimum, they can keep a contact list and the building plans, if given.
- If local responders offer joint training at the site, it is advisable to do it when feasible.

#### 4.7.4 Emergency Medical Services

**Internal capability**

- Immediate and short-term care of the injured may fall to the organization’s own medical personnel, if any.
- Some industrial facilities and many military ones have internal medical support. Most public and private ones do not, except perhaps an occupational health nurse.
- Even with internal support, it may not be available 24 hours or have transport capability.
- First step: know the type and capabilities of the internal medical support. Ask that personnel to define their capabilities and under what conditions they can give support in emergencies.
- With internal support: sufficient medical supplies. Provide for how to obtain more from local suppliers or other facilities in the area.

**First aid**

- Option A: selected personnel trained in basic first aid, including cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), until medical personnel arrive.
- Option B: rely only on local emergency medical services (EMS) personnel.
- If employees are trained, define: scope of training · whom to train · what medical equipment to have.
- Consult a lawyer on legal liability, licenses, and certifications.
- **Security role:** security professionals should buy life-saving equipment, such as automated external defibrillators (AED), and train personnel in its use.

**Hospitals and transport**

- Contact local hospitals: how many emergency patients each can receive and what treatments it offers.
- Plan the transport of victims to hospitals or care centers.
- Know how many ambulances there are in the area and make agreements with ambulance companies.
- Backup plan: use the organization’s vehicles if ambulances are insufficient or cannot arrive.

**Mass casualty**

- Designate space for triage: trained medical personnel evaluate the injured and prioritize them for treatment.
- It may be necessary to set up temporary hospital facilities within the organization. They are for those the hospitals cannot receive or those who cannot be moved because of their injuries.
- The plan must reserve enough space for those facilities.

**Access for outside medical responders**

- In facilities with several access points, define the best entry and exit points and the most direct internal routes.
- Example: in a school, hotel, or industrial plant with several wings, guiding responders to the nearest access saves time.
- Provide for the movement of gurneys, especially in multistory buildings. For that, consider the location of public, staff, and freight elevators.

**After the incident**

- Consider medical care after the incident.
- Depending on the incident, provide for trauma counseling and other psychological help, during and afterward.
- That help is for victims, their families, and also the emergency responders.

Sources cited: ASIS 2003, 2004; FEMA 1993, 2010a; OSHA 2001, 2004.

#### 4.7.5 Security and Fire Protection

- Do not assume that the local police and fire department will help in every emergency. They may not arrive because of impassable roads or lack of personnel to attend to everyone.
- Depending on the nature and location of the organization, the plan may provide for extra employees to support those assigned to security and fire protection.
- Total self-sufficiency may be needed, at least for a limited time.

| Type of facility | Fire and rescue |
| --- | --- |
| Industrial | May have an internal volunteer fire and rescue brigade |
| Nonindustrial (above all offices) | Operates differently: occupants identify the problem · notify the authorities · evacuate the affected area |

**Partnership with local first responders**

- They must inspect the facility regularly to detect hazardous conditions that would increase losses, and recommend corrections.
- They must visit it to learn the layout and where hazardous materials are.
- Planners give them the phone numbers of key management personnel and floor plans updated periodically, for their training.
- Regularly review neighboring facilities, in case they have hazards or conditions that aggravate a crisis.

**Local police**

- Invite them to tour the facility and give them a plan with entrances and exits.
- Inform them where safes, vaults, cash, and especially valuable material or equipment are.
- Give them the contacts for key management personnel.

**Security during the disaster (security role)**

- Fences, walls, and other normal physical measures can be destroyed or damaged.
- There is a risk of looting by employees and by outsiders. Every disaster attracts onlookers.
- More security personnel may be needed to protect assets and personnel.
- Do not use all security personnel on disaster or fire control. Enough must remain to keep protecting assets and people.
- Controlling personnel is difficult, however many guards there are.
- Badges or passes to identify employees and authorized persons help.
- Simple arm bands can be quickly distributed to auxiliary emergency personnel.

Sources cited: ASIS 2003, 2004; FEMA 1993; OSHA 2004.

### 4.8 Training and Exercising

- Training and testing are key so that plans are understood and can be used.

| Key audience | What training it needs |
| --- | --- |
| Crisis teams and responders | The highest level, on their roles and responsibilities in the plan · can be in person or online, on the full plan |
| General personnel and non-team members | Know that the plan exists · training in what concerns them, such as training and evacuation drills |
| Visitors | Awareness through signage · in complex organizations, sign-in with a safety brief · in areas with hazardous or regulated activities, additional requirements may apply |

**Periodic drills and exercises.** They reinforce and test training and induction. They can include:

- Tabletop exercises at the strategic, operational, and tactical levels. They serve to train members in several roles (cross-training) with real scenarios.
- Continuous public relations outreach strategy: reaching stakeholders, their participation and buy-in, and community relations.
- Evacuation and shelter-in-place drills for all employees.
- Walkthrough of the plan, or of specific response functions, by selected personnel.
- Online training: knowledge-based, scenario-based, and with role play.
- Full exercise with outside response agencies.

**What tests and exercises are for**

1. Verify that the plan, or a part of it, works.
2. Measure the level of awareness and training of personnel.
3. Assess whether the emergency communications are adequate.
4. Detect flaws in the evacuation procedures.

**Design rules**

- The whole plan does not need to be tested at once. But each exercise must evaluate one or more aspects of the plan and its application.
- To validate the plan, an unannounced test can be done, with the appropriate controls and safeguards.
- Exercises are rehearsals of the real event. Except for physical safety reasons, they must follow the real plan as closely as possible.
- The book’s analogy: a theater rehearsal uses the same script as the performance; a security exercise must do the same.
- One of the worst outcomes is for personnel to think “it’s just a test” or “in the drill we only pretend to…”.
- Golden rule: train to the plan and exercise the plan.

**Recommendations for conducting exercises**

- Realistic, but without putting anyone at mental or physical risk.
- Carefully control the use of force by armed guards and by any actor in the exercise (role player).
- Notify outside response agencies. They usually participate; if they do not participate, inform them of the type of exercise and its duration.
  - It avoids confusion if a third party reports it believing it is real.
  - It avoids unintended responses that put responders and bystanders at risk.
- Alternative: participants record the outside contacts they would make, or describe them to an exercise controller.

Sources cited: ASIS 2003, 2004, 2005, 2009; Broder 2012; FEMA 1993; MSU 2002; NFPA (National Fire Protection Association) 2010; NOD (National Organization on Disability) 2009a; OSHA 2001.

**Box: recommendations from David H. Gilmore, CPP (printed on the references page, p. 50)**

2. Train the trainers first and make clear to them that exercises are not competitions.

## Capítulo 5 — Crisis Management Planning (págs. 53–62)

**Central idea:** Crisis management starts as soon as any disruptive event is reported, whether physical or not. The plan is tailored to each organization, anticipates a response for each type of crisis, and integrates crisis communications, which is strategic. Planning never ends: the plan is tested, revised, and tested again.

**Starting points (chapter introduction):**

- Crisis management begins when any disruptive event is reported: a physical emergency or a nonphysical event.
- The security manager designs and implements measures to protect people, information, and capabilities.
- The word “emergency” immediately brings evacuation to mind. But each type of crisis has its own ideal response.
- The general response flow is similar across all crises. Even so, the specific situation must be addressed, and there must be a plan for each type of crisis.

### 5.1 Parts of a Crisis Plan

The plan is tailored to the needs and mission of the business. Its typical parts are five:

| # | Part (English) | In plain words |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Crisis management team | Crisis management team (CMT) |
| 2 | Crisis management activation and escalation | Activation and escalation of crisis management |
| 3 | Crisis command and management succession | Crisis command and succession in management |
| 4 | Crisis recovery logistics and resources | Logistics and resources for recovery |
| 5 | Crisis communications | Crisis communications |

**Exam note:** the list presents “activation and escalation” as a part of its own. In the text, however, it is an unnumbered heading within 5.1.1. Crisis communications is developed separately, in 5.2.

#### 5.1.1 Crisis Management Team

- The composition of the crisis management team must be documented in the plan.
- The book refers to a description of the team given earlier, outside this chapter.

**Activation and Escalation**

- The plan must set the circumstances in which it is activated: the crisis activation triggers.
- Examples from the book: imminent hurricane · fire or explosion · cyberattack · social media crisis.
- Triggers vary widely between organizations, but documenting them is critical.
- The activation method is also key. The plan must define seven things:
  1. How team members are notified that there is an activation.
  2. Who can convene the crisis team.
  3. Where the team meets (online or in person).
  4. How meetings are run.
  5. How the crisis is declared for the whole organization.
  6. If there are several crisis teams (by geography or by line of business): when the crisis is escalated to the parent organization.
  7. How executives are notified that the team has been activated, whether or not a crisis is declared.

#### 5.1.2 Crisis Command/Management Succession

- As in the emergency plan, succession for the crisis management team must be defined.
- For details, the book refers to the succession section of the Emergency Response Planning chapter.

#### 5.1.3 Crisis Recovery Logistics and Resources

Equipment and logistical support can arrive through several channels, depending on the incident and the type of organization:

| Source | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Reserved equipment | Kept only for emergencies or crises |
| Daily-use equipment assigned to crises | Used daily, but also designated for crisis events |
| Commercial suppliers identified in advance | Purchased or rented when needed |
| Other organizations | Through mutual aid or interagency support arrangements |

- In a large-scale event, local resources can become saturated. Security must look for nearby sources and also distant ones.

**Vehicles**

- Control of response and crisis vehicles is assigned to one person, with a designated alternate.
- All of the organization’s vehicles are inventoried and included in the emergency plan.
- Extra vehicles can be arranged in advance with leasing companies or garages.
- The plan can include employees’ own vehicles, with an agreement to pay them for their use.
- Uses in an emergency: hauling supplies and debris · transporting personnel · conducting rescue operations.
- Source: ASIS 2003, 2004, 2005, 2009, 2021.

**Key questions for planning logistical support (16)**

They are mainly useful for the equipment and services that must be obtained from outside the organization. They were prepared by David H. Gilmore, CPP (Certified Protection Professional).

1. What equipment or services are needed?
2. In what quantity?
3. Within what time frame?
4. For how long?
5. What sources are available?
6. Can the source provide support at all times?
7. How long does the source take to respond?
8. How much does it cost?
9. How is the source contacted during normal hours?
10. How is it contacted after hours?
11. Does the source deliver the equipment to the user?
12. If not, how does the equipment get to the user?
13. Who maintains or repairs the rented equipment?
14. Who has authority to purchase or lease in an emergency?
15. What documentation is required?
16. How often is the list of sources reviewed and verified?

### 5.2 Crisis Communications

**Strategic, not tactical**

- Like crisis management, crisis communications is strategic, not tactical.
- It does not just convey messages about the disruptive event. It also sustains organizational resilience, the survival of the business model, and future strategic plans.
- Example: in the middle of a crisis, the organization must decide on and communicate its future plans to key external audiences. The book cites changes in stock value · initial public offerings (IPOs) · acquisitions of other companies · expansion into new markets.
- **Communications tactics:** tools that deliver messages to specific audiences. Examples: news releases · social media · the company website · town halls.
- Tactics must not be confused with strategic communications.

**Working definition of crisis communications: four features**

| # | Feature | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Effective communication with public relations standards | No “spin” · Spin: going on a news program to downplay the crisis, deflect blame, and divert attention from the substance · It almost never works, violates professional standards, and can cause loss of trust in the organization |
| 2 | Messages carefully written for specific audiences | In a crisis there is no “general public” · The crisis affects specific audiences: employees · shareholders · suppliers · customers · Each has its own concerns and needs messages tailored to it |
| 3 | Focuses on critical issues, disruptive events, and crises | The book does not develop this further |
| 4 | Executed in a compressed time frame | See 5.2.1 |

**Place within crisis management**

- It is an essential part of the crisis management process and of the crisis lifecycle.
- It is not an isolated function. It is integrated into the crisis structure: senior leadership team · crisis management team · incident command.
- Outsiders judge it too. Even if the response to a major event is excellent, the media focus on how well it was communicated to key audiences.

#### 5.2.1 Challenges

- In a crisis there is never time to communicate as in a normal situation.
- Time is compressed. That magnifies the impact and consequences of the communication.
- Execution must be fast, with little time to research, deliberate, or draft messages.
- That is why the crisis communications plan is prepared beforehand. That way less time is spent deciding what to say, how to say it, and who says it.
- Every organization has detractors. They are organized and already have their messages and tactics ready.
- Often the organization receives criticism before issuing its first message. It ends up on the defensive and loses credibility.

#### 5.2.2 Programs and Teams

- An effective crisis communications program has the same five elements as crisis management: planning · training · exercising · response · recovery.
- The size of the team depends on the organization: its size, management levels, regions, or facilities.

Typical roles in medium and large organizations:

| Role | Functions |
| --- | --- |
| Senior Communications Advisor/Lead | Usually the highest-ranking communicator and ultimately responsible for crisis communications · In the crisis, sits with the senior leadership team while it discusses strategy · Brings those deliberations to the communications team, so that messages fit the response strategy · Gives strategic communications advice to senior leadership |
| Crisis Communications Team Lead | Leads the team · Executes the crisis communications plan · Ensures deadlines are met |
| Crisis Communications Team, CCT | Represents the organization’s various communications functions · Develops messages, deploys tactics, and handles media and community relations · One CCT member acts as liaison with the CMT to track the situation |
| Adjunct members | Internal subject matter experts · Help the CCT draft accurate messages |
| Incident Command Staff | In a major event at a facility under intense public scrutiny, a CCT member is assigned to the incident commander · Sometimes there is a full-time communicator at the facility · If not, a CCT member travels there |

- The CCT grows or shrinks according to the severity and duration of the event.
- With few in-house communicators, it is common to hire an outside public relations agency with crisis experience.
- CCT members must take part in the organization’s risk assessment.
- That assessment gives them a great deal of information for identifying key external audiences and issues. With it they build important parts of the plan.

#### 5.2.3 The Plan

- The crisis communications plan (CCP) can be a standalone document or be integrated into the crisis management plan.
- In both cases, its notification and activation procedures must be identical to those of the crisis management plan.
- The CCP lists the CCT members and their roles.
- Its most important part is the communications section.
- Using the risk assessment and other sources, the CCT documents five steps for each disruptive scenario:

| Step | What is done |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Identify the affected or key audiences |
| 2 | Identify each audience’s concerns |
| 3 | Develop specific messages for those concerns |
| 4 | Designate a spokesperson/messenger for each audience |
| 5 | Choose the tactics for delivering each message |

#### 5.2.4 Communications Training

- CCT members are trained in how to deploy the crisis communications plan.
- CCT members and designated spokespersons receive **annual** communications training.
- Possible content: on-camera interview · telephone and radio interview.
- In all cases, the spokesperson learns to deliver effective messages.
- The CCT is part of the overall exercise program at two levels: crisis management and incident command.

#### 5.2.5 Recovery

- At some point in the crisis lifecycle, the organization moves from response to recovery.
- Recovery communications supports the return to normal or, often, to the “new normal.”
- The CCT begins planning recovery communications while the organization is still in response mode.

**Family and Victim Support**

- Caring for the well-being and morale of employees and families can be a serious challenge in an emergency.
- Help and guidance can come from: American Red Cross · FEMA (Federal Emergency Management Agency) · similar government and public health agencies.
- Not every emergency requires family or victim support. Example from the book where it does: collapse of a building with victims trapped.
- The organization must be prepared for frightened, distraught, or angry victims and relatives.
- There must be a designated point of contact for families and victims. That person informs them of what is being done to resolve the incident.
- Logistics to plan for families and victims:
  1. A family and victim support center, far from any media facility.
  2. Food, drink, and temporary lodging.
  3. Counseling or pastoral support.
  4. Transportation to the incident site, if applicable.
- Lesson from major air disasters: the treatment of families and victims attracts media scrutiny.
- Any mistake in that treatment can badly damage the organization’s public image.
- Source: ASIS 2005, 2009.

**Public Affairs and Media Relations**

- The media ask for information immediately. Mishandling it can bring bad press or permanent damage to the brand.
- The emergency plan must provide for orderly disclosure, preferably from a single source within the organization.
- The existing public relations or community relations division can take charge.
- The emergency coordinator keeps the public relations director and alternates up to date.
- As soon as the media make contact, they are told what happened through prepared press releases and oral briefings.
- “No comment” should be avoided.
- If the media believe not enough information is being given, they seek out people far from the event who have little or no information. That is how rumors, conjecture, and speculation spread.
- If the public believes the organization is hiding something, the negative image can last.
- The press usually cooperates if it understands why information is limited.
  - Example: access to the disaster area is restricted for safety.
  - The reason is explained and people who were in the area are offered for interviews.
- Names of dead and injured: they are released as soon as possible, but families are informed first. The press understands this.
- Information about victims matters for internal human relations and for public relations.
- The method for handling it is defined in writing in the emergency plan.
- Requests for interviews with experts on specialized topics must be anticipated.
- In an ongoing incident, such as a barricade or hostage situation, it must be assumed that the perpetrators follow the local and national media.
- Media logistics in a prolonged incident:
  1. Place for media briefings and filings.
  2. Guided tours of the incident scene.
  3. Media credentials.
  4. Media access control points.
  5. Parking for microwave remote vehicles.
- Photographing and recording the scene serves insurance purposes and supports legal claims.
- This is done by the organization’s photographers, or copies are requested from press photographers.
- Sources: ASIS 2003, 2004, 2005, 2009; FEMA 1993; MSU (Michigan State University) 2000; NFPA (National Fire Protection Association) 2010.

### 5.3 Communicating, Testing and Exercising Plans

#### 5.3.1 Communicating the Plan

**Developing the plan**

- Creating and implementing a plan for possible emergencies takes a great deal of time.
- Software greatly reduces the work of capturing the necessary information.
- Emergency management consultants help make the plan complete.
- Neither software nor consultants replace the responsible participation of the management team in preparing the plan.
- Management must directly identify and assess the organization’s assets. That is how the key assets to be protected are detected.
- Managers of day-to-day operations make the risk analysis more manageable.
- Purpose of the plan, like that of any part of an ESRM (Enterprise Security Risk Management) program: to show the problems that decision makers and key emergency personnel will encounter.
- And to force them to think ahead about how to react when the emergency arises. Source: ASIS 2005, 2009; NFPA 2010.

**What makes a plan effective**

- The planning process is critical and often misunderstood.
- Often, if plans are made at all, they are filed away and forgotten.
- To be effective, the plan meets four conditions:
  1. It reflects the requirements of the organization it belongs to.
  2. Each person with responsibilities understands them clearly and is trained to carry them out.
  3. It is tested through practice.
  4. It is revised in light of those tests.
- An exercise or a real activation can show that the plan needs revision, responsibilities need reassignment, or personnel need retraining.
- After those changes, the plan is tested again.
- Most important: planning is a continuing process that never ends as long as the plan exists. Source: ASIS 2005, 2009; FEMA 1993; NFPA 2010.

## Capítulo 6 — Event-Specific Planning (págs. 63–118)

**Central idea:** The book groups crises into four threat families. The general continuity and response plans serve for all of them, but each threat calls for its own actions. For each event, the chapter gives what to do before, during, and after.

- **Threat families:** natural or environmental · human · active · cyber-based.
- Depending on the crisis, plans for crisis management, emergency response, and/or business continuity management are activated.

### 6.1 General Response to a Crisis

- **Order of the response:**
  1. Warnings, notifications, and communications are issued to the affected employees. They carry initial action instructions: evacuation, shelter-in-place, or lockdown.
  2. Then, the threat management team coordinates and directs the entire response.
- That team oversees internal and external responders. Law enforcement stands out in an active assailant event.
- It also establishes a perimeter to contain the incident and prevent entry to the danger zone.
- **What must already exist before the event:** policies and procedures for that type of incident · responders trained in their roles and responsibilities · specialized training, especially for security personnel · drills, exercises, and tests, from tabletop exercises to full-scale exercises.
- The threat management team acts throughout the event and continues into the post-incident recovery phase.
- More detail in Appendix A: “Standards in Security.”

**Exam note:** the section is titled “general response,” but its text and its two lists are written for an active assailant event.

#### Pre-incident considerations

1. Define who makes up the threat management team.
2. Set roles, responsibilities, procedures, and training for key response personnel.
3. Create emergency alert, warning, and notification systems with multiple communication methods and modes.
4. Give employees awareness, education, and training programs. Emphasis on: warning signs of workplace violence · how to report · the organization’s guidance on the “run, hide, fight” protocol.
5. Equip key personnel with response equipment and materials: portable radios · flashlights · keys and access cards · building and floor plans · bleeding control supplies.
6. Coordinate with public safety agencies: police, fire, and ambulance.
7. Choose an emergency operations center (EOC) and an incident command center (ICC). They serve to direct, coordinate, and communicate with internal and external responders.
8. Set a media staging area and a family notification/reunification area. Both secure and away from ongoing operations.
9. Take into account serious psychological factors, such as trauma. This applies to pre-planning, operations, post-incident recovery, and business continuity.

#### Post-incident considerations

| Phase | Actions |
| --- | --- |
| Immediate | Protect life safety · account for everyone who was on site · communicate with employees, families, the public, and the media |
| Short-term | Preserve the crime scene · watch for signs of psychological trauma · assist with reunification and other incident tasks · keep informing |
| Long-term | Conduct the post-incident analysis (debrief) with internal and external responders and stakeholders · replenish supplies · maintain communication with the relevant groups |

### 6.2 Natural and Environmental Threats

#### 6.2.1 Inclement Weather

- **Includes:** floods · tornadoes · hurricanes · snow · ice storms.
- It can damage property, people, and other assets in an instant.
- **Effects:** storms cause power surges and damage to buildings · floods put employees at risk on their commutes to work · in severe cases, they threaten property, critical infrastructure, and lives.
- The security response depends mainly on geographic location. It combines advance preparation and immediate response.
- Weather is hard to predict. That is why knowing local patterns and vulnerabilities, such as flood plains, is key.
- A weather response plan must be kept up to date. It must give clear instructions to employees and to internal responders: security, HR (human resources), corporate communications, and IT (information technology).
- Inclement weather cuts the supply chain of resources that continuity needs: utilities · employees · security · public safety.
- Security must be ready to put additional temporary security and alternative solutions in place to protect facilities.
- Some security areas are tasked with the first damage assessments after the event.

#### 6.2.2 Planning Considerations

Security must get the main stakeholders in the exposed assets to participate:

| Area | What it contributes |
| --- | --- |
| HR | Decisions about personnel |
| Marketing | Decisions that can affect the brand and reputation |
| Operations | The functional impact of a closure due to inclement weather |
| Facilities | Ongoing information on the state of the building and whether the space is functional |

#### 6.2.3 Preparing for General Inclement Weather

| Topic | Actions (24 in total) |
| --- | --- |
| People and communication | Emergency notice to employees when severe weather approaches · all employees accounted for and all shifts in each area covered · notification lists given to managers to track personnel and issue notices · all teleworkers confirmed their remote access · batteries of communication equipment charged and everyone with a car charger · calls forwarded to unaffected facilities · consider satellite phones in areas that may lose power or communications |
| Power and equipment | Fuel tanks full in vehicles and generators, if applicable · power-down procedures tested · backup HVAC (heating, ventilation, and air conditioning) units available · all nonessential equipment turned off · contact with backup service providers: generator, UPS (uninterruptible power supply), HVAC (the book leaves the list open) · generators load tested within the last 7 days and with full fuel · all restoration equipment tested and ready for recovery · consider putting the fuel supplier on standby · consider a safe stopping point for data center jobs in progress |
| Facilities and property | Consider moving the fleet to a safe place, away from the crisis · if strong winds are expected, consider boarding up windows exposed to the wind and securing outdoor objects that could be blown away · consider sandbags at low-lying facilities, distributed to all of them · consider moving warehouse goods up to higher levels · consider additional security for building damage or blackouts · consider relocating to areas the event will not affect |
| Supply chain | Consider canceling inbound or outbound shipments · ask suppliers to be ready to ship product as soon as they are asked |

#### 6.2.4 After the Weather Event

- Contact the local electric company to learn the progress of restoration.
- Update the employee hotline.
- Consider assistance for employees affected by the storm.
- Consider shelter for those who cannot get home or have lost their home.
- Identify safe rooms and tell employees where they are.
- Consider provisions for those who cannot leave the facility: cots · food · water · clothing · personal hygiene items (open list in the book).

Sections 6.3 to 6.5 are the checklists for specific types of inclement weather.

### 6.3 Floods

#### 6.3.1 Preparing for a Flood

| Topic | Actions |
| --- | --- |
| Risks on site | Take all measures so that stored hazardous chemicals are not released · locate the main gas valves and electrical shutoffs · anchor all fuel tanks |
| Water entry | Install plugs so that water does not back up through the drains · or install flood vents or flood-proof barriers |
| Operations and property | Postpone all receipt of goods: deliveries and courier service · keep an accurate inventory of the product on site |
| Insurance and recovery | Talk to the insurance agent and review the policy · update the disaster recovery kits · start the crisis backup procedures |
| Communication | Notify the emergency response or crisis team · follow local media and community messages · review the emergency communication plan before the storm, evacuation, or threat · update contacts for employees, suppliers, and customers · use an alert notification system to inform all stakeholders of status and next steps · in an evacuation, have a central point of contact for all employees |

#### 6.3.2 During a Flood

- Send home personnel who are not needed.
- Raise the elevators to the second floor and shut them off.
- Follow local media and evacuate when ordered.
- Take cell phones, chargers, and emergency kits.
- Consider forwarding company phones to cell phones, to an answering service, or to other sites.

#### 6.3.3 Post Flood Response

- Follow the news to find out whether the community’s drinking water is safe.
- Do not touch floodwater. It can carry oil, gasoline, or sewage. It can be electrified by underground or downed power lines.
- Be careful where the water has already receded: roads may be weakened and collapse under the weight of a car.
- Clean and disinfect everything that got wet. Mud can contain sewage and chemicals.
- Activate the disaster recovery plan and follow announcements from local authorities.
- Contact employees through the agreed means and discuss next steps.
- Contact the insurance agent.

#### 6.5.1 Preparing for a Winter Storm

| Topic | Actions |

- The four communication measures are the same as in flood preparation (6.3.1).

#### 6.5.2 During a Winter Storm

- Locate heaters, snow blowers, generators, and cold-weather equipment in case they are needed.
- Keep vehicle accesses, sidewalks, and doors free of snow and ice.
- Open faucets slightly so they drip and water circulates through pipes that could freeze. Ice may form anyway, but the open faucet relieves pressure and keeps the pipe from bursting.

### 6.6 Special Considerations: Extended Utility Loss

In a prolonged power outage, the precautions depend on the geographic location and the environment of the facility.

| When | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| During the outage | Turn off unnecessary equipment and electrical appliances: when power returns there can be a surge that damages electronics and sensitive equipment |
| During the outage, in subzero temperatures: lines | Shut off and drain these 4 lines: fire sprinkler system · standpipes · drinking water lines · toilets |

### 6.7 Earthquakes

- **Risks:** injuries from collapses and falling objects.
- A strong earthquake leaves ongoing problems: unstable buildings · damaged infrastructure · other natural disasters, such as landslides, tsunamis, and aftershocks.
- They are hard to anticipate. Their impact cannot be predicted until they occur, and that complicates security’s work.
- They are among the hardest crises to manage: the impact is wide and public help is assumed to take a long time.
- That is why the team must be sized for a recovery with less outside support.

#### 6.7.1 Planning Considerations

- The crisis management planner must ensure enough members on the earthquake response team. That way there will always be trained personnel at each facility.
- To understand the risk, the planner can study the earthquake hazard maps and the seismic building codes and standards of the area.

#### 6.7.2 Preparing for an Earthquake

| Topic | Actions |
| --- | --- |
| Sprinklers | Automatic sprinklers must have earthquake sway bracing. Standard: NFPA Standard 13, “Standard for the Installation of Sprinkler Systems” (NFPA = National Fire Protection Association, a fact external to the book) |
| Furniture and equipment | Bolt to the floor or wall: bookcases · filing cabinets · display cases · workstations · flammable liquid cabinets · cable or equipment racks · other tall, heavy objects · strap sensitive, critical, or expensive equipment (computers and servers) to desks, workbenches, or racks · place equipment that cannot be secured or that is moved often on seismic damping mats · base-isolate large equipment: boilers · pumps · chillers · backup electric generators |
| Nonstructural hazards | Identify and mitigate: light fixtures · storage racks · hanging objects · mirrors (open list in the book) · secure water heaters to the wall and put flexible connections on the gas line; if they tip over, they cause fires and a possible source of drinking water is lost |
| Building | Consult a structural engineer to meet seismic safety standards · arrange for the engineer to inspect buildings after an earthquake |
| Personnel | Train in first aid and CPR (cardiopulmonary resuscitation) · train in the location of shutoff valves for gas, electricity, water, and hazardous gas or chemical lines · encourage employees to keep emergency supplies at their desk or in their car |

- **Base-isolate:** prevent the base of the equipment from moving, or install devices that let the base move independently.

**Minimum employee kit, at the desk or in the car (14):**

1. First aid supplies and prescription medications.
2. High-energy, low-salt food, packaged, dehydrated, or canned.
3. Manual (non-electric) can opener.
4. Portable battery-powered radio.
5. Flashlight or light sticks.
6. Extra cash.
7. Sneakers.
8. Blanket.
9. Safety whistle, which should always be in a pocket or bag.
10. Photo of the children or family.
11. Phone numbers of out-of-town contacts.
12. Change of underwear and socks.
13. Toiletries and personal hygiene items.
14. Proof of address, such as a water bill.

#### 6.7.3 During an Earthquake

| Where you are | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Indoors | Take cover under a desk or table, or sit or stand next to an interior wall (not under a doorframe) · hold on tight to the furniture until the shaking stops · move away from windows and from objects that could fall · do not run out while it is shaking · when it stops, evacuate if there is visible structural damage · do not use the telephone and hang up again any receivers that have fallen · do not use open flames |
| Outdoors | Do not enter any building · move away from buildings, falling glass, poles, wires, and large trees · drop to the ground: duck, cover, hold · when it stops, watch for glass, electric wires, poles, and other debris |
| Driving | Move away from overpasses and underpasses · stop in a safe place · set the parking brake · stay in the vehicle; if wires fall on it, do not get out until you are rescued |

**Exam note:** the book says “duck, cover, hold.” The most widely used official formula is “drop, cover, and hold on” (a fact external to the book). Inside the building, you should NOT get under a doorframe.

### 6.8 Fire

- Fires can have internal or external causes. Plans must cover both scenarios.
- **Fire safety at work includes:** maintaining the safety infrastructure, such as fire equipment · updating exits and escape routes · eliminating electrical and chemical hazards · training employees in an evacuation strategy.
- **Wildfires:** they are a growing threat to businesses and infrastructure and call for different planning.
  - They can arrive with little or no warning and last weeks or months.
  - They are more common in high-risk areas, but can occur almost anywhere.
  - Practical measure: trim trees and brush near the building.
  - The most important thing security can do: an evacuation plan with support and frequent contact with employees · communication channels established within the organization · management of critical infrastructure.

#### 6.8.1 Planning Considerations

- Security is often a key piece of fire evacuation, with two roles:
  - floor wardens, who direct people out of the building;
  - maintenance representatives, who attend the fire alarm panel.
- That is why security must take part in all planning for: evacuation protocols · assembly locations · a solid relationship with the local fire department.
- Fire safety and evacuation are often heavily regulated. The book refers to local requirements for building a detailed prevention, response, and mitigation checklist.

**Exam note:** it is the only section from 6.2 to 6.11 without a checklist of its own.

### 6.9 Medical Emergencies

Every security department and every crisis team must be able to respond to any medical emergency. The range goes from simple first aid to coordinating the response to a mass casualty event.

#### 6.9.1 Planning Considerations

- Security officers are the most likely first responders. They must be trained in first aid regularly.
- They must know where the automatic external defibrillators (AEDs) and other critical equipment are.
- There must be a medical response plan with: where to direct and where to receive responders · whom to notify within the organization · other clear procedures to speed up the response.
- **The exact level of medical training depends on two things:** the availability of professional medical care near the facility · the level of specialized care that the facility’s risk profile requires.
- **Examples:** chemical plant → train security to care for employees exposed to the chemicals on site · shopping mall → train officers in trips and falls, heart attacks, and similar incidents.

**Exam note:** the usual name of the equipment is “automated external defibrillator” (a fact external to the book). The book writes “automatic.”

#### 6.9.2 Preparing for a Medical Emergency

- Identify and train a building emergency response team. It is activated at the affected facility.
- **Its 4 responsibilities:**
  1. Immediate safety of lives, evacuation or shelter-in-place.
  2. Initial assessment of the event.
  3. Escalating or notifying the crisis management team when appropriate.
  4. Liaison with local public emergency services.

#### 6.9.3 During a Medical Emergency

- Call the medical emergency number; for example, 911 in the United States.
- **Information for emergency medical personnel:** nature of the emergency · location (address, building, room number) · your name and the phone number you are calling from.
- Do not move the victim unless absolutely necessary.
- Call personnel trained in CPR and first aid to attend until professional help arrives.
- **If no first aid personnel are available, at a minimum try to:**
  - stop the bleeding with firm pressure on the wound, avoiding contact with blood or other body fluids;
  - clear the airway with the Heimlich maneuver in case of choking;
  - if attending to people exposed to hazardous materials, consult the internal documentation and use the proper PPE (personal protective equipment);
  - give first aid only if trained and qualified.

**Exam note:** the list asks to help “if there is no trained personnel,” but ends by saying to give first aid only if trained. The book does not resolve that tension.

#### 6.9.4 After a Medical Emergency

- Investigate the root cause and correct the hazards or safety problems.
- Deploy the biohazard cleanup teams or work needed to restore the facility.

### 6.10 HAZMAT Incidents

- HAZMAT = hazardous materials.
- **Forms:** chemical spills · gas leaks · vehicle crashes.
- Security must regularly assess the risk that on-site hazardous materials pose to workers. Especially in warehouses, factories, or industrial operations.
- **Multi-leveled approach, 5 elements:**
  1. Identify the material.
  2. Control the leak or spill.
  3. Assess the threats to personnel or infrastructure.
  4. Share information with employees and the community.
  5. Decontaminate and clean up.
- Emergency services almost always have to be called. Even so, security needs a detailed plan to identify, mitigate, and resolve the incident.
- **What is most often forgotten on the crisis team: public relations.** If the spill covers a large area or harms the community, immediate crisis communications will be needed.
- When forming the crisis team for this event, include those who can protect the company’s physical and reputational assets.
- Every HAZMAT incident is unique; local laws and guidance must be followed.
- The following two lists are an example of actions after a chemical spill.

#### 6.10.1 and 6.10.2 When a Large / Small Chemical Spill Has Occurred

| Aspect | Large spill (6.10.1) | Small spill (6.10.2) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Whom to notify | Immediately the designated official and the emergency coordinator | The emergency coordinator or the supervisor |
| Contain and isolate | Contain with available equipment: pads, booms, absorbent powder (open list in the book) · secure the area and alert the rest of the personnel | If there are toxic vapors, cordon off with caution tape or cones so no one enters |
| Cleanup | Do not attempt to clean up unless trained · call a local spill cleanup company or the fire department (if there is a prior agreement) for large chemical spills, for example of mercury | Treat the spill according to the safety data sheet · do it safely and with the proper PPE · review the general spill cleanup procedures |
| People | Attend to the injured and call the medical emergency number if needed · evacuate the building if necessary | The book does not indicate anything |

**Key difference:** the large spill is cleaned up by a specialized company or the fire department. The small one is handled on site, with the safety data sheet and PPE.

### 6.11 Pandemic

- An infectious disease in the community can keep the organization from functioning. It affects personnel, customers, suppliers, and community services.
- **Pandemic (WebMD definition cited by the book):** an outbreak that spreads across several countries or continents and affects and kills more people than an epidemic.
- A local outbreak or a regional epidemic can also affect the organization.
- **The impact depends on:** the number of infected people in the area · the severity of the effect on people · how much it limits their ability to function in the community.

#### 6.11.1 Planning Considerations

Crisis team objectives in a pandemic or local outbreak:

1. Reduce transmission among employees, customers, visitors, and partners.
2. Maintain critical operations and services.
3. Minimize the economic impact of the event.
4. Maintain operational efficiency even if absenteeism of essential employees increases.

- **Difference from other threats:** instead of the before/during/after model, it is best to align the response with the pandemic phases of the WHO (World Health Organization).

#### 6.11.2 Pandemic Phases

- Source: the WHO’s global influenza preparedness plan. It distinguishes 6 phases.
- Actual spread may not follow these phases and may span several at once.

| Phase | What defines it |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | No new influenza virus subtypes have been detected in humans. An animal subtype that has already infected humans may be present in animals; if so, the human risk is considered low |
| 2 | Likewise, no new subtypes in humans. But a circulating animal subtype poses a considerable risk of human disease |
| 3 | Human infections with a new subtype. No spread between humans or, at most, rare cases of spread to a close contact |
| 4 | One or more small clusters with limited human-to-human spread. Highly localized: the virus is not well adapted to humans |
| 5 | Larger clusters, but human-to-human spread is still localized. The virus is becoming increasingly better adapted, although it may not yet be fully transmissible (considerable pandemic risk) |
| 6 | Pandemic: increasing and sustained transmission in the general population |

- **Grouping the book uses to plan the response:** phases 1 and 2 (6.11.4) · phase 3 (6.11.5) · phases 4 and 5 (6.11.6) · phase 6 (6.11.7) · post-pandemic transition period (6.11.8).

**Exam note:** in 2013 the WHO replaced this 6-phase scheme with continuous phases: interpandemic, alert, pandemic, and transition (a fact external to the book). For the exam, use the book’s 6 phases.

#### 6.11.3 Planning Assumptions

- Up to **40%** of the workforce may be absent during different periods of a pandemic.
- Infected people (or negative people with symptoms) may have to quarantine under mandatory public health rules.
- Those who had close contact with an infected person may have to quarantine.
- Some employees will have to care for sick family members or for children in remote classes.
- High-risk employees may have to work from home.

#### 6.11.4 Planning a Response to Pandemic Alert Phases 1 and 2

| Area | Actions |
| --- | --- |
| Business continuity | Identify the essential services and business processes needed to operate · prepare for a socially distanced workforce: telework or new workplace safety rules · plan for enough backup personnel in case of high absenteeism · the continuity team must create formal succession protocols for delegation of authority |
| Communications | Appoint a pandemic manager and a response team · create processes and protocols for sharing internal and external information · raise employee awareness of flu preventive measures, through multiple channels · create an internal and external communication matrix for the 6 alert levels |
| Technology management | Prepare VPN (virtual private network), communication software and hardware, and technical support for those who work from home · plan for the increased use of the VPN and those tools · provide the technology, support, and protocols to share information effectively · plan how to upgrade, buy, rent, or share the necessary communication technologies · set emergency communication and information-sharing protocols for each technology · ensure sufficient, planned technology redundancies |
| Facilities | Review building access procedures and identify where the critical equipment is · have enough tissues, medical and hand hygiene products, and cleaning supplies for anyone who falls ill at work (open list in the book); buying them can be difficult once the pandemic has started |
| Human resources | Review policies and procedures on an influenza pandemic · create standard operating procedures for employees who report sick · set or communicate HR policies such as: flexible hours · telework · the Family Medical Leave Act or a similar government leave program |

### 6.12 Human Threats

#### 6.12.1 Workplace Violence

- **Workplace violence.** It is defined by the ASIS standard *Workplace Violence and Active Assailant—Prevention, Intervention, and Response* (ANSI/ASIS WVPI AA-2020; ANSI = American National Standards Institute). Elements:
  - A range of behaviors: overt violent acts, threats, and other behaviors that cause reasonable fear for safety.
  - A nexus between that behavior and the physical safety of employees.
  - The relationship with the perpetrator can be internal or external.
- **Targeted / intended violence.** A conscious decision to kill or physically harm specific or symbolic victims, at a workplace or a campus.
  - It is classified as **planned, emotionless, and predatory**.
- **Pathway to violence.** Research and cases indicate that the perpetrator starts from an unresolved grievance, real or perceived.
- The crisis response revolves around detecting and attending to whoever may be on that path.

| Stage | Name in English |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Ideation | Ideation |
| 2. Planning | Planning |
| 3. Acquisition | Acquisition |
| 4. Probing / surveillance | Probing / surveillance |
| 5. Action / implementation | Action / implementation |

- **Goal:** break the pathway as early as possible. This is achieved by identifying, mitigating, and responding to pre-incident indicators.

**Exam note:** the book gives 5 stages and treats the grievance as the origin, not as a stage. Other models, such as that of Calhoun and Weston, put the grievance as the first stage and use other names (a fact external to the book). On the exam, use the book’s 5.

#### 6.12.2 Workplace Violence Profiles

Internal and external sources of threat according to ANSI/ASIS WVPI AA-2020 (7):

- Current and former employees.
- Customers.
- Vendors.
- Contractors and others who work with the organization or on its behalf.
- Friends, family members, and intimate partners.
- Visitors and guests.
- People opposed to the mission, goals, or objectives of the organization.

#### 6.12.3 Before a Workplace Violence Threat Occurs

Preparation is the most important thing and takes many forms.

**Leadership, best practices, and assessment**

- Train senior leadership on workplace violence threats and their risk management.
- Review and adopt best practices. Examples: the DHS (Department of Homeland Security) *Workplace Violence Framework* or the cited ASIS standard.
- Assess the level of competence in workplace violence and the physical security “hygiene.” It spans from the board to the new hire, including those responsible for physical security.
- Diagnose competence gaps and create an executable education and training plan for the whole company.
- Preemployment screening and vetting with appropriate background checks. It aims to detect psychological problems or unacceptable work histories.

**Concerning behaviors of employees or other related persons (16 signs; the grouping is our own, for memorization)**

| Area | Signs |
| --- | --- |
| Mood and mental health | Sudden, intense mood swings · Signs of depression · More anger or tantrums · Suicidal ideations |
| Substance use and isolation | Increased alcohol and drug use · Social isolation |
| Appearance | Change in appearance or neglect of hygiene |
| Performance | More unexplained absences · Lower output |
| Relationships at work | Arguments with coworkers and supervisors · Bullying, workplace harassment |
| Threats and damage | Implicit or explicit threats · Destruction, theft, or sabotage of property · Increased interest in weapons, firearms, or violence |
| Personal life | Changes in romantic or family relationships · Changes in life insurance |

**Plan, partnerships, and programs**

- Create an executable plan for responding to threats and incidents.
- Foster trust, collaboration, and mutual help with those who bring competencies in an incident:
  - Police and other agencies.
  - Public-private partnerships.
  - Outside counsel.
  - Public relations firms.
  - Investigative and physical security firms.
- Integrate training curriculum standards based on competencies or on the gaps identified. It improves operational readiness.
- Ensure that legal counsel knows the legal aspects of these incidents.
- Create formal awareness and prevention programs, sponsored by the organization. They include referral to psychological or medical help.
- Maintain a physical security posture that protects the workforce from risky visitors. Examples:
  - Former employees.
  - Family members.
  - A jilted partner.
  - Disgruntled vendors.

**Policy and manual**

- Write a formal policy and procedures manual, approved and backed by senior leadership. At a minimum it includes:
  - All the preparation and response recommendations above.
  - A zero tolerance policy for employees, visitors, and contractors.
- The manual includes procedures to avoid and de-escalate a violent situation.
- The policy also covers personnel who work after hours or who handle or transport large amounts of cash.

**Working with managers and employees**

- Create a management culture aware that an incident can happen at any time.
- Use the manual to train at all levels: how to react in an incident and how to notice early signs of strange behavior.
- Communicate the policy to every employee, so that they recognize early signs and incidents.
- After each training session, hold a postmortem after-action debriefing. It serves to correct the training and incorporate lessons learned.
- Promote a culture of watching for prior signs, with mandatory reporting of any sign or incident.

#### 6.12.4 During a Workplace Violence Threat

- Follow the current threat management plan.
- If applicable, alert and activate the workplace violence incident response team.
- The response depends on the situation and must be proportional:

| Observed behavior | Response |
| --- | --- |
| Intimidation, harassment, disrespect, or verbal abuse | Observe, document, and report for assessment · A supervisor meets with the employee, addresses the problem, and recommends and coordinates actions according to policies |
| Angry person who argues, disobeys policies, sabotages or breaks equipment, steals, says they will harm others, or shows the pathway to violence with notes or threatening phrases | Immediately notify supervisors · If applicable, call the police · Protect your own and others’ safety |
| Any violent action | Security observes, documents, and reports · Supervisor, security, and possibly outside police meet with the employee and coordinate actions according to policies |
| Violent or threatening behavior with immediate danger to people or property | Evacuate · Leave the area of the attack · Activate the workplace violence protocols (below) |

**Protocols for immediate danger**

- Call 911 and other emergency contacts, such as the building’s response teams. Especially if medical personnel or police are needed.
- Stay calm and notify a supervisor.
- Ensure personal safety first.
- Leave the area if safety is at risk.
- Cooperate with the police when they arrive.
- If there is an active assailant or shooter: run, hide, or act against them to protect yourself or others.
- Mitigate continuing harm according to the response plan.
- Preserve all data: logs, notes, and files.

**Follow-up and notifications**

- Monitor for attacks in progress. In a serious incident, add cross-functional teams to check that no other systems are compromised.
- According to the response plan, notify:
  - Management and relevant personnel of the victim organization.
  - Police.
  - DHS or another national security organization.
  - Other possible victims.

**Exam note:** here the order is “run, hide, or act.” In 6.14.3 the book presents hiding first and then evacuating. The DHS formula is Run, Hide, Fight (a fact external to the book).

**Exam note:** 6.12.3 to 6.12.5 repeat almost point by point the structure of 6.18 (cyber threats). That is why “other systems compromised,” “victim organization,” and notifying DHS appear in a workplace violence topic.

#### 6.12.5 After a Threat of Workplace Violence — Not an Active Threat

- Keep monitoring, in collaboration, personnel, customers, workers, vendors, and suppliers for anomalous activity. Confirm that the threat is mitigated.
- Prepare an after-action report and review.
- Continuously assess the effectiveness of the management and response plan:
  - What worked well.
  - Vulnerabilities and deficiencies in response to threats and in physical security risk competencies.
  - Failures of planning, preparation, and execution of the plan.
- Design and deploy measures to be more effective going forward.

### 6.13 Domestic Violence

- It is a **type of workplace violence** that is often overlooked.
- It can become workplace violence if the partner comes to the workplace to confront the employee.
- It is a delicate scenario. Because of its personal nature, traditional risk assessments and reporting channels can fail.
- **Role of security:** in close partnership with HR (Human Resources), foster a culture that encourages victims to seek support within the organization.
- That culture includes a policy covering:
  - Privacy and confidentiality.
  - Schedule flexibility.
  - Social service resources.
  - A safety plan.

#### 6.13.1 Planning Considerations

- If a case is reported, security prepares an action plan for when the abuser:
  - Calls or emails employees.
  - Threatens the workplace.
  - Shows up at the office.
- More surveillance outside the office may be required, for example in parking lots, and escorts for the affected employee.
- Frequent check-ins with HR may be required, per the policy, to adjust the response to the threat.
- Encourage employees to tell security as soon as possible about protection orders they request or threats they receive.
- When a spouse leaves, work and school are often where the ex-partner can locate the victim. They are the first places the ex-partner looks.
- Security must know about the threat. The plan includes appropriate access control and visitor management at the site where the victim works.
- **Kimberly Brunell**, associate director of the Crisis Security and Consulting practice at Control Risks, proposes integrating domestic violence into the workplace violence policy. Article: “COVID-19: The Rise in Domestic Violence” (Security Management, July 2020). According to her, the policy should:
  - Define domestic violence.
  - Address safety and protection measures.
  - Set recordkeeping protocols.
  - Clarify when domestic violence violates the contract or terms of employment.
  - Cover off-duty conduct and other non-sanctionable conduct that worries coworkers or affects work.

#### 6.13.2 Signs of Domestic Violence

Knowing the signs makes it possible to help the employee access support. Source: Government of Australia, *Employer Guide to Family and Domestic Violence* (2021). There are 10 signs (grouping is our own):

| Area | Signs |
| --- | --- |
| Attendance and schedules | Excessive absences or lateness, especially on Mondays · Frequent or unusual breaks, or unusual arrival and departure times · Inability to make work trips |
| Performance | Sudden or sustained drop in productivity · Lack of concentration or difficulty making decisions |
| Physical signs | Frequent unexplained bruises or injuries · Clothing that covers the body even in hot weather |
| Emotional state | Visible anxiety · Distraction, depression, or excessive startling |
| External contact | Excessive personal calls, messages, or visits |

#### 6.13.3 Preparing for a Domestic Violence Threat or Incident

According to the Australian guide (2021):

- Know the legal obligations as an employer.
- Create a workplace policy that supports those who experience family and domestic violence.
- Educate and raise awareness on an ongoing basis. Example: posters with contacts for local support services.
- Regularly review policies, safety plans, and procedures.
- Create an open culture that makes it easy to raise concerns and receive support.

In addition, according to Brunell:

- Check that policies include accommodations for victims. Example: leave for domestic violence in compliance with state and local laws.
- Require or strongly encourage reporting of restraining orders. Also address cases where the employee is the abuser.
- Encourage reporting of safety concerns without fear of losing one’s job.
- Give managers proportionate training to detect signs and respond.
- Ensure managers know how to escalate concerning cases.
- Have a trained team to support or manage the most serious cases.

#### 6.13.4 Responding to Domestic Violence

If an employee is suspected of experiencing it (Australian guide, 2021):

- Start a conversation.
- Explain their workplace rights and options, such as leave or flexible work.
- Discuss safety measures if they feel unsafe at work:
  - Screen their incoming calls.
  - Block emails.
  - Change their phone number.
  - Change their schedule or workplace.
- Give them information on where to get help.
- Keep all disclosures and actions confidential.

Additional steps from Brunell to respond and prevent another incident at work:

- Assure the victim that they will have time off without work consequences. They may need it to seek shelter or legal help, care for affected children, or other services.
- Ask whether they already have protection or restraining orders. Request a copy and help monitor compliance with their conditions.
  - Corporate restraining orders are requested only with the participation and input of the victim.
- Ask for a recent photo of the abuser and a description of their vehicle. Tell reception whom and what to look for.
- Talk with the employee and look for solutions together. Follow up on their well-being.
- Create a physical space where they feel safe. It may include relocating them so they cannot be seen through windows or from outside.
- Offer flexible hours if appropriate.
- Do not publish their contact information in public directories or on the company website.
- Have someone else screen and log the abuser’s calls. Forward their voicemails and emails to a designated mailbox.
  - That mailbox is monitored by someone other than the victim, watching for signs of escalation.
- Program 911 into the landline or cell phone. Install a panic button at their workstation or give personal alarms.
- Consider hidden, secure parking, away from the street. It keeps the abuser from monitoring whether the employee is in or not.
- Provide an escort to the car or public transportation.
- Call the police if the abuser commits crimes: stalking, trespassing, or unauthorized electronic surveillance.
- If victim and abuser work in the same place, do not schedule them at the same time or in the same location, if possible.
- Use the disciplinary system to hold the abuser accountable for their conduct at work.

### 6.14 Active Threats

- This section deals with the crisis response to ongoing violent acts. Actors with subtle prior signs are dealt with under workplace violence (6.12).
- There are unexpected incidents caused by people without prior signs. That is why no preventive action diverted them from the pathway to violence.
- **Definition:** active threats (formerly, active shooter events) are varied incidents in which one or more people seek to injure or kill as many people as possible.
- They are usually planned attacks with firearms, knives, or vehicles.
- At work, they are targeted or predatory violence, although many victims are chosen at random.

#### 6.14.1 Planning Considerations

A comprehensive active threat plan has 5 sections:

| Section | English |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Prevent | Prevent |
| 2. Protect | Protect |
| 3. Mitigate | Mitigate |
| 4. Respond | Respond |
| 5. Recover | Recover |

Prevention and survival depend heavily on 5 factors:

- Strength of the workplace violence prevention program: training, reporting channels, and employee assistance programs, for example.
- Facility layout and access control.
- Alert technologies.
- Staff training in active threat / active assailant response.
- Security or police response.

Recommended approach:

- It is impossible to protect against every type of hostile intruder attack. The **all-hazards approach** is the most efficient way to prepare employees and security.
- First, a risk assessment to see whether the site faces a special risk of a certain type of attack.
- Then, security creates a complete response plan:
  - With partnerships with local police.
  - With defined roles for other departments: HR, legal, and IT (information technology).
- Regular training and drills have a major impact on prevention and on response.

#### 6.14.2 Preparing for an Active Threat/Incident Occurs

- Make a crisis management plan specific to each facility, adapted to local conditions, without losing the fundamentals of the general policy.
- Designate a crisis management team at each facility. Members: security personnel, supervisors, managers, or others, according to knowledge, work area, and needs.
- **Annual** training in workplace violence prevention and active threat response, with drills. Include it in the onboarding of new employees.
- Walk through the facilities to review:
  - Evacuation and escape routes.
  - Lockdown or hiding places.
  - Several rally points around the site.
  - Other considerations for an incident.
- Share information only with those who need to know for official matters.
- Consider the design of work areas:
  - Access control.
  - Customer and guest area, and protection of employees.
  - Functional accessibility.
- Be able to quickly alert everyone, employees and customers, by a means other than the fire alarm. Examples: a specific active threat alarm, a public address system, or other technologies.
- First aid kits and emergency supplies well located and marked. Consider AEDs (automatic external defibrillators) or trauma kits in certain areas.
- Have a **mobile threat/evacuation kit** with:
  - Plans of the facility or office, including critical or sensitive areas.
  - Management contacts, also after-hours or alternate (personal cell phone, home phone).
  - Site keys or access cards.
  - Access codes, usernames, and passwords for critical applications:
    - Surveillance cameras.
    - Access control systems.
    - Life/emergency systems.
- Have a media contact point for public relations, customer messages, and crisis communications.
- Be able to set up a mobile command post or an emergency operations center (EOC).

#### 6.14.3 During an Active Threat/Incident

**General principles**

- These are highly changing and chaotic incidents. There will be moments without control, but the duty to act and respond remains.
- Immediately assess the environment and choose the option that saves lives.
- Communicate with police or emergency services only from a safe place.
- Recording or photographing the suspect is secondary to saving lives.
- There is permission to break internal rules if needed to be safe. Examples: breaking a window to escape or using physical violence against the attacker.
- People may look to security personnel for guidance and direction.
- **Options-based response** mindset: being able to change the response according to the situation.

| Option | English names | When |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hide / shelter / lock down | Hide · Shelter · Lockdown | If it is not possible to escape the area |
| Evacuate / escape / flee | Evacuate · Escape · Flee | If it is possible to escape the area |
| Fight / resist / overcome / take action | Fight · Resist · Overcome · Take Action | Last resort, with life in imminent danger |

**Hide / shelter / lock down**

- A good hiding place must:
  - Be out of the attacker’s sight. Take into account what can be seen from outside, such as windows.
  - Protect if shots are fired nearby. Example: an office with the door locked.
  - Avoid trapping or limiting later movement or escape.
- To create a safe space:
  - Lock the door or doors.
  - Block the door with objects, in case the lock fails, the attacker has a key, or there is no lock.
  - Close, cover, and secure the windows, and move away from them.
  - Do not stand in the center of the room. Better corners, behind large objects (filing cabinets, desks), or remote areas out of the line of sight of doors and windows.
  - Do not stay at the door.
  - Turn off all lights.
  - Silence cell phones.
  - Stay quiet.
- Quickly choose the best available place.

| Concept | What it does | Example from the book |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cover | Protects from gunfire | Metal filing cabinet full of papers |
| Concealment | Only keeps the attacker from seeing | Window curtain |

- When possible and safe, call 911 or the local number. The operator will ask:
  - Location of the event.
  - Your location.
  - Your name.
  - Your phone number.
  - Number of attackers.
  - Physical description of the attackers.
  - Number and type of weapons.
  - Last known location of the attackers.
  - Number of potential victims.
  - That you stay on the line and give updates.

**Evacuate / escape / flee**

- Warn others not to enter the area and encourage them to escape too.
- Have a route or escape plan.
- Do not leave in a group: split up and flee.
- If running across a large or open area, consider running in a disorderly zigzag.
- Know the several rally points and choose the safest one depending on where the attacker is.
- Evacuate even if others do not want to follow you.
- Leave belongings behind.
- Help others escape, if possible.
- Do not stay behind to attend to the injured.
- Keep hands visible if the police have already arrived.
- Follow the instructions of police and emergency services.
- Be ready to move again if the attacker approaches the hiding place or the rally point.
- When possible and safe, call 911 or the local number.
- Security personnel can direct employees to safe areas, without neglecting their own safety.
- Security personnel or the crisis management team begins the employee headcount and coordinates with police when appropriate.

**Fight / resist / take action**

Last resort, with life in imminent danger. The attempt is to disrupt or incapacitate the attacker:

- Act as aggressively as possible.
- Throw objects or use improvised weapons: fire extinguisher, books, bags, keys.
- Yell or make a lot of noise to create chaos for the attacker.
- Commit to the action.
- A group is more effective than one person. With no alternative, try to get the whole group to act.
- Some employees have concealed carry permits. Consider a separate policy and consult legal counsel on personal firearms.
- If security personnel are armed, they follow the organization’s use of force policy.
- This action is self-defense and is protected by applicable laws.
- If the attacker’s weapon is obtained, do not use it to defend yourself. Secure it away from the attacker.

**When the police arrive**

- Follow their instructions.
- Drop whatever you are carrying in your hands (bags, jackets).
- Immediately raise your hands with fingers spread.
- Keep your hands visible at all times, until told otherwise.
- Be ready to be searched and questioned.
- Depending on the number of people and the incident, you may be asked not to talk to anyone until the police interview.
- Expect not to be able to leave for quite a while.

**Exam note:** the book puts “hide” first and then “evacuate.” But it says one hides if one cannot escape. Therefore, escaping is preferable when possible.

**Exam note:** two nuances that seem to clash. Helping others escape, yes; staying to attend to the injured, no. And although maximum aggressiveness is called for, the weapon taken from the attacker is not used: it is secured.

#### 6.14.4 Vehicle Ramming Attacks

- Immediately escape from the area where the vehicles are.
- If the vehicle is still moving, go to a place out of its path.
- Assess whether it is a crash (traffic accident) or a targeted attack.
- Do not try to enter the vehicle or pull out the driver or passengers.
- Evacuate the building or structure it crashed into.
- When possible and safe, call 911 or the local number.
- Consider the risk of a secondary attack or device: VBIED (vehicle-borne improvised explosive device).
- Be alert to a possible fire or chemical leak, depending on what it crashed into.

### 6.15 After an Active Threat Incident

The subsequent recovery is divided into **3 phases**:

| Phase | Focus | Actions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Immediate | Life safety · Accounting for all people on site · Communication with employees, families, the public, and the media | Confirm with police and emergency services that the danger has passed · Once safe, start business continuity or crisis response · Consider temporarily closing part or all of the site · Notify management and corporate offices · Record each employee’s location, well-being, and status (fled to a neighboring business, went home, injured, deceased) · Ask employees not to share details (news, social media) · Locate the organization’s mobile command post or EOC, separate from the police’s · Communicate the temporary closure to customers, if the site is open to the public |
| Short-term | Preserve the crime scene · Watch for signs of psychological trauma · Support reunification and other tasks · Keep communicating | Have counseling or psychiatric services · Release the day’s employees when the police finish the interviews · Plan when and how to reoccupy the site · Prepare messages for the whole organization · Collaborate in the criminal investigation (evidence, internal review, surveillance video) · Prepare statements to media and messages to customers if applicable · Consider more security resources when the police finish at the scene · Hire environmental cleanup when the police release the facility |
| Long-term | Post-incident analysis (debrief) with internal and external responders and stakeholders · Replenish supplies · Maintain communication with the appropriate groups | Anticipate prolonged absences days or weeks later · Long-term counseling and workers’ compensation issues · Employee assistance programs · Psychological impact on everyone involved · Quantify the financial impact · Rehire if some employees do not want to or cannot return · Consult legal counsel about possible litigation · Complete the after-action report and review · Set a deadline for removing memorials and tributes |

### 6.16 Civil Unrest

- Protests, strikes, or riots can create security challenges even if they do not target the workplace or its employees.
- They range from large peaceful crowds to a strategic disruption against an organization, a facility, or critical infrastructure (for example, transportation).
- They can turn violent or destructive.
- **Key difference:** the scale of response they demand sets them apart from other human threats at work.
- Demonstrations are usually planned and publicized on online platforms, or internally by groups or unions. That allows planning and gathering resources.
- They can also be spontaneous and demand an on-the-fly response.
- Whether or not there is lead time, the crisis management team must first understand the **nature, size, scope, and “mood”** of the event.

#### 6.16.1 Planning Considerations

- Once the threat is understood, the team chooses the protective approach that best de-escalates and avoids harm to everyone.
- At this stage communication must be clear and decisive.
- Possible consequences of protests and riots: deaths, serious injuries, significant property damage, and a complete halt to legitimate business.
- Security may not know in advance whether the protest will be peaceful or lawful. But **it is not security’s responsibility to keep people from protesting**.
- If the building is a target or is on the protest route, the team focuses on the classics: protecting **people, property, information, and reputation**.
- Many protests have “captains” who lead or organize the crowd. Contacting them and agreeing on the time, place, and manner of the protest can benefit everyone.

#### 6.16.2 Preparing for Civil Unrest

**Plan and assessment**

- Have a disciplined, documented plan as a guide.
- Understand the legal differences between civil unrest and an employee or union protest.
- Closely monitor the possibility of protests and understand the threat:
  - Is the facility likely to be a target or be near a target area?
  - Can it affect employee safety? And customer safety?

**Employees**

- Maintain communication with employees about threats, curfews, or other dangers.
- With HR, activate an employee assistance program for the situation. Communicate what the organization is doing to protect them.
- Consider working from home or at another location on the day of the event.
- Anticipate staffing disruptions:
  - Employees with school-age children who cannot come because of school closures.
  - Public transportation disruptions that prevent getting to work.
  - Employees’ right to take part in the protest.

**Building and coordination**

- If the property is vacated before the event, consider:
  - Securing the building (boarding up windows and doors).
  - Leaving a security team for critical areas and resources.
  - Remote surveillance.
- Communicate with the police to coordinate planning or intelligence received.
- Define the steps for handling the protest:
  - Who makes certain decisions?
  - Who do internal messages come from (for example, the CEO or a vice president)?
  - Who is the contact with the organizers?
  - Who is the contact with the media?
  - Timeline and milestones for deciding.

**Decisions and exercises**

- In a protest or riot, protecting property and lives has special urgency and calls for methods different from normal. Decide thoughtfully and quickly.
- Hold tabletop exercises before the event. They serve to set “when/then” or “if/then” decisions and review the plan.
- Consider recording all security and management actions and communications during the event. It protects against future litigation, especially in union protests or over working conditions.
- Decide whether a **highly visible** security profile is needed to deter protesters or looters. In some protests a **discreet, low-visibility** approach is enough to calm the situation.

**Advance actions to withstand disruptions**

- Use other offices or properties.
- Communicate with neighboring businesses.
- Hire an outside security provider for more personnel or resources.
- Plan capabilities in case electricity or other services are cut.
- Protect the property in layers. Examples:
  - If the outer fence falls: reinforced doors and window protections that keep people from entering the building.
  - If the lobby of a multistory building is breached: shut down elevators and lock stairwell doors.

**Equipment, evacuation, and legal aspects**

- Have alternate evacuation plans if routes lead to or cross the event.
- Test and inspect emergency and communication equipment, and repair or replace. Examples: new batteries in portable radios, repairing a public address speaker.
- Consult legal counsel on any prepared statement or announcement.
- If it is an employee or union protest: review the HR and operations plans. Consider having an attorney witness on site, nearby, or by camera.
- Depending on the type of event, the organization may have little or nothing to say about where or how the protest takes place.
- Assume that local resources may not respond immediately. Examples: graffiti, break-ins to buildings, fire in trash cans, vegetation, or buildings.
- Thoroughly secure, move, or back up with remote access any sensitive or valuable object, material, or information.
- Remove or secure easily moved objects around the building: trash cans, rocks, benches.

#### 6.16.3 During Civil Unrest

**Business considerations**

- Address employees’ concern for their safety and that of their families.
- Keep up to date on the location and travel plans of all employees, and allow them to report their status.
- Give periodic updates of notices from local and state police or the government.
- Inform personnel of changes in the situation and of the status of operations.
- Inform customers of the status of operations.
- Set milestones or advance plans to operate differently or in another place.
- Have clear, concise emergency communications in case of evacuation: portable radios, mass text messaging system, public address system.
- Have a recorder, scribe, or system that documents:
  - Decisions, time, and who made them.
  - Communications to employees.
  - People involved (managers, supervisors, security teams).
  - Costs incurred (damage or loss of product, additional personnel or overtime).
- Follow social media, news, and other sources in real time.
- Decide based on safety, policies, plans, and applicable laws. These events become emotional. The risk is deciding on emotion rather than with decisions that can be justified later.

**Personal guidelines** (communicated before the event)

- Be alert to your surroundings.
- Go around crowds, not through them.
- Avoid talking to people in the crowd.
- If you sense trouble is coming, turn around and avoid the crowd.
- Carry a cell phone and identification.
- Avoid loose or snag-prone clothing and accessories. Wear stable, protective footwear; avoid sandals, heels, slip-ons, or footwear that limits movement or comes off.
- If you suddenly find yourself inside a large protest or riot: stay calm, move away from the most agitated areas, and get into a safe place or leave the area as soon as possible.
- Move away from temporary structures that could collapse under the weight of the crowd.
- Know the escape routes in the area.

#### 6.16.4 Post-Civil Unrest Response

- Check notices from local authorities to see whether a recurrence is likely.
- Communicate to employees and customers when it is safe to return or normal operations resume.
- Consider internal communication about the event and the company’s position.
- Inspect the facility and grounds for damage.
- Check on the status of potentially affected personnel.
- Hold an after-action review with everyone involved. Adjust plans and response, or request resources.
- Meet with HR and legal counsel about possible follow-up or sanctions for employees involved.
- Meet with the police to report damage or crimes.
- Replace the objects removed earlier.
- Consult legal counsel to mitigate future litigation.

### 6.17 Bomb Threats

- Motives vary; the book ranges from disgruntled employees to upset customers.
- **Main objective of the perpetrator:** to cause panic and anxiety in employees or customers, interrupt operations, and cause financial harm.
- Ways of receipt: electronic, telephone, postal mail, or in person.
- Many, perhaps most, turn out to be hoaxes. Even so, **all are taken seriously** and responded to appropriately.
- All personnel must know how to handle a threat in order to protect everyone.

#### 6.17.1 Planning Considerations

- Management responds with limited information and little time.
- Consequences of mismanagement: from operational impact (evacuating unnecessarily) to deaths (not evacuating in time).
- The reflex is to evacuate, but **evacuating is not always best**:
  - Constant evacuation undermines confidence that management provides a safe and productive environment.
  - It can generate copycats: employees seeking time off or outsiders who enjoy causing disruption.
- To respond well, the organization follows 5 steps:

| Step | Action |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Recognize that it has been threatened |
| 2 | Capture the information about the threat |
| 3 | Report it to the relevant internal authority |
| 4 | Assess the threat |
| 5 | Respond appropriately |

- Assessing the threat is a complex management decision. It requires procedures, planning, training, and rehearsal.
- Everyone must know how to write down the exact wording of a threatening call and forward a threatening email or other message.

#### 6.17.2 Preparing for a Bomb Threat

- Train all personnel to receive a threat:
  - Use of telephonic bomb threat cards.
  - Strategies to keep the caller on the line and get more information.
  - Being able to record the call or have others listen in.
  - Forensic and safety aspects if it arrives by postal mail (suspicious letter or package).
  - Forensic and safety aspects if it arrives by email, fax, or other electronic means.
  - Appropriate internal reporting channels (supervisor, manager, security).
  - Limiting internal and external communication to avoid premature or unauthorized responses, panic, or rumors (talking to coworkers, posting on social media).
- Have a mobile threat/evacuation kit, the same as in 6.14.2:
  - Plans, including critical or sensitive areas.
  - Management contacts, also after-hours or alternate (personal cell phone, home phone).
  - Keys or access cards.
  - Codes, usernames, and passwords for critical applications: surveillance cameras · access control · life/emergency systems.
- Set evacuation zones **beyond the potential blast radius**. **They are not the same as those for fire evacuation.**
- Create a **threat evaluation team**:
  - Members: security, managers, administrators, supervisors, leads, or other leaders regularly present.
  - They must bring different knowledge, including a good knowledge of the site, its operations, and its security.
- Practice the response **once a year** and include it in the onboarding of new employees.
- Check **“biannually”** that every point where calls are answered has the bomb threat response card.
- Identify the local first-response agency to call for bomb threats: municipal police, county sheriff, fire department, or other authority.
- Have site-wide communication and predefined scripts to announce an evacuation.
- Have a media contact point for public relations, customer messages, and crisis communications.

**Exam note:** “biannually” usually means twice a year (semiannual). Some confuse it with every two years (biennially). Semiannual is most likely (a fact external to the book).

#### 6.17.3 During a Bomb Threat

**Assess credibility** according to:

- What the suspect knows about the business or the site.
- Their possible identity (recognized voice, phone number, handwriting).
- How specific the threat is:
  - Time frame.
  - Area or location.
  - People, if named.
  - Type or size of the device.
- Sources of evidence that support it:
  - Camera or closed-circuit television (CCTV) recordings.
  - Access control records.
  - Interviews with personnel and others.
  - Searches: from having each employee check their area to formal searches in designated areas. Consider police support.
  - Knowing current events that could change the organization’s threat profile.
- Immediate safety of employees and customers.

**Other actions**

- Decide quickly and decisively.
- Notify the organization’s management.
- Notify neighboring businesses or locations: building management in multitenant offices or shopping centers, neighboring factory, adjacent units in a strip mall.

| If the threat is NOT plausible or credible | If the threat IS plausible or credible |
| --- | --- |
| Keep working · Tell the police that it was received and support the report or investigation · Tell personnel who know of it that it was carefully assessed and a decision was made to keep operating · Consider a message for the whole organization | Notify the police and, if needed, ask for help evacuating · Evacuate in coordination with the responsible local agency, with routes and gathering areas chosen according to the threat · Inform emergency services of the emergency rendezvous point · Look after site security during the evacuation · Depending on the threat, activate the business continuity plan: the evacuation can last hours |

- In most jurisdictions, **the final decision to evacuate is made by the organization’s management, unless a device is located**.
- Prepare statements to media and messages to customers if applicable.

**Exam note:** whoever decides to evacuate is the organization’s management, not the police, unless a device turns up. The book does not say expressly who decides in that case.

#### 6.17.4 Post-Bomb Threat Response

- Plan when and how to reoccupy the site.
- Prepare messages for the whole organization about the threat and the response.
- Collaborate with the police in the criminal investigation (evidence of the threat, internal review or investigation).
- Prepare for new threats from the same perpetrator or from copycats.
- Replenish the supplies used: bomb threat card, warning tape, evacuation checklists.
- Complete and file a record of the assessment and the internal report for future reference.

### 6.18 Cyber Threats

- Cyberattacks are almost ubiquitous for governments, organizations, and individuals.

| Book data point | Figure |
| --- | --- |
| Increase in cybercrime since the start of COVID-19, according to the FBI (Federal Bureau of Investigation) | 300% |
| People connected to the internet today | 4 billion |
| Devices connected today | 40 billion |
| Devices projected to be connected by 2030 | 1 trillion |
| Ports or end point vulnerabilities per application | 65,000 |

- The attack surface of asymmetric cybercrime has grown exponentially.
- They should **no longer** be viewed as low-probability events. Reasons:
  - Global interoperability and connection.
  - Recent unprecedented events in the country and in the world.
  - Social unrest.
  - Rising unemployment.
  - Prolonged isolation.
- Attackers have more vulnerabilities and opportunities to infiltrate critical ecosystems. They seek to **exfiltrate data or trigger an action**.
- New technology solutions are being integrated into critical areas of: Fortune 10, 50, 100, and 500 companies · healthcare organizations · critical infrastructure · federal, state, local, and tribal governments · public and private organizations · individual networks.
- Examples of impact raised by the book:
  - A hospital’s water is cut off.
  - A major online logistics platform goes down.
  - A local supermarket loses electricity.
  - The state 911 system is compromised.
  - Worse still: several or all of them happening at once in a community.

**Exam note:** technically, ports belong to each network device, not to each application, and there are about 65,535 (a fact external to the book). If asked for the book’s figure, it is 65,000.

#### 6.18.1 Planning Considerations

- There is **no single technology** that solves all cyber crisis planning.
- The **human factor causes 95%** of cybersecurity breaches.
- Technology must be addressed and also the physical, people, and processes.
- Critical elements of an effective crisis response plan (6):
  - Collaboration.
  - Communication.
  - Education.
  - Mutual aid assistance agreements.
  - Training.
  - Collaborative exercises.

#### 6.18.2 Before a Cyberattack or Intrusion

**Leadership, framework, and assessment**

- Train senior leadership on cyber threats and their risk management.
- Review and adopt best practices, such as the **NIST Cybersecurity Framework** (NIST = National Institute of Standards and Technology).
- Identify the mission-critical data and assets. Apply tiered security measures to them to align the strategy and mitigate their risk.
- Assess competence and “cyber hygiene”: from the board to those responsible for cybersecurity and the new hire.
- Diagnose competence gaps and create an executable education and training plan for all levels.

**Plan and exercises**

- Create an executable incident response plan.
- Test its effectiveness with collaborative exercises. They yield lessons that are shared across the company and help design a crisis management playbook.
- **Collaborative exercises do not seek to expose weaknesses.** They seek to leverage the strengths of one area so they can be shared across functions.
- Continuously evaluate the plan to reflect changes in personnel and structure.
- Foster trust, collaboration, and mutual help with: police, other agencies, public-private partnerships, outside counsel, public relations firms, and investigative and cybersecurity firms.

**Technology and controls**

- Leverage and integrate available technology to respond, or ensure that it can be easily obtained.
- Implement cybersecurity measures and procedures (9):
  - Anomaly detection.
  - Cloud service.
  - Duplicate (mirrored) database.
  - Data lakes.
  - End point artificial intelligence.
  - Firewalls.
  - Identity management.
  - Role-based access management.
  - Patch management program.

**People and legal aspects**

- Workforce education and training program in cyber hygiene and situational awareness, for example to resist phishing. Give the option to forward suspicious emails for evaluation.
- Integrate training curriculum standards based on competencies and gaps, to improve operational readiness.
- Train and supervise system administrators to reduce the risk of insider threats.
- Have procedures that allow lawful network monitoring.
- Ensure that legal counsel knows the legal aspects of cyber incidents.
- Align the organization’s strategic goals and policies (for example, HR and personnel) with the response plan.

#### 6.18.3 During a Cyberattack or Intrusion

- Follow the current plan.
- Alert and activate the cyber incident response team.
- Assess size, scope, and nature: intentional malicious act or unintentional technology failure?
- Mitigate continuing harm according to the plan.
- Collect forensic analysis according to the plan, including network imaging.
- Log and preserve all data: logs, notes, and files.
- Monitor ongoing attacks, **but do not engage or counterattack**.
- Check that any duplicate network brought online is not also compromised.
- According to the plan, notify:
  - Management and relevant personnel of the victim organization.
  - Police.
  - DHS or a similar government agency.
  - Other possible victims.

**Do not:**

- Use compromised systems to communicate.
- Hack back or enter another network. It can be a crime in some districts or countries, and the goal is to de-escalate.

#### 6.18.4 After Recovering from a Cyberattack or Intrusion

1. Continuously monitor the network and personnel for anomalous activity. Confirm that the attack is mitigated and that control of the network has been regained.
2. Prepare an after-action report and review.
3. Continuously assess the effectiveness of the plan: what worked and what vulnerabilities or deficiencies there were in cyber competence, planning, preparation, and execution.
4. Design and deploy measures to improve future effectiveness in managing cyber risk.

### Appendix A — Standards in Security (pp. 119–122)

#### What a standard is

- **Definition:** a set of criteria, guidelines, and best practices to improve the quality and reliability of products, services, or processes.
- Thanks to national and international standards, parts, processes, and systems work regardless of who creates them, who uses them, or where.
- They are created to meet a market need or to fill a gap in industry standards.
- They are coordinated through accredited standards developing organizations (SDOs).
- SDOs bring together stakeholders from the private and public sectors to identify industry best practices.
- They are the basis of innovation and competitiveness. They provide a common language that promotes national and international trade.
- They are developed on four principles: consensus · openness · due process · transparency.
- In security they help nations, communities, societies, organizations, and individuals protect assets.
- They also improve resilience against natural and human-caused threats.

#### Types of standards

| Type | What it is | Examples from the book |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Voluntary standards | Not required by law, but widely adopted · Some have been integrated into the regulatory frameworks of several countries | ISO (International Organization for Standardization) · National standards institutes · In the U.S., trade or professional associations such as ASIS International |
| Statutory or regulatory standards | Binding by law · Enforced by formal authorities | The book gives no examples |
| Mixed standards | The line blurs when a voluntary standard is incorporated into laws or regulations · Also when it remains voluntary in theory, but mandatory in practice | Fire, construction, and life safety areas · Insurers that set the availability and cost of casualty insurance according to approved materials or standardized practices · Contracts that incorporate standards as a requirement: adoption is increasingly driven by the customer |

**ISO topics relevant to assets protection:** facilities management · health, environment and safety · risk management · security and resilience · IT (information technology) and information security.

#### Bodies that issue standards

| Body | Scope according to the book | Nature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ISO (International Organization for Standardization) | Topics from the list above · Management system standards ISO 9001 and ISO 14001 | Voluntary, widely adopted |
| National standards institutes | National | Voluntary |
| ASIS International | Security management standards and guidelines · Has an extensive library | Voluntary (U.S. professional association) |
| NFPA (National Fire Protection Association) | The book does not detail topics | Not all voluntary: some are incorporated into regulations, such as building codes |
| UL (Underwriters Laboratories) | Security equipment: locks · alarms · access control systems | The book does not say |
| Illuminating Engineering Society | Lighting standards and practices | Trade or professional association |
| Electronic Industries Association | Electronic components and products | Trade or professional association |

**Exam note:** the NFPA appears in the paragraph on voluntary standards, but the book clarifies that not all of its standards are voluntary. It fits as an example of a mixed standard.

**Exam note:** the Electronic Industries Association was renamed Electronic Industries Alliance in 1997 and ceased operations in 2011 (a fact external to the book).

#### Benefits of Standards

They make the security professional’s work easier. A standard can fulfill one or all of these nine roles:

1. Codify best practices and processes, and share lessons learned.
2. Provide tools to assess threats, risks, vulnerabilities, criticalities, and impacts.
3. Define measurement methods.
4. Document equipment performance requirements, to ensure its effectiveness and safety.
5. Set design requirements so that devices, systems, and infrastructure withstand threats.
6. Define effective methods of identifying people.
7. Increase knowledge and competence.
8. Improve information sharing between jurisdictions and interoperability.
9. Provide consistency in services.

#### Management System Standards

- They help improve how the organization delivers services and executes processes.
- They are widely accepted and used in many fields and disciplines.
- The most prominent: ISO 9001 (quality management systems) and ISO 14001 (environmental management systems).
- The management systems approach leads to analyzing the requirements of the organization and of interested parties, and to defining processes that contribute to success.
- A management system provides the framework for continual improvement.
- This increases the likelihood of achieving strategic, operational, tactical, and reputational objectives, and strengthens resilience.

#### Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) Cycle

- It is the operating principle of management system standards.
- It is a structured problem-solving method, focused on continual improvement.

| Phase | According to the text | According to figure A.1 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plan | Identify and analyze the organization’s problems and assets · Rank them by importance | Define and analyze a problem and its context |
| Do | Prioritize the next steps · Develop a detailed action plan · Implement it | Devise a solution · Develop a detailed action plan · Implement it systematically |
| Check | Examine whether the solutions yield results consistent with the plan | Confirm the results against the plan · Identify deviations and problems |
| Act | If the solutions resolve the problems, standardize them across the organization · Review the list of problems · Define new problems: there the cycle begins again | Standardize the solution · Review and define the next problems |

## Capítulo 1 — Enterprise Security Risk Management (págs. 1–20)

**Central idea:** Enterprise Security Risk Management (ESRM) replaces management by "security programs" with a risk management process. The asset owner decides about the risk, and the security professional guides as a trusted advisor. ASIS International, the association that grants the CPP (Certified Protection Professional), adopted ESRM as the basis of all security management.

### 1. A brief history of ESRM (1.1.1)

**Earlier model (until the 21st century):**

- A professional or specialist managed security with a security program.
- The program was divided into subprograms, one for each exposure. Examples from the book: travel security, physical security, and contractor security.
- The "program" was the main management method.
- The security professional or department was the primary or sole owner of security.
- Risk and its assessment were used alongside the program and its subprograms. Focusing on risk was a growing trend.

**Timeline:**

| Year | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| 2010 | A group from the ASIS CSO Roundtable meets to treat risk management as the process for managing security. CSO = Chief Security Officer. The group is now called the CSO Center. |
| 2010 | They wanted to apply risk principles and coordinate with the senior management whose operations generate the risk. They surveyed their members. Results in *Enterprise Security Risk Management: How Great Risks Lead to Great Deeds* (CSO Roundtable, ASIS International, 2010). |
| 2017 | After a thorough review and evaluation, the ASIS Board of Directors decides to integrate ESRM as the foundation of security management. It launches a strategic initiative and a transition plan for members and the profession. |
| 2019 | The *Enterprise Security Risk Management guideline* (ASIS) is published. It was approved by the Board and followed the ASIS standards and guidelines process. It defines ESRM, its key components, and the actions to implement it. |
| No date in the book | Subject matter experts start the plan. To involve as many professionals as possible, ASIS creates the ESRM Community, responsible for executing the plan. |

**Two outcomes the Board set when adopting ESRM:**

1. Help the security professional become an integral part of the company's business leadership.
2. Move security practice from managing programs to managing risks.

### 2. What ESRM is (1.1.2)

**Definition:** a strategic approach to security management. It links the organization's security practice with its overall strategy. It uses risk management principles that are accepted and established worldwide.

Characteristics:

- It is a management process or system.
- It is not a program or a piece of an existing program. It replaces the program methodology.
- It connects all the key elements of security risk with the organization's assets. This informs asset owners' decisions.
- It is scalable and dynamic.
- It works for private or public organizations of any size or scope.

### 3. How ESRM differs (1.1.3)

| Aspect | Traditional program management | ESRM |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structure | One program with many subprograms | Not built around programs |
| Scope | One program for each specific threat or topic | All security risks to assets: they are identified, prioritized, and specific mitigation steps are defined |
| Where the risk goes | It is assigned to a program | It is not assigned to any program. The goal is to mitigate effectively |
| Role of the professional | Manages a delegated role. A task manager who executes service steps. Does what is asked or ordered | Trusted advisor and partner of asset owners. A strategic resource with a holistic view of risk |
| Who decides about the risk | The security professional or department, owner of security | The asset owner, with input and guidance from the professional. The ultimate responsible party, as with the other risks to their assets |

The professional gives information and guidance to owners to:

1. prioritize assets;
2. identify and prioritize the risks to those assets;
3. choose mitigation strategies and plans.

- As partners, the professional and the owner identify risks, prioritize them, and set the mitigation steps or methods.
- The professional supports the mission and strategic plans. They do so by proactively managing and communicating risks to senior management and to owners.
- Result: senior management can take risk into account when managing the organization's priorities.

**Two misconceptions about ESRM:**

| Misconception | Why it is false | The book's nuance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. ESRM is convergence: bringing traditional, corporate, or physical security together with information security/cybersecurity under a single leader | ESRM manages risk whatever the organizational structure. A single command is not needed. There is effective ESRM with different leaders for physical and information security | Converging can make management more agile and less cumbersome, and allocate resources better, because cyber risk is key for everyone. Key to success: that everyone who manages security risks adopts and uses ESRM |
| 2. ESRM is Enterprise Risk Management (ERM) | ERM covers all key risks: financial, operational, and strategic. ESRM is limited to security risks | Not every organization has a working ERM function. ESRM can be a subcomponent of ERM. High-impact security risks can be formally tracked in ERM; not all reach that level. ESRM is an independent risk management system, whether or not there is ERM. Where ERM is well defined, security is usually on its operations committee |

### 4. Benefits of ESRM (1.1.4)

#### 4.1 For the professional and the security function (10)

| # | Benefit |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | The risk decision passes to the asset owner. This brings more resources and more perspectives into risk management. |
| 2 | The professional moves from tactical owner of programs to strategic partner and trusted advisor. They can oversee mitigation processes and systems, supported or directed by the owners. Example: a centralized access control, operated by security, mitigates risks for several owners. |
| 3 | Owners and other managers do not work by programs. With a risk approach, security works like they do and communicates and collaborates better. |
| 4 | The professional better understands the organization, its strategy, and its goals. That improves risk identification and prioritization. |
| 5 | Communication improves with internal and external stakeholders. The professional learns what they consider critical. This way they operate better and refine priorities and mitigation plans. |
| 6 | By focusing on risk, they can be more innovative in solving problems and reducing risks. |
| 7 | By promoting a holistic approach, they add more value. It helps reduce security risk and risk related to it. |
| 8 | Security moves from reactive to proactive. Result: fewer incidents and lower impact when they occur. |
| 9 | Resources are allocated better, because risks are identified and prioritized with the owners. Budgets are more manageable and receive more support when aligned with the owners' goals. |
| 10 | As a partner who understands the business, they are invited into other strategic areas. They gain visibility and value within the organization. |

#### 4.2 For the organization (9)

| # | Benefit |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | It makes it possible to decide on critical security risks at the level of the whole enterprise, supporting mission and objectives. It works for multinationals and for organizations with a single facility. |
| 2 | Owners and stakeholders understand the security function and risk more broadly and consistently. |
| 3 | Senior management sees key risks better, because they are raised by senior managers who are asset owners. |
| 4 | It aligns security resources with the strategy to mitigate prioritized risks. |
| 5 | It identifies early and proactively monitors a wide range of risks. |
| 6 | It improves stakeholders' understanding of and commitment to risk and the security function. |
| 7 | It better supports legal, regulatory, and contractual functions and matters. |
| 8 | It provides a platform to integrate security into the organization's culture. |
| 9 | It strengthens resilience, event response, and crisis management. |

### 6. The ESRM context (1.2.1)

- To mitigate threats, you must understand the threats **and** the organization itself.
- The organization is the environment where assets and risks live. Risks and incidents vary greatly from one organization to another.
- That is why understanding the organization is the initial step of ESRM. It is at least as important as understanding the threats.
- Before implementing ESRM, four aspects must be known: mission and vision, core values, operating environment, and stakeholders.

#### 6.1 Mission and vision

- One of the most critical requirements is to align the security strategy with the organization's overall strategy.
- Mission and vision are the top of the organizational strategy. They must be thoroughly known to detect risks that undermine them.
- Reinforcing this knowledge is often the first step of a new ESRM adoption.

| What to know | What it is for |
| --- | --- |
| The organization's services and products | Basis for engaging with owners and stakeholders, and for identifying and prioritizing risks. The larger the organization, the more complex the task |
| Key personnel, leadership, and operating structure | Moving comfortably around the organization and creating key relationships |
| Legal requirements and regulations, both security-specific and organization-wide | Detecting situations and risks that directly or indirectly affect compliance. Understanding stakeholder positions. Designing mitigations with legal or regulatory impact |
| Strategic plans and long-term goals (the "roadmap") | Supporting management partners. Reviewing goals and strategies looking for general and security risks, and bringing them to leadership |

- Message this approach conveys: security exists to help you achieve your goals, and we want the same thing you do.
- Effect: more stakeholder commitment and less image of security as an obstacle. This makes risk management more effective.

#### 6.2 Core values

- They usually go beyond making money and increasing shareholder value.
- Examples from the book (not a closed list): environmental stewardship, the community, employee safety, product quality, brand and image protection, and new product development.
- ESRM emphasizes partnership, collaboration, and transparency. If the values fit with that, the organization will receive ESRM better.
- Linking ESRM to the values ensures alignment with senior management's priorities. They should be cited in the security strategy and messages.
- Values usually define the culture. A culture that does not support security makes it hard to implement ESRM.

Questions to assess values and culture (6):

- How does the organization handle change?
- What is its risk tolerance or risk appetite?
- Are some business units more open than others to working with security?
- What do employee surveys say about values, culture, and change?
- Is there effective communication with all stakeholders?
- What are the organization's key motivators?

#### 6.3 Operating environment

It serves to assess risks and build relationships. It has three parts:

| Type | What it is | Elements given by the book |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Physical | What influences the traditional security factors | 1) Type of location, building, and surroundings. 2) Pedestrian and vehicle traffic in the area. 3) Required access by non-employees. 4) Industrial control systems. 5) Criticality and sensitivity of processes and assets on site. 6) Products in stock or stored |
| Nonphysical | Factors that influence the professional's owners and partners. They are sources of risk, including security risk | 1) Geopolitical environment. 2) External pressures on the industry. 3) Legal, regulatory, and compliance requirements. 4) Intensity of competition. 5) Growth stage of the organization. 6) Speed required to decide. 7) Impact of technology. 8) Complexity of continuous change, including leadership change |
| Logical | Types of information, digital assets, and the network or digital space that links them to each other and to stakeholders | 1) Servers. 2) Workstations. 3) Network infrastructure. 4) Connectivity. 5) Other endpoints and devices |

- Physical: you must understand how processes relate, the importance of each function, and the entire product or service process. That makes it possible to partner with owners, assess and prioritize risks, and be accepted as a trusted advisor.
- Nonphysical: serves as a reference point to help owners define mitigation options.
- Logical: knowing it is essential, because every type of business depends heavily on digital connection and on data in all its phases.

#### 6.4 Stakeholders

- **Definition:** anyone who interacts directly with the organization. They can affect or be affected by the organization, its assets, or its personnel.
- Knowing what matters to them makes it possible to advise them better and help them design mitigations.

| Stakeholder | Who they are and what matters for ESRM |
| --- | --- |
| Organizational leaders | Management team, board of directors, and anyone who sets strategy. The more ESRM is implemented, the more contact there is with them |
| Asset owners, of tangible and intangible assets | Key partners of the professional; the professional works most closely with them. They must be identified. Some assets have shared or multiple owners |
| Employees, contractors, vendors | Some are key allies. Some are owners of intangible assets: purchasing, human resources, legal, or marketing. Others are partners for mitigating a specific risk of an owner |
| Those who provide knowledge or support | Suppliers, distributors, consultants, and audit firms |
| Industry organizations | Their actions affect the organization and its course. They can generate risks or serve as a mitigation resource |
| Clients and customers | Distributors, resellers, retailers, and direct customers. They pose very varied risk challenges |
| Community | The most forgotten. The organization influences its surroundings, traffic, noise, and economy. There are joint risks, and partnering with the community can reduce them |

**Exam note:** the book's bulleted list has 5 groups: leaders, asset owners, employees/contractors/vendors, customers, and community. The text later adds two more: those who provide knowledge and industry organizations. Also, "distributors" appears in two different groups.

How to build the relationship with an asset owner:

1. If there is already a relationship, the partnership forms more easily.
2. If there is not, directly ask for a short meeting or enter through a "champion."
   - The champion is an asset owner you have already worked with, at the same level as the other owners.
   - They open doors and can be a great advocate for ESRM and for the professional.
3. First meetings: short. They serve to get to know the owner and what matters to them.
4. Follow-up meetings: focused on risk as the owner sees it.
5. Look for opportunities to propose a partnership to manage security risk.

Keys:

- Stakeholder support is critical for adopting ESRM. You must identify them, involve them, understand what matters to them, and align with their priorities.
- Understanding them does **not** mean harmonizing their interests. It means understanding their needs and their view of risk to facilitate the process.

### 7. The ESRM cycle (1.2.2)

- It describes how security risks are mitigated.
- It resembles other industry processes. Its most distinctive feature: understanding assets and involving their owners in risk management.
- **Pending:** figure 1.1 (The ESRM Cycle) has no legible text. Its four processes do appear in the text.

| # | Process | What is done | Who contributes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Identify and prioritize assets | They are prioritized according to their criticality to the mission and strategy. The prioritized list is the basis for the rest of the cycle | Guidance from senior management and input from owners and other stakeholders |
| 2 | Identify and prioritize risks | They are prioritized according to their potential to undermine the ability to fulfill mission and strategy. Prioritized risks and assets form strategic priorities that flow down through the whole cycle and guide the owners | Guidance from senior management and input from other stakeholders |
| 3 | Mitigate prioritized risks | They are mitigated in order of priority, with controls recommended by the professional and approved by the owner. The professional documents assets, risks, and decisions, perhaps in a security risk management plan. That plan is executed by the professional | The owner decides; the professional guides |
| 4 | Continuous improvement | Spans the whole cycle. It arises from ESRM results: better communication, more risk visibility, and effective prioritization and mitigation. Mechanisms: lessons learned and feedback loops | Functions that usually contribute: incident response; investigations and analysis; information sharing |

Key roles in this phase and throughout the cycle:

| Role | Definition | Function in ESRM |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Asset owner | Person most directly responsible for the asset performing well | Is the risk owner. ESRM assigns this responsibility to them because they know the asset best. They are trusted to decide |
| Top management | Highest level of executive leadership. Often the C-suite or an executive committee | Is always above the security leader, for example the CSO or the security director, and does not include them |
| Security professional | Security risk subject matter expert and trusted advisor to owners, top management, and stakeholders | Guides the owner in the security risk decision process |

- ESRM covers all types of assets, for example physical, information, cyber, or personnel. It does not divide disciplines or work in silos.
- Everything within the professional's scope is within the scope of ESRM.

**Exam note:** the asset owner decides and approves. The professional recommends, documents, and executes the plan.

### 8. The ESRM foundation (1.2.3)

Besides understanding the organization, there are four concepts critical for ESRM to last and succeed:

| Concept | Meaning | Key points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Holistic risk management | "Holistic" = inclusive, all-encompassing, complete | It has two senses |
| Partnership with stakeholders | Identify, involve, and align with stakeholders | Collaborative role of advisor and advocate |
| Transparency | Clear, open, and honest approach | Risk transparency and process transparency |
| Governance | Rules and processes by which a function or organization is governed | Organizational governance and ESRM governance |

**Exam note:** the list says "Partnership with stakeholders," but the section heading says "Partner with Stakeholders."

#### 8.1 Holistic risk management: two senses

1. **All stakeholders take part.** All have a role, an interest, and a benefit in good risk management. Especially asset owners and senior management, who are encouraged to participate actively.
2. **It covers all types of security risk without labels or silos:** physical, cyber, information, or personnel. Whatever is within the scope of the security function enters ESRM: one, several, or all of those disciplines. The more ESRM covers, the greater the positive impact.

#### 8.2 Partnership with stakeholders

- ESRM calls for a collaborative and supportive role: the professional is an advisor and advocate for stakeholders. This is key with asset owners and senior management.

| The professional in ESRM… | …instead of… |
| --- | --- |
| Gives guidance | Giving instructions |
| Supports decisions about assets | Making the decisions themselves |
| Speaks of measured risk | Speaking from fear, uncertainty, or doubt |

- They must define and socialize their role with senior management and owners proactively. Otherwise others will define it for them.
- This makes relationships more positive, valuable, and partnership-based.
- Other typical results (4):
  - More stakeholder engagement.
  - More ownership and buy-in.
  - More understanding of security risk.
  - A culture that more strongly supports security priorities.

#### 8.3 Transparency

- It improves culture and staff morale. It makes stakeholders feel like partners in the process.
- Since ESRM relies on partnership, transparency helps those relationships and the strategy work.

| Type | What it requires |
| --- | --- |
| Risk transparency | Presenting risks as they are: neither exaggerated nor minimized. Using objective statements, quantitative analysis, and quantifiable metrics. It reinforces the owner as the risk owner and enables informed and accurate decisions |
| Process transparency | That owners and stakeholders understand the risk management process and each step. In most organizations there is no benefit to keeping it confidential. Sharing it increases commitment and eases defensive, cautious, or anxious stances |

What else to share with owners and stakeholders (5):

- Current and planned security measures.
- The reasons for those measures.
- Other relevant decision makers.
- Other mitigation options that can be considered.
- Final decisions on mitigation.

#### 8.4 Governance

- It helps manage expectations and improves clarity and consistency.
- It ensures that efforts across the organization meet its needs.

| Type | Definition | Details |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Organizational governance | System by which the organization is directed and controlled | Addresses the role of senior executives and the board, audit and oversight, stakeholders' rights and responsibilities, and decision procedures. ESRM governance must align with it |
| ESRM governance | Process of setting enterprise security risk policy and direction, allocating resources, and ensuring compliance | It is a subset of corporate governance and is modeled on organizational governance. It is exercised by a security governing body: committee, council, or other group. Its members should be mostly risk stakeholders, not security professionals |

Documentation framework, a typical result of governance:

| Document | Level | What it does | Typical verb |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Policies | High level | State corporate strategy and direction. They are the organization's "laws" | — |
| Standards | Tactical | Say how to comply with policy. More detailed than policies | "shall" / "must" (mandatory) |
| Guidelines | Tactical, less rigid than standards | Recommend how to meet expectations | "should" / "may" (recommended) |
| Procedures | The most operational | Step-by-step instructions to comply with the documents above | — |

## Capítulo 2 — Introduction to Enterprise Assets Protection (págs. 21–28)

**Central idea:** Assets protection covers people, property, information, and intangibles. With Enterprise Security Risk Management (ESRM), the professional guides and the asset owner decides. The chapter reviews what an asset is, what has changed the profession, and two basic frameworks: the four ways to manage risk and the four Ds.

### 1. The environment of today's professional (chapter introduction)

Factors that influence how the professional works (8):

- Globalization.
- Information technology.
- Instant communications.
- Complex and asymmetric threats.
- Public opinion.
- Mergers and acquisitions.
- Conglomerates and alliances.
- Regulation.

Profile required of them (6):

- Broad security experience.
- Adaptable.
- Strategic thinker.
- Skilled in process management.
- Knowledgeable about their employer's or clients' business.
- Able to act quickly and accurately.

### 2. What assets protection is (2.1.1)

- For many people "assets protection" sounds like finance. In security it has a broader meaning.
- **Traditional view:** three types of assets: people, property, and information.
- **Expanded view:** adds intangibles, such as reputation, relationships, and creditworthiness.
- **With ESRM:** the focus shifts from implementing security processes and programs to guiding asset owners.
  - The owner decides about the risk to their assets and is the ultimate party responsible for security decisions.
  - Security partners help identify and prioritize risks and set mitigation steps and methods.
- In guiding the mitigation of hazards and threats, natural and man-made, security must be seen as value-adding.
- Depending on the mitigation plan, physical, procedural, and electronic measures are combined.
- Owners and professionals must know what to protect. Often owners do not know well what their real assets are.

| Type of asset | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Tangible | Can be seen, touched, or measured directly |
| Intangible | Has no physical form |

List of assets proposed by the book (our own count: 18 tangible and 17 intangible):

| Tangible | Intangible |
| --- | --- |
| People | Intellectual property |
| Land and real estate | Knowledge |
| Infrastructure | Proprietary processes |
| Customer data | Information technology capabilities |
| Current contracts | Credit rating and financial stability |
| Financial investments | Continuity and resilience posture |
| Facilities and buildings | Safety posture |
| Equipment | Reputation and image |
| Tools | Goodwill and trust |
| Inventory | Brand recognition |
| Vehicles | Relationships |
| Raw materials | Supplier diversity |
| Cash and money | Longevity and history |
| Accounts receivable | Expertise |
| Supplies and consumables | Workforce morale |
| Telecommunications systems | Workforce retention |
| Machinery | Human capital development |
| Products |  |

### 3. Relationship with security and other disciplines (2.1.2)

- It is a broad and complex function. Many departments or areas are involved.
- According to ESRM, deciding how to mitigate risk belongs to the owners. But they should not decide in isolation: the professional advises and guides them.
- It includes all security risks and many related functions (6):
  - Investigations.
  - Risk management.
  - Safety.
  - Quality/product assurance.
  - Compliance.
  - Emergency management.
- That is why the senior assets protection manager needs:
  - strong collaboration and coordination skills;
  - a deep understanding of how the business works.
- Current countermeasures must include people, process, and technology.

### 4. Influences on assets protection (2.1.3)

| When | Event | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1970s | Computer security flourishes as a separate discipline | Cause: society depends more and more on information systems |
| Since then | Globalization, civil unrest, natural disasters, and the arrival of the Internet | They change the way the professional works |
| 1993 | Attack on the World Trade Center (New York) | In the United States, attention to critical infrastructure security grows greatly |
| "Two years later" (1995, our own calculation) | Bombing of the Alfred P. Murrah Federal Building (Oklahoma City) | Same as above |
| September 11, 2001 | Terrorist attacks (9/11) | The most important turning point in assets protection worldwide |

**Critical infrastructure:**

- Another major influence was recognizing that it is vulnerable to natural and man-made attacks.
- In the United States it consists of these sectors (16 in the list; our own count):
  - Chemical.
  - Commercial facilities.
  - Communications.
  - Critical manufacturing.
  - Dams.
  - Defense industrial base.
  - Emergency services.
  - Energy.
  - Financial services.
  - Food and agriculture.
  - Government facilities.
  - Healthcare and public health.
  - Information technology.
  - Nuclear.
  - Transportation.
  - Water and wastewater.
- **Fact:** in the United States, most nondefense critical infrastructure is owned or operated by private companies (Cato Institute, 2017).

**Exam note:** the text cites "Cato Institute, 2017." In the references it appears as Edwards, Chris (2017), *Who Owns U.S. Infrastructure?*

**Immediate effects of 9/11 (3):**

- More security budget and fewer obstacles to its policies and procedures.
- More communication between security managers and senior executives (front-office).
- More threat awareness and more vigilance from managers and employees.

**Negative effect of 9/11:**

- Solutions focused on terrorist attacks, not on the broader spectrum of realistic risks.
- Resources were diverted from computer security, information asset protection, and traditional crime and loss prevention.
- Those resources went to antiterrorism measures: blast-resistant materials, stand-off zones, bollards, chemical/biological sensors, and similar items.

**Beneficial long-term changes (7).** Over time, 9/11 partly redefined assets protection:

- It changed what the public expects, and increased the level of security measures it tolerates.
- Ongoing review of personal privacy versus public protection.
- More serious study of security budgets and strategies and protection services.
- Better information sharing within and between the security and law enforcement communities. The fight against crime improved.
- More use of advanced technologies in threat analysis, vulnerability assessment, information sharing, and protective measures.
- More debate on strategic protection concepts with ESRM principles.
- More emphasis on research on security and assets protection.

### 5. Patterns of change (2.1.4)

- The professional must be prepared for rapid changes at work.
- Example: the global pandemic of 2019 and 2020 brought challenges in remote work, cybersecurity, physical protection of facilities, and worker safety.

Paradigm shifts in assets protection (10):

- Surveillance technology.
- Integrated security systems.
- Scope of the security professional's functions.
- Legal and liability issues.
- Regulatory environment.
- Use of computers in the security function.
- Public-private partnerships.
- Antiterrorism.
- Convergence.
- Global business relationships.

**The most important change:** assets protection is now managed with the principles of ESRM. It is a process or methodology for managing risks, instead of programs.

**Definition of risk** (ESRM Guideline, ASIS International, 2019): "the effect of uncertainty on the achievement of strategic, tactical, and operational objectives." That is, the effect of uncertainty on the achievement of strategic, tactical, and operational objectives.

- This definition fits assets protection.

## Capítulo 3 — Theft and Fraud Prevention in the Workplace (págs. 29–48)

**Central idea:** Employee theft and fraud are among an organization's greatest threats. ### 1. Definitions and basic principles

### 2. General characteristics of employee theft and fraud

**General observations:**

### 4. Motivation to Steal and Defraud: Theories

**4.1 Context**

- Psychologists, sociologists, and criminologists have looked for the traits and the social and environmental forces that explain dishonesty.
- Research historically focused on street and violent crime. The white-collar offender was studied later.
- The Enron, Global Crossing, and ImClone scandals made executive greed and employee theft more visible.
- Workplace theft received academic attention in the mid-19th century. It was then ignored until the early 1980s.

**4.2 Clark and Hollinger (1982)**

- John Clark and Richard Hollinger, of the Department of Sociology at the University of Minnesota. A 3-year study.
- They studied three private sectors: retail, manufacturing, and hospitals.
- They reviewed the literature of criminology, sociology, psychology, anthropology, and industrial security.
- They found 5 groups of hypotheses, separate but related:

| Hypothesis | What it holds | What the study concluded or contributed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. External economic pressures | It was the most cited justification before the study: people steal because of personal problems such as alcohol, gambling, illicit affairs, or the like. | The link between economic need and the way the stolen goods meet it had never been demonstrated. It was vague, at best. |
| 2. Youth and work | Young people would be less honest and hardworking than earlier generations. Two studies of retailers found a disproportionate share of recently hired young people among those caught. | There was no clear and convincing evidence. |
| 3. Opportunity | Security professionals believed opportunity was the main factor. Every employee would be tempted at some point, depending on opportunity. | It was not studied empirically until Clark and Hollinger's later research, in 1983. |
| 4. Job dissatisfaction | The employer causes the theft, because management is responsible, directly or indirectly, for the dissatisfaction. | It was hardly included in earlier studies. Clark and Hollinger incorporated it. |
| 5. Social control | The social structure, formal and informal, shared within the organization strongly influences whether theft occurs. Work group norms and the supervisor-employee relationship carry weight and can deter or encourage theft. | It was not tested empirically until their study. |

- **Exam note:** the book dates the study to 1982, but places the research on opportunity in 1983.
- **Deterrence doctrine:** the threat of negative social sanctions or criminal prosecution influences how much is stolen. More is stolen when little threat of detection or punishment is perceived.
- The book says that "both theories" resemble the deterrence doctrine. **Pending:** the text does not clarify which two. From context, they appear to be the role of group norms and the supervisor-employee relationship.
- **Production deviance:** Clark and Hollinger found it difficult to separate theft from other deviant behaviors. Examples:
  - Unauthorized or extended coffee or meal breaks.
  - Misuse of sick days.
  - Punching the time card for another employee.
  - Arriving late or leaving early.
- By current standards, all of that is theft of time.

### 5. Fraud and Related Crimes

**5.1 Common Elements of Fraud (Wells, 1997)**

- Joseph Wells, *Occupational Fraud and Abuse* (1997). The ACFE study confirms this basis.
- Three factors present in every fraud:
  1. A strong financial pressure.
  2. An opportunity to commit it.
  3. A means of justifying it as appropriate.
- If all three coincide in almost any work situation, fraud is likely.
- Key difference: workplace theft is usually spontaneous; fraud is premeditated.
- Sources of financial pressure according to Wells:
  - Gambling debts.
  - Drug use.
  - Living beyond one's means.
  - Unexpected medical expenses.
  - The desire to succeed or to appear successful.
- Predominant factor: greed.

**5.2 How Fraud Is Proved** The factfinder must demonstrate these 5 points:

1. The perpetrator misrepresented or concealed a material fact.
2. The perpetrator knew that what was asserted was false.
3. The perpetrator intended for the victim to rely on that falsehood.
4. The victim relied on the misrepresentation.
5. The victim suffered harm as a result of relying on it.

**5.3 Symptoms and Indicators of Fraud (Red Flags)**

- Theft is evident because something disappears. Fraud and embezzlement leave only symptoms or indicators.
- The opportunity for fraud generally arises from absent or weak internal controls.
- Knowing the situational pressures and the symptoms helps the security professional to prevent fraud.
- Four categories of warning signs:

**a) Employee situational red flags, 8:**

1. High personal debts. Examples from the book: medical, gambling, or from excessive stock market speculation.
2. Poor credit history or other financial difficulties.
3. Living beyond one's means.
4. Excessive use of alcohol or drugs.
5. Perceived inequities. Examples from the book: not being promoted, low pay, pressure to meet unrealistic goals.
6. Prior convictions for fraud or for related trust violations.
7. Low moral character.
8. Compulsive behavior.

**b) Employee opportunity red flags, 5:**

**c) Organization situational red flags, 10:**

2. Heavily aged or excessive inventories.
3. Very rapid expansion of the business in general or of some line.
4. Always operating in crisis mode.
5. Unrealistic sales quotas or revenue goals.
6. Significant cash flow problems.
7. History of corruption in the company's industry.
8. Strong competition from other companies.
9. Company products or services becoming obsolete.
10. High turnover in key financial positions.

2. Unethical management role models.

**6.1 Moving from Reactive to Proactive** Process that most companies follow, almost by default, when they detect losses (Albrecht, 1994):

1. A theft or fraud incident is discovered.
2. Investigative resources are identified and the investigation begins.
3. Action is taken based on the results.

### 7. Response Strategy (McCormack, 2018)

After an internal threat or a fraud, companies may overlook key lessons about the origin of the theft. McCormack proposes a detailed 5-step strategy:

1. **Form a quick reaction team.** It includes security, information technology, legal, human resources, and corporate communications. It handles the immediate, for example revoking access credentials.
2. **Create a cross-divisional team to establish the facts.** The departments with information share knowledge and evidence. It may meet several times.
3. **Investigate why the theft occurred.** Understanding the motive makes it possible to improve defenses. Example: if the employee stole because they earned much less than their peers, review payroll to detect others who are underpaid.
4. **Conduct a root cause analysis.** Classify each factor as a failure of technology, process, or people. A factor can fall into more than one category.
5. **Develop a remediation plan.** Take measures so it does not recur and document them.

### 9. Dangers of Undetected Theft and Fraud

- The direct loss is not the only consequence.
- Organizations become complacent about ongoing losses and even build them into their standards.
- Example: setting an allowable negative variance between the book count and the physical count.
  - Losses from theft or fraud hide inside that variance and are not discovered.
  - Over time the variance grows and allows large accumulated losses.
- If theft goes undetected, the company cannot recover it through insurance or deduct it for taxes.
- Losses damage employee morale, shareholder value, and public trust.
- Because of perceived pressures and the ease of rationalization, losses will remain significant.
- Organizations without a comprehensive program will suffer even greater losses.
- Role of the security professional: to give priority to theft and fraud prevention within their loss prevention strategy.

## Capítulo 4 — Cost-Effectiveness and Loss Reporting (págs. 49–66)

**Central idea:** Asset protection must be cost-effective and demonstrable in monetary terms. The chapter teaches how to quantify losses avoided, calculate return on investment (ROI), use and present metrics, write a business case in 8 steps, and use incident reports to predict and prevent losses.

### 1. The Problem

- Asset protection must be cost-effective: you do not spend $1,000 to protect a $10 asset.
- Exception: certain high-value and irreplaceable assets.
- Outside that exception, the protection strategy must have a realistic and cost-effective logic.
- As the profession matures, four business tactics enter security management:
  1. Return on investment (ROI) strategies.
  2. Metrics management.
  3. Data capture and analysis.
  4. Cost-benefit analysis.

### 2. What Cost-Effectiveness Means

- **Definition:** producing good results for the money spent.
- For senior management it is a primary strategic factor.
- Anecdotal evidence is interesting, but the activity must be measurable in financial terms.
- To maximize it, the security manager must:
  1. Operate in the least costly way that remains effective.
  2. Keep costs as low as is consistent with the required operating results.
  3. Ensure that the money spent generates the greatest return.
- It also requires:
  - Balancing spending and results, and reviewing the plan when necessary.
  - Critical judgment based on a complete knowledge of the company's operations.
  - A broad knowledge of the state of the art in security.
  - Accepting that some elements of the program will take several years to implement.
- Procedural controls are the cheapest countermeasures and are often forgotten. Changing how things are done improves security and the bottom line.
- Historical and persistent problem: being unable to demonstrate that spending on protection achieves tangible and more valuable goals. That is, not knowing how to justify its cost to management.

**2.1 Elements of Cost-Effectiveness**

Question asked by senior management and business unit owners: does asset protection achieve something quantifiable that justifies its cost?

| Element | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Major loss prevention | Protection is justified if it is shown that the probable actual losses would not occur with the proposed measures. Here, "cost avoidance" is the total cost of the probable losses that are assumed to have been prevented. |
| Other loss prevention | Losses that are almost never quantified. Example: security rounds detect and correct maintenance or cleaning problems and, at the same time, prevent risks such as fires. |

- **Real test of major loss prevention:**
  - That actual losses are lower than probable ones.
  - That the sum of actual losses plus the cost of protection stays within the risk-assumption boundaries that management accepted when approving the program.

**Typical findings by officers on their rounds (7):**

1. Expensive tools or materials not securely stored.
2. Lights improperly on or off.
3. Machines improperly running or stopped.
4. Doors or hatches improperly open or closed.
5. Temperature too high or too low.
6. Pressure too high or too low.
7. Levels too high or too low.

- In facilities with a security force, the officer usually corrects the problem during the round.

**Example of the light left on:**

- A motion sensor that turns off the light after a period without movement would solve it.
- If that technology is not available, the value of having the round turn off the light is measured with 3 questions:
  1. Without the round, how long would it have stayed on until someone else saw it?
  2. How much does it cost the company to have a light of that wattage on for an hour or a shift?
  3. What cost was avoided by turning it off? → result of question 1 × result of question 2.
- One light for one hour is insignificant. Hundreds of lights for thousands of hours a year add up to significant savings.
- The same applies to shutting off machinery, with an extra benefit: less wear.
- There may be consequences more serious than the energy expense. If the temperature is too high or too low, a process can fail or a vessel can burst.
- Each loss of this type is minor on its own. In a large facility, the cumulative effect is significant and must be documented.

### 3. Quantifying Loss Prevention

**Example of the $250 million company:**

| Data | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Annual gross sales | $250 million |
| Annual cost of asset protection | $1 million |
| Weight of security relative to sales | 0.4% |
| Alternative: invest that $1 million in financial instruments at 4.5% annually | $45,000 in one year |
| Real cost of the security program to the company | $1,045,000 per year |

- Management will want to know why spend $1 million on security and not on something else, even a short-term financial investment.
- That real cost must be compared with the consequences of having no security program.

**Cost-effectiveness within the department itself:**

- The expense budget assigns monetary value to each activity.
- The manager must ask whether each resource is the most effective available at that cost.
- Example: $30 padlocks to secure loaded semitrailers in the yard. Two questions:
  1. Is a padlock the right countermeasure?
  2. If it is, is this $30 padlock the most suitable one?
- In general, the second question is harder to answer than the first.

**Financial view and budget:**

- Senior management looks at all operations through a financial lens. A department that directly generates revenue is a profit center.
- A security professional without this perspective will not be able to justify the continuation of the program, especially in times of austerity.
- Three main categories of expense that security must review with business unit owners when budgeting:
  1. Salaries.
  2. Operational expenses.
  3. Capital expenditures.
- Essential step: review the organization's overall strategy and goals. Is the security budget aligned with, or does it exceed, what is realistic and acceptable to management?
- Many necessary programs are cut because no one presents a persuasive argument in the competition for scarce funds.
- The losses that follow a security cut can far exceed the supposed savings.

### 4. Return on Investment (ROI)

**4.1 Definition and Use**

- A performance measure used to evaluate the efficiency of an investment or to compare several.
- It directly measures the return on an investment relative to its cost.
- It is popular because it is versatile and simple. It serves as a basic indicator of profitability.
- Examples of use: a stock, the expansion of a factory, or a real estate transaction.
- If the ROI is net positive, the investment is probably worthwhile. If there are options with a higher ROI, it helps to choose or rule out.
- In short, it answers "is all this worth it?".

**Formula:** ROI = (current value of the investment − cost of the investment) ÷ cost of the investment

- **Exam note:** the book writes the formula without parentheses ("current value – cost ÷ cost"). Taken literally it would be incorrect. The correct way is to subtract first and then divide.

**Limitations:**

- It is not as useful over long time frames.
- It has limits in capital budgeting, where cash flows and periodic returns matter.
- For more detail, the book refers to *POA (Protection of Assets): Business Principles*, ASIS International, 2021.

**4.2 Three Calculation Examples**

| Case | Cost of the solution | Benefit or loss avoided | Result according to the book |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nuisance fire alarms | $10,000 per year | $40,000 per year ($50,000 → $10,000) | For every $1 invested, $4 is saved |
| Two-way radios | $60,000 capital investment | $25,000 per year | $0.41 for every $1 in one year; $4.10 for every $1 over 10 years |
| Consolidating monitoring centers | $225,000 for installation and training + $25,000 per year | Losses of $500,000 → $250,000 (−50%) | Year 1: total cost "about the same"; afterward, substantial savings |

**Case 1: nuisance fire alarms**

- Data from the security department's incident reports were analyzed.
- Hard costs:
  - Lost productivity of employees who evacuate.
  - Lost productivity of employees who respond to the alarms.
  - Fire department fines.
- Soft costs:
  - Wear on the building's mechanical systems when the alarms are activated.
  - Employees who learn to ignore the alarms and put themselves at risk during a real one.
  - Possible staff injuries during evacuations.
  - Frustration of staff and firefighters over so many alarms.
- Lost productivity was calculated with the average hourly wage provided by human resources. The fines were easily added up. The soft costs were only estimated.
- Three causes:
  1. Old equipment.
  2. Lack of training and of familiarity with the alarm system.
  3. Lack of communication between staff and building contractors.
- Solutions, one per cause: replacement parts, a training program, and a formal communication program.
- Result: $50,000 in year 1 and $10,000 in year 2. Loss avoided: $40,000. Annual cost of the initiative: $10,000.
- **Exam note:** "$4 for every $1" is the benefit/cost ratio (40,000 ÷ 10,000). With the book's own ROI formula it would be (40,000 − 10,000) ÷ 10,000 = 3, that is, 300%.

**Case 2: two-way radios**

- The company used a trunk cellular radio system with dead zones in the building.
- Consequences:
  - Time lost leaving the dead zones.
  - More risk for personnel outside coverage.
  - Late responses to security, civil protection, and medical incidents.
- Hard costs: $25,000 per year in airtime. The soft costs, hard to quantify, were left out.
- Losses avoided projected with a new system: possible lawsuits and workers' compensation claims. They were estimated at $25,000 per year.
- The useful life of the solution changes the ROI. One method is to multiply the savings by the years the original system would have remained in use. Example: if it had 10 years left, the annual savings are multiplied by 10.
- Cost of the new system (purchase and installation): $60,000.
- In addition, the other problems for staff and tenants were resolved, such as delays in urgent incidents.
- **Exam note:** the figures do not quite add up.
  - 25,000 ÷ 60,000 = 0.4167. The book truncates it to $0.41; rounded it would be $0.42.
  - Over 10 years, 250,000 ÷ 60,000 = 4.17. The book gives $4.10 because it multiplies the truncated 0.41.
  - It is a benefit/cost ratio, not the ROI formula. With the formula, year 1 gives (25,000 − 60,000) ÷ 60,000 = −0.58.
  - The calculation uses only the $25,000 of losses avoided. It does not clarify whether the $25,000 of airtime is also saved.

**Case 3: consolidating security monitoring centers**

- The previous cases are simple evaluations of a single element. A plan that affects the entire organization involves many intangible factors.
- Case: a global manufacturer wants to save money by merging its 5 regional monitoring centers into a single global center.
- Advantages:
  - Do more with less: more efficient and effective operation.
  - A smaller, more cohesive team.
  - Force multiplier: immediate flow of information and immediate response.
  - Active participation in business continuity.
  - A unified way of thinking about security, monitoring, and business operations.
- Factors to explain and discuss with decision makers (7):
  1. Potential deterrent effect.
  2. Displacement.
  3. System design.
  4. Installation.
  5. Streamlining of costs.
  6. Increased productivity.
  7. Better communication and coordination with public first responders.
- These factors can save money by reducing staff and budgets, while improving performance.
- The ROI explanation must answer: "what do they get for their money?".
  - Include the approximate number of investigation cases, policy violations, or additional security problems that the center would detect.
  - Example: employee theft cases that adding one operator to the center would generate.
  - If what is recovered (money, goods, or restitution) equals or exceeds the salary of the new position, approval is likely.
  - Do not exaggerate the results: the manager will be held accountable for that forecast in the next budget.
- **Deterrent effect:** to deter, the punishment must be swift, certain, and severe. If one is missing, the effect drops.
  - Process in a proactive center: the theft or violation is observed and recorded; the offender is identified; they are interviewed; human resources applies the discipline.
  - When it is known that misconduct is dealt with quickly, others refrain.
- **Displacement effect:** when expanding the center's scope, overt cameras and signage are placed on perimeters, parking lots, and facilities.
  - The location is decided with the center's own data.
  - Criminal activity is displaced to less secure properties and business continuity is supported.
- Savings: going from staffing 5 centers to a single one, and ceasing to operate, equip, and maintain the regional centers.
- Those savings are offset by the cost of the new center. Items to review (6):

| Item | What it includes |
| --- | --- |
| Design | Plans, type of system, and components: monitors, servers, computers, intrusion detection devices, sensors, detectors, card readers, cameras, conduit, wiring, and consultants. |
| Installation | One of the most expensive items. Permits, cost, and installation of sensors, contacts, and access control devices. |
| Operation | Staff, training, policies, and procedures. |
| IT expenses | Antivirus, system patches, backup and archiving, and database management. |
| Maintenance | Software updates, emergency repairs, cleaning of camera domes, and overall proper functioning. |
| Replacement | Before buying, define the life cycle and calculate the approximate replacement cost. It is presented by assigning a cost and an expected life range to each element. |

- Every organization is unique; there is no one-size-fits-all solution.
- **Hypothetical example from the book:**
  - Estimated annual cost of false claims, lawsuits, false alarm activations, crimes, and other negative events: $500,000.
  - System installation and staff training: $225,000.
  - Equipment maintenance and training, each year: $25,000.
  - In year 1 those costs drop to $250,000, a 50% reduction.
  - Adding the year 1 installation cost, the total cost turns out to be "about the same". With the book's figures: 250,000 + 225,000 + 25,000 = 500,000.
  - In subsequent years the savings grow substantially, because only maintenance and training remain.

### 5. How to Gather and Report Security Metrics

- **Definition:** "security metrics" are measurements related to security.
- Kovacich and Halibozek (2016) describe them as the process of measuring the costs and benefits of an asset protection program, and its successes and failures.
- Security budgets are being scrutinized as never before; metrics help justify the spending.
- First step of good planning: analyze the areas of potential loss, their probability, and their severity or impact on the company.
- That analysis, together with the metrics, provides the information to present the budget to senior management.
- Security management was slow to adopt metrics, but that has changed. With them, cost-effectiveness is better demonstrated.

**Examples of metrics by type of operation (those given by the book):**

| Type of operation | Metrics |
| --- | --- |
| Loss prevention program | Apprehensions, recoveries per year, recoveries per officer, apprehensions per shift, and apprehensions per location |
| Commercial high-rise | Number of thefts, cost per square foot, fire alarms per year, number of incidents, doors found open, number of undesirable persons, recoveries and investigations conducted |
| Shopping mall | Apprehensions, persons banned from the property, interactions with the public, prevention seminars with the stores, rounds conducted, and cars stolen from the parking lot |
| Corporate security | Investigations conducted, recoveries, risk assessments conducted, and travel briefings given to staff |

- With baseline data, the manager can experiment and adjust the program to make it more effective.
- The analysis can also show whether a specific measure works or not.
- Each manager decides what to measure. Metrics help them answer 9 questions:
  1. What am I trying to achieve?
  2. How will I know if I am successful?
  3. What would convince me that I am not?
  4. What are my obstacles to success?
  5. How much does each unit of success cost?
  6. Is the cost worth it?
  7. How will I collect and display the information in a useful way?
  8. What is the cost of success?
  9. What is the cost of failure?
- Security competes with other departments for funds.
  - In engineering it is easy: if a piece of equipment will fail without repair or replacement, the expense is approved.
  - Security and marketing have a harder time and must use metrics to persuade.

### 6. Presenting Metrics to Senior Management

- Source: ASIS Foundation report *Persuading Senior Management with Effective, Evaluated Security Metrics* (ASIS, 2014).
- Basic rule: use metrics aligned with the organization's objectives or with the topics that interest management most.
- It is critical to focus them on the organization's risks and objectives and on what matters to executives, above all ROI.
- 4 other recommendations:

| Recommendation | Content |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Present metrics that meet measurement standards | Figures seem scientific. If they are based on invalid or unreliable data, the conclusions fail and lose external credibility. A well-designed metric evaluated with statistical criteria is more persuasive. |
| 2. Tell a story | In prevention metrics: name the threatened resources, their value, and the consequences if the event occurs. Show how events evolve over time and progress toward a strategic goal. Incident management software speeds up trend analysis. Comparison with other organizations (benchmarking) enriches, but is less common because it depends on others sharing data. |
| 3. Use graphics and be brief | Less is more: management cares about few metrics. If the professional uses a dashboard, they should make an even simpler one for management. Presentation of 5 minutes or less; summarize findings and avoid trivia. |
| 4. Present metrics regularly | Research does not indicate an optimal interval. But data ages and becomes historical, less actionable and less useful. It is worth distinguishing time-sensitive metrics from those that provide value over time. |

### 7. Report Writing: The Business Case

- For incident reports, the book refers to *POA: Personnel*. For investigation reports, to *POA: Investigations*.
- This section deals with how to promote security to senior management through a business case. Source: ASIS 2013 (*Best Practices for Developing and Promoting Your Security Business Case*).

**7.1 Promoting the Business Case**

- It is commonly believed that writing the documents is enough. But approval is much more likely if security has a good reputation and has been actively promoted throughout the structure.
- Under the principles of ESRM (Enterprise Security Risk Management), the professional sees themselves as a business partner.
- They cannot assume that decision makers already know the value of security, either in money or as a contribution to a safe environment.
- Their job is to ally with the different departments and functions so they understand that value.
- Security is increasingly complex and expensive, while budgets shrink.
- To obtain support, the professional must:
  - Understand the different departments and have a holistic view of the company.
  - Communicate skillfully to sell security as a partner that contributes to the mission.
- A rational business case is no longer enough. Supporting conversations and interactions are needed.

**Making security a priority:**

- The most important aspect of the business case is its alignment with the organization's goals.
- It must be realistic and have clear implementation timelines.
- It must consider external factors that could delay it. Doing so from the start makes it easier to report to management over the long term.
- It must include provisions for continuous improvement.

### 8. How to Write a Business Case in 8 Steps

- Many companies have a formal process for requesting capital funds or resources. The business case justifies those resources.
- It gathers all the relevant facts into a coherent story: what, when, where, how, and why.

| Step | Key content |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Executive summary | First section. Brief, but it tells the whole story, because it may be the only thing some executives read. |
| 2. Project description | Details the need, problem, or business issue. It shapes everything else. |
| 3. Business impact | Which functions or processes will be affected and what extra resources are needed. |
| 4. Justification | Why implement the project and why it was chosen over other alternatives. |
| 5. Cost-benefit analysis | One of the most important parts. Compares costs with benefits and savings. |
| 6. Alternatives and analysis | Options to the proposed project and analysis to choose the preferred one. |
| 7. Recommendation | Closes the case and summarizes how the project will solve the problem. |
| 8. Approvals | Next steps if approved and what happens if it is rejected. |

**Step 1, executive summary:**

- Gives general information about the problem or project, drawn from the details of the longer sections.
- Briefly describes the business problem that the project addresses.
- Highlights the benefits and how it aligns with the organization's goals and objectives.
- Summarizes the detail of the following sections.
- It is the chance to win over the decision makers; it is written with the audience in mind.
- A security investment can yield two types of benefit: a better security picture and a better financial picture.
- Knowing how to quantify the investment and its return as the CFO (Chief Financial Officer) expects can be decisive, especially in hard economic times.

**Step 2, project description:**

- Includes goals and objectives, performance criteria, assumptions, constraints, and milestones.
- Includes the expected benefits of investing the organization's resources.
- Clearly states the problem, the issues to be resolved, and the solution.
- If the description is well stated, it largely determines the outcome of the case.
- Two components of the decision: value and priority.
- Underlying executive question: "is it worth it?". Answering it requires understanding what the project will achieve and why it matters.

**Step 3, business impact:**

- How it will change the organization's processes, hardware, or software.
- Additional resources needed: staff, training, or other expenses.
- Impact of approving the case across the whole company.
- Which services would be affected and what additional risks there are if it is not accepted.
- The case competes for limited resources with other worthy causes.
- The advantages must demonstrate security's value proposition and benefit the company.
- Costs and advantages oppose each other. For the case to succeed, the advantages must prevail.

**Step 4, justification:**

- Provide quantitative support where applicable.
- Set out the impact of not doing the project, with the costs and risks of inaction.
- The justification is derived from this.

**Step 5, cost-benefit analysis:**

- Historically, companies saw security as a necessary expense to reduce risk and avoid losses. They must be helped to see its financial value.
- Quantify the financial benefits, normally with a cost-benefit analysis.
- Purpose: to show the costs, compare them with benefits and savings, and decide whether it is worth it.
- A well-documented ROI case is a powerful tool for competing for resources. ROI helps prioritize investment.
- Nonfinancial benefits and costs are often left out because they are hard to quantify. Examples from the book: "improved job security" and "damage or loss of intellectual property".
- Cash flow statements and other budgeting tools do not see them, but they are real. Intangibles such as "shorter customer wait time" or "better parking for employees" should be highlighted.
- Intangibles that influence the case without affecting cost (7):
  1. Corporate culture.
  2. Employee morale and retention.
  3. Greater awareness.
  4. Process improvements.
  5. Risk mitigation.
  6. Worker productivity.
  7. Workplace safety.

**Step 6, alternatives and analysis:**

- Key questions:
  - How will we get there? Viable options with their costs, plus the evaluation criteria for recommending.
  - Which is the best option? It can be answered with a financial evaluation of funding, affordability, and the balance of costs against benefits and risks.
- The analysis and recommendation phases are "the heart" of the business case.
- The analysis also helps management prioritize this project against others requesting capital and resources.

**Step 7, recommendation:**

- Usual options:
  1. Full adoption of the proposal.
  2. An intermediate or blended option.
  3. The "do nothing" option.
- Security can offer as many options as it needs, including a phased approach due to budget or staffing limits.
- The recommendation must be clear and concise.
- It must explain how the desired results will be achieved.
- It must be supported by evidence from the previous sections.
- The conclusions refer back to the business impact and the justification, and highlight the advantages of the analysis.

**Step 8, approvals:**

- Details the next steps if approved and the outcome if rejected.
- **Exam note:** the book calls three different things "the most important". Alignment with goals is "the most important aspect". Cost-benefit analysis is "one of the most important parts". Analysis and recommendation are "the heart" of the case. Answer according to what the question asks.

### 9. Predictive Modeling by the Security Organization

- The ultimate value of incident reports lies in avoiding future incidents, events, and losses. This is achieved through planning, awareness training, and security improvements.
- Categories to track (9):
  1. Most vulnerable assets, such as those with frequent losses.
  2. When the loss occurs.
  3. Locations of the losses, especially high-frequency ones.
  4. Useful or ineffective countermeasures.
  5. Highest-cost losses.
  6. Types of incidents.
  7. Slips, falls, and other incidents that expose the company to lawsuits.
  8. Health and safety violations that cause lost time, lower productivity, and higher workers' compensation premiums.
  9. Any type of incident that costs time, effort, or money.
- With this information, protection resources are allocated cost-effectively.
- **Warehouse example:**
  - The reports show constant losses of small, high-value items, and no significant loss of the rest.
  - Limited precautions for those items are enough: a chain-link cage with a reliable lock and interior space alarms.
  - With those data, other typical measures could be dispensed with: intrusion alarms on doors and windows, officer rounds, and closed-circuit television (CCTV).
- The selection of countermeasures also depends on ROI. Each one is weighed according to:
  - Probability of preventing losses.
  - Cost of implementation.
  - Potential recoveries.
  - Value of the losses avoided.

**9.1 Pilot Verifications of the Model**

- It is advisable to test the program with a pilot.
- Method: protect some exposure points and leave others unprotected that have the same probability of loss.
- During a controlled period, actual losses in the unprotected areas are measured. Example: careful inventories or counts, without applying measures even if there are losses.
- The losses in the unprotected areas are compared with those in the protected ones to measure the effectiveness of the countermeasures.
- If the pilot's losses are unacceptable, the test is reduced or suspended and the countermeasures are applied across the entire company immediately.
- With the pilot data, the countermeasures are adjusted.

**9.2 Adjustments to a Growing Database**

- Building an incident database takes time.
- When loading incidents, it may be necessary to modify the classifications.
- Security management must be flexible, but review the data periodically.
- Rule from the book: if 80% of each month's reports fall into the "other" category, new categories must be created.

**Chapter close:**

- A cost-effective program considers the major incidents it seeks to prevent. It also considers incidental costs avoided and recoveries of assets or value during the operation.
- What moves management to approve is that the proposed spending is reasonable against the losses that could occur.
- To maintain its support, continuous evidence of losses avoided is needed.
- Reporting cost-effectiveness requires a reliable database, created and maintained with an enterprisewide loss reporting system.
- With ROI and other formulas, it is easier to justify security spending.

## Capítulo 5 — Information Asset Protection (págs. 67–94)

**Central idea:** Information Asset Protection (IAP) is another name for information security. It must be risk-based, using the Enterprise Security Risk Management (ESRM) approach. It must support the business and combine security measures with legal measures. The program seeks to prevent losses, but must also respond and recover when they occur.

### 1. Information Assets: Definition and Forms (5.1)

- **Information assets.** They include:
  - sensitive and proprietary information;
  - privacy-protected data;
  - intellectual property;
  - intangible assets;
  - information defined by international, federal, and state laws on trade secrets, patents, and copyrights.
- Concrete examples: scientific knowledge, branding, reputation, and business processes.

**Forms in which information exists:**

| # | Form |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | A person's knowledge |
| 2 | Spoken information |
| 3 | A process or procedure |
| 4 | Information written on paper |
| 5 | An object: model, prototype, device, or machine |
| 6 | Data in an IT (information technology) or communications system |
| 7 | An electronic transmission |
| 8 | Data and electronic media on the company network, on individual devices (smartphones, computers), and in removable or cloud storage |

- **IAP = information security.**
- What the chapter aims to enable the professional to do:
  1. Formulate an information security program.
  2. Apply a risk-based mitigation strategy.
  3. Identify protection gaps and their solutions.
- All of this while helping the organization's mission, not hindering it.

### 2. History of Espionage and Business Intelligence Collection (5.1.1)

| Era | Event | What it teaches |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1940s | Spies sought U.S. nuclear secrets (Manhattan Project). According to the U.S. Department of Energy (2007), Soviet espionage probably advanced the Soviet bomb by at least 12 to 18 months. | Information theft has strategic impact. |
| Cold War | Espionage in the national and commercial sectors. Target: dual-use technologies, with military and commercial application. Main threat to the West: the KGB (Soviet intelligence service) and the GRU (its military counterpart). | Dual-use technology is a priority target. |
| Cold War | The KGB's Line X (Directorate of Science and Technology) and Eastern European agencies attacked private companies, government agencies, and universities. | Espionage is not limited to government. |
| 1970s | The FBI (Federal Bureau of Investigation) creates the DECA program (Developing Espionage and Counterintelligence Awareness). | Educate industry and ask it to report suspicious activity. |
| Evolution | DECA → ANSIR (Awareness of National Security Issues and Response) → Counterintelligence Domain Program → today the Counterintelligence Strategic Partnerships Program. | FBI liaison with the private sector to protect U.S. competitiveness. |
| 1996 | Economic Espionage Act (EEA). | Stealing trade secrets becomes a federal crime. The FBI receives authority to investigate economic espionage. |

- DECA sought to mitigate espionage, open-source collection, and aggressive targeting.
- DECA was aimed mainly at cleared defense contractors.
- The EEA was drafted with input from ASIS International and its Safeguarding Proprietary Information Council.
- That council is now called the Intellectual Property Protection Community.
- Espionage technology has advanced greatly. The tactics for protecting information remain almost the same.
- **Exam note:** the text cites the Department of Energy with the year 2007. In the references it appears undated ("N.D.").

### 3. ESRM Approach Applied to IAP (5.2)

Every organization has information assets to protect. That protection must be based on risk management. ESRM (see Chapter 1) is applied in four steps:

1. Identify and prioritize assets.
2. Identify and prioritize risks.
3. Mitigate the prioritized risks.
4. Continuously improve the security program.

- The information protection strategy must support the organization's goals, strategy, and timelines (ASIS 2019).

### 4. Categories of Threats (5.3)

| Category | What it is / how it is assessed | Key points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intentional threats | Historically, the most attended to. Adversaries are identified and their capability and intention to attack key assets are assessed. | Think broadly. Adversaries: foreign and domestic competitors, foreign governments, activist groups, terrorist groups, criminal enterprises, information brokers, and vandals. |
| Natural threats | Natural disasters. | Many companies disappear because they lose their information, not their facilities. They lacked a preparedness plan within their business continuity plan. That plan includes off-site backup of critical data and warm or hot sites. |
| Inadvertent threats | Unintentional errors. | Perhaps the most ignored. Also the hardest to identify and assess. They cannot be ignored. |

- Companies that fail after a disaster are usually small, single-site, without a robust security and risk function.
- All entities, large and small, must prepare for natural threats.
- For cyber threats, including nation-state actors, the book refers to Chapter 6 of Protection of Assets (POA): Crisis Management.

**Inadvertent threats — Winkler (2005):**

- The greatest losses come not from terrorists or hackers, but from "the people in the mirror".
- Human errors and accidents cause losses of hundreds of billions of dollars a year.
- If not anticipated, they can destroy a life or a company.
- **Causes** given by the book:
  1. Insufficient employee training.
  2. Misunderstandings.
  3. Lack of attention to detail.
  4. Lax security enforcement.
  5. Pressure to deliver a product or deliverable.
  6. Insufficient staff.
  7. Other factors (the book leaves the list open).
- Remedy: develop, disseminate, and enforce practical IAP policies.

#### 4.1 Noteworthy Threats (5.3.4)

They are considered when conducting risk assessments or designing mitigations, because they are frequent or trend-setting.

| Threat | Definition | What is examinable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Data mining | Software collection of data from open sources and public information. Source: Trends in Proprietary Information Loss (ASIS Foundation 2007). | Governments and companies use automatic tools to collect, filter, organize, analyze, and disseminate information. Effects: information brokers grow and university courses, books, and "build your home business" websites appear. Most serious: international terrorist groups use it for intelligence and attack planning. They also use advanced knowledge management techniques. |
| Insiders | Exploitation of trust relationships. | Besides employees, possible insiders are: vendors, customers, joint venture partners, visitors, subcontractors, and outsourced providers. |
| Counterfeiting | Manufacturing or distributing goods under another's name, without permission. | Examples: jeans, handbags, chemicals, pharmaceuticals. It has economic and safety impact. It is very serious for small companies entering the global market. |
| Piracy | Copying, stealing, reproducing, transmitting, or selling another's intellectual property without consent. | New technologies make it easier. Governments shut down file-sharing sites and sources of illegal software, music, and books. They also shut down makers of counterfeit pharmaceuticals and goods. |

**Insiders — what to do:**

- Organizations sometimes do not see the risk of giving partners internal access to networks, systems, information, or facilities.
- Require contractors to screen their staff well, protect data, and apply information security policies.
- The organization's risk assessment must include insiders as a threat.
- Possible harms: accessing customer financial data; stealing trade secrets, customer information, or sensitive documents.
- Confidentiality, intellectual property, and nondisclosure agreements set expectations for employees and partners.
- Those agreements also prepare the organization for legal action (Carnegie Mellon 2018).

### 5. Risk Assessment and Due Diligence (5.4)

- Business principle: "you can't manage it if you can't measure it".
- The risk assessment must:
  1. Identify the risks.
  2. Quantify them.
  3. Prioritize them according to the organization's risk acceptance criteria.
- Its results serve to choose and prioritize risk management actions.
- It is done regularly and includes risk monitoring. That monitoring addresses changes in:
  - security requirements;
  - nature of the information assets;
  - threats;
  - frequency of occurrence of threats;
  - vulnerabilities;
  - impacts.
- The monitoring also evaluates the effectiveness of security and mitigation measures.

**Main business impacts of a loss** (Trends in Proprietary Information Loss, 2007), in the book's order:

| # | Impact |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Loss of the company's reputation, image, or goodwill |
| 2 | Loss of competitive advantage in a product or service |
| 3 | Lower projected returns or profitability |
| 4 | Loss of a core technology or business process |
| 5 | Loss of competitive advantage in several products or services |

- Other impacts: loss of use, ownership, or control of intellectual property rights.
- Another: loss of information or prototypes that facilitate counterfeiting of the product.
- **Exam note:** the same study is attributed to "ASIS Foundation 2007" (p. 70) and to "ASIS International 2007" (p. 72). The references say ASIS Foundation.

**Business benefits of IAP (Moberly 2007):**

| # | Benefit |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Improves fiduciary oversight, control, and stewardship of key intangibles |
| 2 | Aligns information assets with operations and strategic vision |
| 3 | Allocates traditional security and IT resources more efficiently |
| 4 | Allows timely pursuit of information compromises and violations of intellectual property rights (IPR) |
| 5 | Serves as leverage to negotiate coverage and premiums for intellectual property (IP) and IT insurance |
| 6 | Gives consistency to regulatory reporting of intangibles |
| 7 | Standardizes internal and external handling of intangibles |
| 8 | Identifies key internal and external sources of intangibles and intellectual capital |

- The security professional must present IAP as a business resource: an enabler, not an obstacle.
- Ultimate responsibility for protecting information lies with the organization's leadership.
- But it must be assumed by everyone, internal or external, with access to the information assets.

### 6. IAP Policies and Awareness (5.5.1)

**Vital steps when developing the policy:**

1. Leadership shows commitment: it provides resources and requires each unit to align business and protection goals.
2. A dedicated department, group, or person manages and audits the policy.
3. All units, staff, temporary workers, vendors, consultants, contractors, and partners must comply with it.
4. IAP training is given repeatedly:
   - in new employee orientation;
   - during inspections;
   - at all-hands conferences;
   - on visits to other facilities;
   - on the intranet;
   - in newsletters;
   - within IT or human resources training.
5. Awareness and training efforts are documented.

**What to protect:**

- Identify what information to protect and when.
- Identify all the forms it takes during its life cycle.
- Only part of the information merits protection.
- Once identified, it is classified: the most important receives the greatest protection.

**The policy statement:**

- It sets the organization's tone. If enforced, it can support legal action.
- It must make clear that information is one of the most important resources.
- It must require that all information be evaluated according to its sensitivity.
- The measures must ensure seven attributes, in both the physical and the cyber environment:

| # | Attribute that protection must ensure |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Confidentiality |
| 2 | Integrity |
| 3 | Availability |
| 4 | Accountability |
| 5 | Recoverability |
| 6 | Auditability |
| 7 | Non-repudiation |

- Conduct random audits to verify compliance with the policy.

#### 6.1 Identification and Marking of Protected Information

- Information that merits protection is identified and marked.
- Levels used to distinguish sensitivity: confidential, restricted, limited, nonpublic (the book leaves the list open).
- **Most organizations use 2 to 4 levels of sensitivity.**
- Example of 3 categories used by many companies:

| Category | Access |
| --- | --- |
| Approved for external release | No restriction |
| Internal | Employees and contractors |
| Confidential | Specific need to know |

- The information can be electronic, oral, written, or other. It is marked or labeled whenever practical.
- The originator usually sets the classification level.
- Authorized users do not disclose the content without the owner's approval.
- Access to internal information: only company staff or others who signed a nondisclosure agreement (NDA).
- A satisfactory background investigation may also be required.
- **Access is based on current function and need to know, not just on job title or rank.**

### 7. Physical Security Applied to IAP (5.5.2)

IAP professionals must coordinate closely with physical security staff.

#### 7.1 Layered Protection (Defense in Depth)

These are concentric rings or layers of protection around an asset. It is associated with physical security, but applies equally to information. **Three perspectives:**

| # | The layers represent… | Example / note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Increasing levels of trust for whoever accesses successive layers | Maintenance staff have less trust and access than the director of research and development |
| 2 | Different technologies or measures operating together | Diverse and overlapping measures make it possible to see and cover gaps. Benefits are weighed against costs |
| 3 | Successive layers to delay, detect, and deter intruders | Each layer delays the adversary further, gives another chance to detect them, and deters them from advancing |

- These perspectives apply equally to automated systems and to information in other forms: paper, process, knowledge, or intangible.

**How to implement layered protection:**

1. Apply several layers to critical assets, according to their sensitivity or exposure to loss.
2. Ensure that the layers complement each other and do not clash.
3. Build a coordinated strategy that integrates families of measures:
   - physical security;
   - personnel security;
   - technical security;
   - access control;
   - education and awareness;
   - policies and procedures.

- Facilities and systems with company information: always protected against unauthorized entry.
- Access to internal or confidential information: authentication with unique, preauthorized credentials, physical and logical.
- The level of access is adjusted to the information the person needs to do their work.

#### 7.2 Handling of Documents and Records

This is the daily management of sensitive information on paper or in electronic form. Recommended measures:

1. Place shredders or secure containers next to printers, copiers, and fax machines.
2. Post signs reminding people to destroy overruns and bad prints.
3. Document, where appropriate, internal and external transfers of sensitive records.
4. Choose carefully the contractors who destroy records or sensitive information.
5. Destroy in a way that prevents reconstruction, according to sensitivity. Document the date and place of destruction.
6. Destroy obsolete records regularly, according to a record retention schedule.
7. Regularly destroy incidental and duplicate records.
8. Keep media awaiting destruction in secure containers.
9. If possible, do not throw destroyed media into trash bins accessible to the public.
10. Give documentation access categories, such as public use or restricted use.
11. In transport, protect with locked containers, seals, escorts, radio frequency identification (RFID) tags, transport logs, and other means.

More detail in Appendix 5A (section 24).

#### 7.3 Protection of Information in Physical Form

| Type | Measures |
| --- | --- |
| Prototypes and models | They receive the same physical security, access control, classification, employee screening, verification, and documentation as other assets. They can be paper designs, hardware, test vehicles, market test materials, software, or other prototypes. Obsolete ones are destroyed to prevent reverse engineering. Contractors or vendors who receive them are bound by contract to protect them according to the owner's policies. They also receive instructions for return or final disposition. |
| Manufacturing processes and equipment | 1) Access to production only for those who need it for their duties. 2) Restrict photography in production. 3) Contractors with access sign NDAs. 4) Employees, contractors, and visitors wear a badge with their status and access level. 5) Visitors register in an automated system or a log. 6) Obsolete or damaged equipment and scrap are disposed of without revealing information about the area. 7) Information at the loading dock, such as materials and quantities received, may require protection. |
| Compartmentalization and physical or visual barriers | Store information of different classification separately. Use barriers and covers when sensitive information could be exposed to unauthorized persons. |

### 8. Personnel Security (5.5.3)

- It includes:
  - due diligence investigations of potential partners;
  - standard preemployment screening;
  - vetting of subcontractors, vendors, and consultants.
- **Two challenges:**
  1. Screening is often not controlled by security, risk, or IAP. Legal or human resources runs it.
  2. Investigations take a lot of time. For that reason procedures are sometimes skipped, and information is at greater risk.
- **What the IAP professional must do:**
  1. Open an effective communication channel with the areas that do the screening.
  2. Ensure that vetting covers all trusted third parties with whom protected information is shared.
  3. Periodically review the screening, due diligence, and vetting procedures.

### 9. Privacy Protection (5.5.4)

- Every organization handles private information of employees, executives, relationships, customers, or others.
- Some of it may be personally identifiable information (PII).
- To maintain trust and comply with the law, the IAP professional must:
  1. Set privacy policies and designate an employee responsible for the program.
  2. Assess the private information of employees, partners, vendors, customers, and others. Determine the legal and regulatory requirements.
  3. Have systems that protect the privacy of employees and customers.
  4. Have a mechanism to investigate privacy compromises, including identity theft. Inform victims when appropriate.
  5. Review regulatory guidance, such as that of the U.S. Federal Trade Commission and the European Union's data protection directives.
  6. Clearly mark private information. The marking indicates:
     - how it will be used and to whom it will be made available;
     - what notices and actions there will be if it is compromised;
     - how to destroy it or dispose of it when it is no longer needed.
  7. Audit the program to verify that the policies are properly applied.

### 10. Business Practices (5.5.5)

- **ASIS International holds that security is a business function.**
- IAP principles are integrated into daily practice and business culture.
- Conversely, the business mission and philosophy enter the IAP strategy.

**Steps to harmonize IAP and business:**

1. Coordinate IAP with all relevant areas. Those the book names: legal · human resources · security · risk management · safety · IT · research and development · procurement · marketing · public relations · training · logistics · competitive intelligence · international relations · accounting and finance · facilities · and others (open list in the book).
2. Include IAP in the business continuity plan. This way critical information retains availability, confidentiality, and integrity in any crisis, including response and recovery.
3. Include IAP material in onboarding, training, and professional development.
4. Communicate IAP issues at all levels of management.

**Relationships with other companies (alliances, outsourcing), domestic or international:**

- They pose special risks: companies unwittingly lower their guard.
- In the U.S. there are thousands of front companies, often created by foreign governments, to obtain information.
- That is why it is essential to conduct due diligence before partnering and sharing information.

### 11. Operations Security (OPSEC) or Information Risk Management (5.5.6)

- **Definition:** operations security (OPSEC) originated in the U.S. military.
- It protects unclassified information that could reveal sensitive plans and operations.
- **1988:** a National Security Decision Directive implemented it throughout the U.S. federal executive branch.
- Since then it has been applied successfully to:
  - research and development information;
  - technical data;
  - product test data;
  - law enforcement sensitive information;
  - commercial information against competitive intelligence efforts.
- In the private sector it is sometimes called "information risk management".
- It looks at the whole picture and identifies gaps that remain despite current measures.
- Those gaps are avenues of compromise, intentional or inadvertent.
- Examples of gaps: between legal and security strategies; between protection measures of different countries.
- It serves organizations of all sizes. It is very valuable for small companies with little staff or security resources (Peterson 2005).
- **Aggregation principle:** small pieces of data from several sources, when combined, can reveal sensitive information.

**Steps to avoid releasing small but combinable data:**

1. Create IAP policies specific to key projects.
2. Watch that peripheral information does not give intelligence to a competitor or adversary. Examples: supply orders, hiring plans, facility improvements, other observable activities, and other indicators.
3. Consider the vulnerability of what is handed to third parties in joint ventures or trust relationships (vendor, supplier, customer). The other party may not have sufficient IAP.
4. Check whether the company's public information, taken together, reveals anything sensitive. Periodically review your own website and those of partners.
5. Evaluate vulnerabilities from the adversary's perspective. Defend against realistic collection techniques.
6. Implement an approval process for presentations, papers, or articles that touch on sensitive activities or plans.

- **Exam note:** the text cites "Peterson, 2005", but the references list Peterson 2020.

### 12. Security in Travel and Meetings (5.5.7)

Sensitive information is especially vulnerable during travel, trade shows, and meetings on or off the premises.

#### 12.1 Domestic and International Travel

Measures for the traveler carrying any sensitive information:

1. Receive a security briefing beforehand, or consult travel advisories, notifications, information services, and publications before leaving. One source: the Overseas Security Advisory Council (OSAC) of the U.S. Department of State.
2. Keep a low profile so as not to be a target. Avoid items that reveal the employer or personal or national identity.
3. Carry only the strictly necessary information.
4. Carry the information on your person, not in checked luggage. Do not hand that luggage to porters or bellmen.
5. Keep in mind the risk of technical surveillance (electronic eavesdropping).
6. Do not work or talk about sensitive topics in public areas or on public transportation.
7. Use privacy filters, cable locks, and other measures on computers.
8. Before leaving, erase unnecessary data from devices. Or leave the usual ones at home and travel with clean equipment.
9. Do not use public Wi-Fi, hotel fax, copiers, or business centers for sensitive information.
10. If files are transferred to someone else's computer to print, copy, or project, delete them afterward along with the temporary files.
11. Report actual, failed, or suspected attempts to obtain information during the trip.

#### 12.2 Trade Shows

- They are a traditional setting for business and government intelligence collection.
- Conventions, exhibitions, and seminars bring together experts from many countries and showcase special programs and technologies.

Measures:

1. Create an awareness program and a reporting mechanism for those who attend trade shows, conferences, symposia, and technical meetings. Among its topics it must address elicitation techniques and remind attendees of NDAs in force.
2. Identify high-risk travelers based on position, project, access, or clearance. Create special procedures for them.
3. Consider documenting all the information and equipment carried, such as laptops or handheld devices. If appropriate, back up the information.
4. Conduct a debriefing on return when appropriate. Example: high-risk travelers or attendees of technical conferences abroad.

#### 12.3 On- and Off-Site Meetings

Business or scientific meetings are a historical target for information collection and for approaches to attendees. Measures:

1. Obtain the annual calendar of strategic and critical meetings. Design an IAP strategy for each one, based on the risk assessment.
2. Before confirming the venue, obtain plans and data on its telecommunications and audiovisual infrastructure.
3. Encourage organizers to choose venues and rooms using information protection criteria.
4. Hold low-profile events when appropriate: minimal signage and electronic publication of events, sponsors, rooms, and schedules.
5. Use meeting names that do not suggest the topic or identify the attendees.
6. Decide whether a technical surveillance countermeasures (TSCM) inspection is needed before and during the meeting. Typical vulnerabilities:
   - unencrypted wireless microphones;
   - wireless headsets;
   - audio leakage;
   - electronic equipment brought by participants or others;
   - unsecured telecommunications equipment, cables, and terminals.
7. Decide whether secure shipping and storage are needed before, during, and after the event.
8. Protect printed materials and computer media during their reproduction, transport, and storage.
9. Minimize the distribution of paper copies.
10. Protect electronic presentations when creating, editing, transmitting, and presenting them. Delete them completely from computers that do not belong to the company.
11. Ensure that vendors follow proper information security practices.
12. Limit access to the rooms. If appropriate, use security officers 24 hours for rooms and electronic equipment, including laptops.
13. Collect the information and notes left by attendees. Dispose of them securely.

### 13. Preventing and Detecting Counterfeiting and Illegal Copying (5.5.8)

1. Monitor the Internet to detect counterfeit products.
2. Train employees to detect counterfeits, known suspects, competitors, or common schemes.
3. Require NDAs from all employees, vendors, and subcontractors.
4. Use anti-counterfeiting technology.
5. Number all memoranda and technical reports.
6. Conduct periodic compliance and inventory control audits, inside and outside the organization.
7. Collaborate with law enforcement and prosecutors on detection, awareness, alerts, and information sharing.

### 14. Copyrights (5.5.9)

- **Under international law, copyright does not need to be registered to be protected.**
- The author or holder can register it with the government to formalize ownership. This helps in later actions.
- The holder must not let control of the copyright pass to the organization's agents or distributors.

**Preventive measures:**

1. Include copyright protections in contracts and marketing strategies.
2. Sign written, enforceable contracts that bind agents, vendors, distributors, and employees to protect the copyright.
3. Do not assign or license the copyright until all the consequences are understood.
4. Ensure that the copyright remains with the organization when the assignment, license, transaction, or investment ends.

**If a violation is discovered, act immediately. Response tools:**

1. Hire legal counsel.
2. Inform the competent authorities.
3. Conduct investigations, raids, and seizures.
4. Initiate civil litigation, administrative proceedings, and criminal action.

**Questions for choosing the tool:**

1. Is the copyright registered or protectable in that country?
2. Does the harm occur in that country or abroad?
3. What is the source of the harm: competitors, employees, agents, or contractors?

### 15. Trademarks, Trade Dress, and Service Marks (5.5.10)

- Anyone entering a new market must plan the protection of their intellectual property from the start.
- **The main means is to register the trademark before the product enters the country's stream of commerce.**
- This way the mark is eligible for protection under that country's law.
- And infringement can be remedied through administrative or judicial channels.
- It is also advisable to place trademarks and trade or service notices on the company's materials.
- Outside the country of origin, the best weapon is prevention. Practical steps:
  1. Know the organization's rights before the product crosses the border.
  2. Create and register a version in the host country's language. Otherwise the market invents a nickname. It may not be liked, or someone registers it and forces you to buy the rights from them.
  3. Register the mark in neighboring countries. It protects a possible expansion and blocks marks that consumers could confuse.
  4. Continuously investigate products that infringe the mark or use a confusingly similar mark registered by another.
- **Pending:** the book names trade dress and service marks in the title, but does not define them.

### 16. Patents (5.5.11)

- An inventor can protect their invention with a patent or by treating it as a trade secret.
- **Patent:** gives several benefits, but requires publicly disclosing the elements of the invention. **It lasts only 20 years.**
- **Trade secret:** is not disclosed and can last indefinitely.
- Stealing a trade secret can violate criminal laws. **There are no criminal laws on patent infringement** (U.S. Department of Justice 2006).

**Approach to protecting inventions:**

1. Apply the trade secret guidelines to every new process or product until the patent is granted.
2. Obtain patent protection in all appropriate jurisdictions.
3. Consider the U.S. International Trade Commission as a forum for resolving patent disputes.

### 17. Trade Secrets (5.5.12)

- They are not registered with any outside agency. That is why the owner retains more control over the asset.
- **For something to be a trade secret, the owner must prove three things:**
  1. The information gave it value or benefit.
  2. The secret was specifically identified.
  3. The owner gave it a reasonable level of protection.
- **"Reasonable" is a high standard.** It requires demonstrating a robust security program and strict measures.
- Those measures must be defined, communicated, and applied clearly and consistently.

**Steps to protect trade secrets:**

1. Document the identification and valuation of the secrets, their role in competitive advantage, and all protection measures.
2. Have reasonable traditional security and cybersecurity measures against unauthorized access.
3. Conduct periodic and random security audits to verify compliance.
4. Sign NDAs with employees, vendors, consultants, and others before any disclosure.
5. Set need-to-know criteria: each person accesses only what they need for their work.
6. Use effective warning notices, so that everyone knows exactly what is being protected.
7. Properly destroy materials that are no longer needed.

### 18. Comparison of Intellectual Property Forms (Using Only Data from the Chapter)

| Form | Registration? | Public disclosure? | Duration | Legal avenue | Key to protection |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Copyright | Not mandatory under international law; registering it helps enforce it | The book does not address it | The book does not give it | Civil litigation, administrative proceeding, and criminal action | Written contracts; do not leave control to agents or distributors |

### 19. International Aspects (5.5.13)

Before introducing intellectual property into another country:

1. Work with lawyers well in advance. They must have experience in that market.
2. Inventory the intellectual property and sensitive information. Make a protection plan for each type.
3. Apply for patents, assign patent rights, and license technology as appropriate.
4. Evaluate the risks of contractual relationships. Many intellectual property disputes arise between companies that do business together.
5. Use NDAs in negotiations and contracts.
6. Identify the technical data and restrict it to those with a need to know. Compartmentalize knowledge.
7. Protect certain forms of information with traditional physical security: tools, dies, or formulas.
8. Conduct an intellectual property due diligence investigation. Look for existing infringements in that market and study the experience of similar companies there.
9. Know the country's intellectual property rules and regulations.
10. Licensors consult an attorney about how much of the license must be registered so as not to compromise their rights.

### 20. Nondisclosure Agreements and Contracts (5.5.14)

- A written NDA ensures a common understanding and a legal obligation to protect information assets.
- The NDA must acknowledge that all information that records business communications or transactions, in any medium, is an official record under the law.
- That information is handled according to the information asset policies and procedures.
- It may be necessary to extend confidentiality to board members, employees, agents, and others.
- **Employees:**
  - All sign an NDA as a condition of employment.
  - They acknowledge that all information of the employer, vendors, and customers is confidential, will remain so, and is the property of the employer.
  - They confirm that they have read, understand, and will comply with the IAP policies and procedures.
  - When employment ends, they are reminded of their continuing obligations under the NDA.
- **Contractors, subcontractors, consultants, and vendors with access:** bound by contract to protect the information at the same level at which it is protected inside the company.

### 21. Technical Protection Measures (5.6)

- Combining traditional measures with technical measures is an application of **convergence** in asset protection.
- Many technical measures require specialized expertise; some services are outsourced.
- Those partners must be chosen carefully, because they will have access to sensitive information.

#### 21.1 Technical Surveillance Countermeasures (TSCM, 5.6.1)

- **Definition:** services, equipment, and techniques to locate, identify, and neutralize technical surveillance activities (electronic eavesdropping).
- TSCM must be part of the overall protection strategy.
- Regularly inspect telecommunications equipment, cables, and terminals.
- Use equipment, methods, and personnel capable of detecting current threats.
- Inspect offices and boardrooms:
  - on a regular basis;
  - at random;
  - just before any sensitive conversation.

#### 21.2 Protection in Special Environments (5.6.2)

**Mobile devices:**

- They can fall outside the company's IT security and the facility's physical security.
- People underestimate the amount and importance of the data they store and transmit.

| # | Measure |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Have the employee acknowledge in writing that company equipment and its information are the property of the employer |
| 2 | Control and discourage devices with a built-in camera, especially near sensitive material or in restricted areas |
| 3 | Do not store Social Security numbers, credit card numbers, or passwords on wireless devices |
| 4 | Lock the cell phone when not in use |
| 5 | Consider remote lock or wipe software in case of loss or theft |
| 6 | Label or engrave laptops with the organization's name, address, and phone number, to facilitate recovery |
| 7 | Prevent the last user name that logged in from being displayed |
| 8 | Use a discreet case, without distinguishing marks |
| 9 | Beware of public Wi-Fi or hot spots (hotel, airport): there is a risk of connecting to a nearby computer used for identity theft |
| 10 | Configure laptops not to connect on their own to access points marked as insecure |
| 11 | Check with the manufacturer for updates and vulnerability notices |

**Web conferencing:**

- Video, telephone, or web meetings are not necessarily secure.
- Find out how the provider protects private information and what its password policy is.
- Encrypt data transfer whenever possible.
- **If the measures cannot be validated, assume the conversation is not private.**

**Outsourcing:**

- It transfers operational control and accountability to third parties. It can increase the risk to information.
- Some risks are not obvious: they come from subtle or hidden clauses in contracts.
- Conduct due diligence on potential vendors:
  1. Review their financial performance and reputation. Look for intellectual property violations, commercial complaints, export control problems, and ties to foreign governments or suspicious companies.
  2. Sign individual and corporate NDAs with the vendors and their subcontractors.
  3. Conduct on-site security reviews before signing and regularly afterward.
  4. Identify, physically and logically, where the outsourced staff will work. Ensure adequate physical and IT measures.
  5. Consult with lawyers on the laws of all participating countries. Topics: export and import of technology, personal and privacy information, and protection procedures.

### 22. Response and Recovery After an Information Loss (5.7)

| Phase | Steps |
| --- | --- |
| Investigation (5.7.1) | 1) Thoroughly investigate known and suspected compromises. Purposes: support law enforcement, litigation, asset recovery, root cause, incident prevention, and loss or damage assessment. 2) Create an investigation plan and coordinate it with counsel. 3) Identify internal or external investigative resources. 4) Maintain liaison with law enforcement, investigative service providers, and information sources. |
| Damage assessment (5.7.2) | 1) Determine as soon as possible, and completely, what information was compromised. 2) Determine the implications. List from the book: financial loss · project delays · operational impact · corporate image · shareholder confidence · legal liability · corporate relationships with partners, vendors, suppliers, and subcontractors (open list in the book). 3) Report the actual and potential impacts to the appropriate level of management. |
| Recovery and follow-up (no number of its own in the book) | 1) The two primary elements of recovery: return to normal operation as soon as possible, and implement measures so it does not recur. 2) Activate the applicable parts of the business continuity plan. 3) Conduct a root cause analysis and apply corrective actions. 4) Maintain an incident database, including complaints and suspicions, for root cause and trend analysis. |

### 23. Chapter Summary (5.8)

- An effective strategy begins with a clear and practical policy, shared with all parties and applied fairly.
- The right mix of measures varies in each organization.
- Normally two types are combined:

| Security measures | Legal measures |
| --- | --- |
| Personnel | Copyrights |
| Equipment | Patents |
| Awareness training | Trademarks |
|  | Nondisclosure agreements |
|  | Contract clauses |

- The program seeks prevention, but must be ready for response and recovery.
- For pervasive risk management, the IAP program is developed in parallel with core operations and business continuity planning.

### 24. Appendix 5A — Information Disposal and Destruction (p. 92)

Based on material from the National Association for Information Destruction (NAID, www.naidonline.org), used with permission. Six principles:

| # | Principle | Content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Every company has information that requires destruction | Customer lists, price lists, sales statistics, draft bids and correspondence, and even memos, are of interest to competitors. Every company also holds information that must be kept private. Without safeguards, it ends up in the trash, where anyone can legally take it. Throwing away private or proprietary data exposes the company to criminal and civil proceedings and business losses. |
| 2 | Destroy stored records on a regular schedule | No record is kept longer than necessary for business or compliance. Routine destruction avoids looking suspicious in litigation or an audit. It also limits what must be handed over in legal proceedings. |
| 3 | Protect incidental records that are discarded daily | Daily trash contains information usable against the company: phone messages, memos, draft bids, and correspondence. |
| 4 | Recycling is not an adequate alternative to destruction | Paper is given away or sold. There is no practical way to prove whether a document was destroyed, or when. It is better to recycle after properly destroying. |
| 5 | Due diligence is essential | Require the destruction service to provide a signed attestation with the date of destruction. That certificate of destruction is an important legal record. **It does not transfer responsibility for confidentiality to the contractor.** If the private information appears later, a court may question how the contractor was chosen. Without due diligence, the company can be found negligent. Due diligence can be: reviewing the contractor's policies and procedures, and conducting initial and follow-up on-site audits. |
| 6 | Most file storage companies do not shred on their own | If they subcontract the destruction, the customer must investigate with due diligence the destruction company they use. |

### 1. What Ethics Is (6.1.1)

- **Ethics:** a discipline or system of moral principles that governs human conduct and relationships between people.
  - It judges whether an action is right or wrong (rightness/wrongness).
  - It judges whether a motive or an end is good or bad (goodness/badness).
- Problem: different cultures, philosophies, and values. What is ethical or legal in one country can be the opposite in another.
- If values are seen as subjective (they depend on context, they are not absolute), deciding what is ethical becomes difficult.

**Test of ethical conduct — Blanchard & Peale (1988), *The Power of Ethical Management*:**

| # | Question |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Is it legal? |
| 2 | Is it balanced? |
| 3 | How will it make me feel about myself? |

- The security professional must ask these questions throughout their work.
- It is difficult because of the many competing interests and demands.

**Types of ethics:**

| Type | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| Descriptive ethics | Explains or describes ethical facts. |
| Analytical ethics | Examines ethical concepts to better understand their meaning and justification. |
| Applied ethics | It is active. It applies ethical concepts to concrete business situations. It judges what is right and what is wrong. It says what should and should not be done. **It is the most relevant to business.** |

### 4. The Ethical Dilemma: A Widespread Problem (6.1.4)

**Three factors that, according to psychologists, motivate unethical or dishonest acts:**

2. Rationalization.
3. Opportunity.

**Ethical Versus Legal:**

| The company's view | Consequence |
| --- | --- |

| It has social responsibility | It makes sense to go beyond what the law requires. |

- Some companies believe that only those that seek solely to maximize profits survive.
- Others believe that only those that at least appear to have corporate governance and social responsibility survive.
- **Several jurisdictions:** a company in several countries faces different legal standards.

### 5. Causes of Poor Ethical Decisions (6.1.5)

| Cause | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Convenience | Almost every invention is born from seeking convenience. The decision is reduced to "the right thing" versus "the easiest thing". Human nature tends to choose the easy. |
| Winning | Society venerates the winner; second place is "the first loser". The shortcut to first place can trample the values that gave the initial success, such as a solid ethical culture. |
| Relativism | Almost any act can be rationalized as right in some situation. Added to the previous two, the unethical seems logical. It is also called **situation ethics**: there are no true ethical values to guide conduct. With it, the company does only what is legal. |

Counterweight to relativism: people know how they want to be treated and do not appreciate someone who acts unfairly.

**Exam note:** there are two different triads. The motivating factors of the psychologists are desire, rationalization, and opportunity (6.1.4). The causes of poor decisions in business are convenience, winning, and relativism (6.1.5).

### 6. The Dichotomy of Ethics (6.1.6)

Idea: it is easy to see ethical lapses in others; it is hard to see them in oneself. We rarely demand of ourselves what we demand of others.

| Source | Data |
| --- | --- |
| Survey of college students (Woellert 2002) | **84%** believed the U.S. was experiencing a business ethics crisis. **77%** believed chief executive officers (CEOs) were responsible. **59%** of those same students admitted to cheating on an exam. |
| Workplace study (Copeland 2002) | **43%** admitted at least one unethical act in the last year. **75%** saw such an act and did nothing. |
| Company | Preaches ethics, demands it, and condemns the competition, but its own decisions are not always ethical. Having a code of ethics does not guarantee ethical altruism. |
| Motivated blindness — Bazerman 2020 | Acting unethically out of self-interest without realizing it. |

**Can Ethics and Success Be Reconciled?**

- The problem is that the right thing is usually the hardest.
- There is no proof that "winning at any cost" yields more long-term success. There is solid proof of the opposite.
- Study by Jiang (2017): service quality is higher where ethics is applied more rigorously. Findings:
  1. Service excellence is essential, but not enough for success.
  2. A service climate and an ethical climate that work together generate positive behaviors without supervision.
  3. The employee's perception of the ethical climate serves as an early warning of future unethical conduct.
  4. Service and ethics are worth more in chaotic or competitive markets.

**How to root ethics in the culture:**

- Ethical conduct must be firmly installed in the corporate culture.
- Introducing something new into an established culture is laborious and does not always work.
- Employees resist change, especially if the current operation seems satisfactory to them or benefits them.
- One avenue: enforce codes of conduct that apply to everyone, from the chief executive down.
- Nothing undermines ethics more than applying the rules to only some.
- To accept change, the employee must see it as beneficial and equitable.
- Key rule: a value that does not apply to everyone ends up applying to no one.

### 7. Business Ethics Simplified (6.1.7)

- **Aristotle:** one becomes just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, and brave by doing brave acts.
- Applied to business: it becomes ethical by performing only ethical actions.
- Difficulty: people, cultures, laws, circumstances, rules, and business obligations differ. That is why there can be more than one right option depending on the perspective.
- Doing the right thing requires determination, because it is usually neither the easiest nor the most popular.
- Solution for setting and disseminating standards: a code of ethics, such as that of ASIS International (Appendix 6A).

### 8. Ethics Codes and Programs (6.1.8)

#### 8.1 Why Ethics Matters Especially to the Security Professional

1. They are an employee, agent, or representative of the organization they protect. The same ethical standards bind them as everyone else.
   - Because of the nature of their work, a **higher** standard than their colleagues may be required of them.
   - Whoever evaluates the acts of others must match or exceed what is demanded of those others.
2. They may be tasked with creating the ethics program and code, or with overseeing compliance with it.

In general, part of their responsibility is to do the right thing for their employer or client.

#### 8.2 Relationship Between an Ethics Code and an Ethics Program

| Concept | What it is | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ethics code | Statement of shared values: accepted concepts and beliefs about the organization's ethical responsibilities and ambitions. It is based on its traditions and personality, that is, the corporate culture. | States the underlying principles. It is the fundamental step in setting standards. |
| Ethics program | Ongoing structure of support and oversight. | Gives guidance and structure to comply with the code. Turns ethical concern into daily operating conduct. |

- The code alone does not guarantee an ethical operation. There are many cases of companies with a published code that acted wrongly.
- The right words, spoken forcefully by leaders, do support ethical conduct.
- Without a program, the code ends up ignored and forgotten.
- Principles and structure can be separate or combined. The ASIS Code combines them.
- **Reasons for wanting to be ethical:**
  - Altruistic: it is the right way to do business.
  - Practical: appearing ethical is a good business decision.
  - Practical: acting ethically avoids the problems that unethical conduct causes.

#### 8.3 Typical Content of an Ethics Code

1. Message from the chief executive that explains and endorses the purpose and importance of the code.
2. Description of the specific nature of the business.
3. List of the organization's values, with reference to:
   - employees;
   - customers;
   - shareholders or stakeholders;
   - partners: vendors and contractors, among those the book cites;
   - society and community.
4. Statement of commitment to the code: implementation, training, and oversight, among those the book cites. Proof that a structure exists to support it.

#### 8.4 Code of Conduct and Ethical-Hazards Risk Assessment

- Feldman (2014): the code of conduct must be a useful, living document. It is not enough to show it at the induction of new employees.
- It must clearly set out the responsibilities of the company and of the employee.
- It includes the **employee's duty to report** lapses or violations they observe.
- It must cover the vulnerabilities common to every company: theft, accounting irregularities, and corruption.
- It must also cover the vulnerabilities specific to the company. They are detected with an **ethical-hazards risk assessment**.
  - **Perverse incentives:** for example, compensation based **100%** on financial goals.
  - **Unintended consequences** of policies, procedures, or expectations. They lead to deciding poorly "for good reasons".
  - Example: the employee justifies unethical conduct because they win a contract that saves their colleagues' jobs.
- **Pending:** the book puts this passage in quotation marks without indicating an author.

#### 8.5 How to Write the Code

- It requires a lot of reflection and research.
- Its audience is almost anyone doing business with or interested in the organization. It must convey values and credibility.
- It must be concise and direct:
  - too short and simple → it is not taken seriously;
  - too long and complicated → it is neither understood nor followed.
- It can be displayed publicly, so it must represent the organization well.

**Six concepts to consider when developing it:**

1. Support from the top: senior executives and also the board of directors or governing bodies. Without that active support, the code and program fail.
2. Thoroughly analyze the company's most important issues. It must reflect its own values, not generic concepts.
3. The codes of other organizations serve as a model.
4. A code of conduct must provide the behavioral guidance that ensures compliance with the code of ethics.
5. Field test it before disseminating it to the whole organization. This ensures that it is complete, exhaustive, and understandable.
6. Once finished, announce and distribute it widely. Employees and others must know that it exists and that it matters.

#### 8.6 The Ethics Program

- It is the step that follows the code.
- The code alone is not enough because the quality and intentions of managers cannot be guaranteed.
- The program guides and supports employees in complying with the code.
- As long as people are involved, a program will always be needed.
- Hosmer: middle and line managers tend to prioritize economic performance over ethics, because they are evaluated on economic criteria.
  - Their ethics should count in their performance evaluation, but this does not always happen.

**Exam note:** the text cites "Hosmer 2020". The reference list says Hosmer, L. T. 2010, *The Ethics of Management*, 7th edition.

**Factors that most determine the ethical level of conduct in an organization** (personal values do not always predict it):

1. Climate or environment of the organization.
2. Role of executives, superiors, and peers.
3. Availability of resources.
4. Corporate culture regarding social ties and corporate governance.
5. Intellectual foundations of the business system.

- Most of the empirical literature downplays the individual as a decisive factor.
- Business principles and executives' conduct influence more than individual conscience.
- That is why ethical direction is provided by corporate leadership, guided by the ethics program.

#### 8.7 Integrating Code and Program in the Organization

The organization must always have:

1. A well-written **ethics statement** disseminated to everyone it deals with, for example stakeholders and customers.
2. A well-written **code of ethical conduct** that guides how the organization, its employees, and its representatives do their work.

- They can be separate documents or a single combined one.
- Without both, the organization runs the risk of acting against its own goals, with serious consequences.
- Even when ethics is already part of the culture, the documents serve as visible proof of intent and as a reminder.

| Good code of conduct | Bad code of conduct |
| --- | --- |
| Provides a framework with reasonable guidelines. | Hampers the operation with narrowly defined rules. |
| Those covered know the principles by which their conduct will be measured. | — |
| They feel they have leeway to decide according to the factors of each case. | — |

Reason: the business is constantly changing.

### 11. Sources of Legal Information (6.2.1)

|  | Primary source | Secondary source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | Statute or case from the state or federal jurisdiction itself. | Case, statute, or journal from another state or jurisdiction. Also reference articles. |
| Strength | The strongest for interpreting the law of a jurisdiction. | Not suitable for supporting a legal argument or justifying a legal proposition. |
| Use | Mandatory for knowing what the law is in a state. | Helps in understanding the legal reasoning of another jurisdiction. It can be used to persuade one's own state or a private entity. |
| Examples | State statute; published appellate court cases in that jurisdiction's *reporters*. | Ruling from another state; *law review* article; industry publication. |

**Exam note:** the book says the secondary source cannot be used for a legal argument. It then says that it does serve to persuade. Understand it this way: it does not prove what the law is, it only helps to persuade.

#### 11.1 Primary Sources

- **Example:** to know whether possessing a stun gun (electrical weapon) is illegal in Massachusetts, the primary source is the Massachusetts statute that prohibits it.
- Legal articles in industry journals are not enough to define corporate policies.
- The security manager must assign the research to lawyers.

**Statutory law:**

- An act of the legislative branch becomes law when the executive branch approves it as the constitution mandates.
- Each statute adds to the earlier ones and forms the body of statutory law.
- It is published in government books, in commercial publishers' books, and online.
- It is worthwhile to know those volumes, but ask a lawyer to interpret the statutes and their effects.
- **Ordinances or local laws:** rules of cities, towns, and villages. They are not called *statutes* because the local level is not sovereign.
- It includes criminal and noncriminal or civil laws.
- There are acts or omissions that are crimes even though they are not in the criminal section of the code. They must be counted when evaluating criminal laws.
  - Traffic example: driving under the influence of drugs or alcohol.
  - Traffic example: leaving the scene of an accident without reporting it to the police.

**Case law:**

| Concept | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Litigation | The parties ask a court to resolve a private dispute. |
| Prosecution | The State brings criminal charges. |
| District court | Usually handles both types of case. Its rulings are primary authority, but are rarely used to argue: they are considered only persuasive. |
| Appellate court | Accepts the facts found by the lower court. It only reviews whether the judge properly applied or interpreted the law. |
| *Reporters* | Bound volumes where the appellate court publishes its rulings: facts, issues raised, and conclusion. They are the basis of case law. The cases published there are a primary source in that jurisdiction. |
| Precedent / *stare decisis* (“let the decision stand”) | A decided case must be followed in that jurisdiction. It is a fundamental legal principle. |

- "Case law" refers, in general, to cases decided by state or federal appellate courts.
- Beware of district court cases that appear in the media: they can be appealed and reversed.
- A lawyer must be consulted to know whether an old case is still good law, was overturned, or was distinguished.

**Exam note:** the book calls district court rulings "primary authority", but says they are "only persuasive". For the exam: the case law that binds is that of appellate courts.

#### 11.2 Secondary Sources

| Source | Value to the security professional |
| --- | --- |
| Law review article | Good starting point for learning an area of law. It is more useful if it cites cases from your own state; those primary sources are then consulted. For drafting a policy: it gives background and helps formulate it. The organization's legal counsel then reviews it. |
| Trade publication | Basic information. Short articles. They may be biased if written by vendors. **Last resort** as a legal reference. |

### 12. Future Effects of Law on Security (6.2.2)

- The use and perception of new technology can greatly change how civil law affects security.
- The security manager must anticipate robotics, artificial intelligence, and other advances.
- They help detect crimes and protect assets, but may create new legal liabilities.

**Security robots:**

- They follow a fixed route or are operated remotely.
- They carry cameras on the head and shoulders.
- They have fire and water leak sensors.
- They use facial recognition and consult databases of wanted criminals.
- Questions it raises:
  - Will there be civil lawsuits from injured or harmed persons? Examples: contract guard companies, proprietary employees, insurers, and others.
  - What civil rules apply today to machinery in the workplace?

**Surveillance technology:**

- Pens, personal digital assistants (PDAs), and cell phones can serve as trackers.
- Could they be used to monitor employees' conversations, body temperature, heart rate, and emotional state?

**Likely sequence of a new technology:**

1. It is implemented.
2. It is challenged in a civil court.
3. A government body regulates it.

The manager must consider this sequence before implementing technology.

**Hypothetical ABC Corp. case:**

- It monitors conversations, skin temperature, and heart rate with an enhanced PDA.
- Security announces by email that it will investigate internal thefts.
- It detects a spike in the heart rate and temperature of employee Jim Smith. It interrogates him and he suffers a heart attack. Lawsuit.
- **Lesson 1:** a good policy considers how **12 average citizens** (a jury) would react. Whoever does not reach an out-of-court settlement usually goes to a jury trial.
- **Lesson 2:** losing a trial or provoking a regulation through negligence has "aftershocks". ABC would be remembered as the intrusive company that lost and prompted a new law.
- **Lesson 3:** the security professional must be proactive and creative to foresee problems before they occur.

### 13. Appendix 6A — ASIS International Code of Ethics

#### 13.1 History of the Code

| Stage | Characteristics |
| --- | --- |
| Origin | ASIS was called the American Society for Industrial Security. It grew out of industrial security programs during and after World War II. |
| First code | Focused on the responsibilities of those who defended the U.S. Simple: general principles, with no conduct guidelines or concrete rules. |
| Second version | ASIS and the industry grew and became international. The focus was broadened. It added detailed examples of ethical conduct. It was based on the codes of similar organizations. |
| Current code | Combines an ethics statement with a code of conduct. It sets values and concrete conduct guidelines. |

#### 13.2 Characteristics of the Current Code

- **Normative:** it says what is expected of the member and gives standards to verify that it is met.
- It governs the conduct of members toward employers, clients, colleagues, and ASIS itself.
- Aimed at members, but serves as a guide to all security professionals.
- It is the most complete ethical standard for security professionals, in content and in the number of people subject to it.
- It uses terms such as "honorable", "rights of others", "faithful and diligent", and "legal". They have recognized definitions that anyone understands in any culture.
- Its intention is unequivocal: to guide the professional to do the right thing.
- ASIS offers ethics training to anyone who requests it.
- It is published at asisonline.org.

#### 13.3 Structure

1. Preamble.
2. Code of Ethics: states the six articles.
3. Ethical Considerations: develops each article with numbered provisions.

#### 13.4 Preamble (I. Preamble)

- The quality of professional security ultimately depends on professionals accepting special standards of conduct.
- It also depends on their acting in good faith in their professional relationships.
- That is why ASIS adopts the code.
- Scrupulous compliance with it is a **mandatory condition** of being a member of ASIS or being affiliated with it.

#### 13.5 Articles and Their Provisions

**Article I — Act in accordance with the law and the highest moral principles.**

1. Comply with the law of the country where the services are provided and always act honorably.
2. Do not knowingly associate, sharing responsibility in a job, with colleagues who do not comply with the law or these ethical standards.
3. Be fair and respect the rights of others when practicing the profession.

**Article II — Truthfulness, honesty, and integrity.**

1. Disclose all relevant information to whoever has a right to know it.
2. **Right to know:** a legally enforceable claim of a person for the member to disclose information to them. It does not depend on that person having known beforehand that the information existed.
3. Do not knowingly disseminate misleading information. Nor encourage or take part in its dissemination.

**Article III — Faithfulness and diligence in professional responsibilities.**

1. **Faithful:** fair and firm in fulfilling promises and commitments.
2. **Diligent:** puts their best effort into each assignment.
3. Do not act in matters involving a conflict of interest without disclosing it and obtaining the appropriate approval.
4. Present services or products fairly and truthfully.

**Article IV — Competence.**

1. **Competent:** has and applies the skills and knowledge that the task requires.
2. Do not accept tasks outside your competence. Do not claim a competence you do not have.

**Article V — Protect confidential information and exercise due care to prevent its improper disclosure.**

1. **Confidential information:** nonpublic information whose disclosure is restricted.
2. **Due care:** do not knowingly disclose confidential information. Do not use a confidence to the detriment of the principal or for the benefit of oneself or third parties.
   - Exception: the principal consents after learning all the facts.
   - Confidentiality continues after the business relationship ends.
3. Limits of the duty of confidentiality:
   - Whoever receives information without having agreed to confidentiality is not obliged to keep it quiet.
   - Confidentiality does not bind regarding acts or omissions that violate the law.
4. What the principal entrusts to the member is **not privileged information** under the law. In a trial, the member may have to testify to it even if the principal's lawyer objects.
5. Do not disclose confidential information for personal benefit without the corresponding authorization.

**Article VI — Do not maliciously injure the reputation or professional practice of colleagues, clients, or employers.**

1. Do not make false and malicious comments about the competence, performance, or professional capacity of a colleague.
2. Whoever knows or has reasonable grounds to believe that another member violated the code should report it to the **Ethical Standards Council**, under **Article VIII of the Bylaws** of ASIS.

**Exam note:** almost all the provisions use "shall" (obligation). The one in Article VI.2 uses "should" (recommendation).

### 14. Appendix 6B — Insurance as a Risk Management Tool

#### 14.1 Definition

- **Insurance:** a formal agreement between two parties, the insurer and the insured.
- The insurer commits to indemnify or compensate the insured for specified losses from specified perils.
- It allows the business owner to **transfer the risk** to an insurance company.

#### 14.2 Types of Insurance

| Type | What it covers |

### 2. Decision Making — 7.2

- Decisions are based on thinking and on knowledge accumulated through education and experience.
- If the decision fails, the problem is solved poorly or not solved.
- **Definition:** decision making is a **process**. It is a series of steps toward an end, with choices when identifying the problem, gathering information, and evaluating alternatives.
- A deliberate, step-by-step process serves to:
  1. Avoid errors of judgment.
  2. Raise vital questions.
  3. Gather and evaluate the relevant information.
  4. Recognize and evaluate assumptions, implications, and consequences.
  5. Reach well-reasoned conclusions.
  6. Achieve commitment and understanding.
  7. Solve the problem at hand.

#### 2.1 Critical Thinking — 7.2.1

- **Definition:** a process that uses knowledge and intelligence to arrive at the most reasonable and justifiable positions on an issue.
- It requires identifying and overcoming the many obstacles to thinking.
- It is high-level thinking. It is demanded more in positions of greater responsibility.
- It shapes the organization and supports its initiatives at the strategic level.
- It creates knowledge and improves organizational learning. That learning serves strategic, conceptual, innovative, and integrative work.

**What critical thinking is NOT:**

| It is not… | In reality… |
| --- | --- |
| Looking for faults or defects, or being smarter | It is a neutral and impartial process for evaluating claims, opinions, and facts |
| Making everyone think alike | Education, experience, and intelligence vary. Objectivity is somewhat subjective because it depends on values and principles |
| A threat to individuality or personality | It is a personal process |
| A belief system of a few | It is a way of evaluating the validity of beliefs and a different way of thinking |
| Only an intellectual skill | The most intelligent are not always the best critical thinkers |
| Something that discourages or replaces feelings | Emotions must be acknowledged. Without emotion there is no humility, empathy, courage, perseverance, or integrity |
| A scientific process that arrives at the most persuasive arguments | — |
| A creator of unquestionable absolutes | It offers generalizations and adapts when new evidence appears |
| A linear, step-by-step process that guarantees "truth" or correct conclusions | It does not gather all available information. It can produce unjustified inferences or erroneous conclusions |

- **Exam note:** do not confuse. Decision making is presented as a step-by-step process. Critical thinking, by contrast, is **not** linear and does not guarantee correct conclusions.

### 3. What Strategy Is — 7.3

- The concept has **military roots**. It consists of analyzing the (competitive) situation and designing a (strategic) response.
- **Basic definition:** a set of goal-oriented plans and actions that allows a company to compete effectively.
- Other definitions cited:

| Author | Definition of strategy |
| --- | --- |
| Barney (2015) | "A theory about how to achieve competitive advantage" |
| Thompson (2016) | The set of actions taken by a company's managers to outperform the competition |

- It is an action plan that takes the company to its desired market position. It serves to attract customers and achieve financial and market goals.
- It must say what to do and **what not to do**. Knowing what not to do is just as important.
- Wrong strategic moves cause operational distraction, organizational confusion, and loss of time and resources.

### 4. Evaluating the External Environment — 7.4

- Analyzing the macro environment is essential for security professionals. It requires four capabilities:
  1. Recognize the macro environment factors with strategic weight, such as risks. Failing to examine risks often leads to poor decisions.
  2. Use analytical tools to diagnose the competitive conditions of the industry.
  3. Map the market positions of the key groups of rivals.
  4. Use several frameworks to know whether the industry offers attractive opportunities for growth and profitability.
- **Macro environment:** the broad context of the industry. Its strategic components are beyond the company's direct control, which can only react.
- Reacting to competitors requires value judgments about what to watch. No analysis is perfect and forecasts are not facts. The analysis must be flexible and adaptable.
- The external analysis begins with the **six components of the macro environment (Walker 2011)**. They can have a positive or negative impact and no company controls them:
  1. Economic.
  2. Demographic.
  3. Socio-cultural.
  4. Political-legal.
  5. Technological.
  6. International-global.

#### 4.1 External Competition — 7.4.1

- The type of competition in an industry depends on the external threats. Economists distinguish four types:

| Type | Level of threats | Traits according to the book | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Perfect competition | Very high | By contrast with monopolistic competition, the products are understood to be homogeneous | — |
| Monopolistic competition | Intermediate | Many firms. Low cost of entry and exit. Products are **not** homogeneous in cost or attributes. Advantage is gained by cost or by differentiation | Toothpaste, shampoo, golf balls |
| Oligopoly | Intermediate | Few firms. Homogeneous products. High costs of entry and exit. Firms can obtain competitive advantage | U.S. cars and steel in the 1950s. Today, U.S. breakfast cereal: **4 companies produce about 90%** |

### 5. The Internal Environment — 7.5

- The internal analysis indicates whether a strategy is likely to yield an advantage over rivals.
- Together with the external analysis, it shows how to seize new opportunities and face emerging competitive threats.
- The nexus between the external and internal environment makes it possible to evaluate whether resources and capabilities make the operating strategy work.
- Even if there are opportunities in the industry, without the right resources and capabilities the company will not succeed.
- **Organizational capabilities:** they are not just possessing resources. They are the routines and processes that transform resources (*inputs*) into products or services (*outputs*).
- **Case:** a pharmaceutical company buys ingredients (inputs) and combines them into a drug (outputs). Its purity and regulatory compliance tests demonstrate its capability. Those tests lead to approval, marketing, and profit.

### 6. Business-Level and Corporate-Level Strategies — 7.6 and 7.7

|  | Business-level strategy | Corporate-level strategy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Definition | Course of action, the result of management decisions, to compete successfully in **a single** product market or industry | Actions to gain competitive advantage by operating in **several** markets or industries at once (Porter 1996) |
| Focus | Delivering more value to the customer than rivals | Portfolio: looks at all the company's businesses to create the greatest value |
| What it analyzes | The value chain activities it must do differently from its rivals | How the businesses fit together, how they affect each other, and how to structure the parent company to optimize human capital, processes, and governance |
| Relationship | It is the foundation | It is built on the business-level strategy |
| Complexity | Lower | Higher. It includes decisions on how to grow in a multibusiness company |

- **"More value" for the customer can mean three things:**
  1. A good product at a lower price.
  2. A superior product worth paying more for.
  3. A product with an attractive combination of price, features, service, and other attributes.
- The company must build resources and capabilities that rivals cannot easily match. It cannot count on rivals being inept or inert.

### 7. International Business — 7.8

- In the past, international strategies were the province of a few risk-taking individuals and companies. Today large and small companies use them, because the opportunities can be substantial.
- These strategies must exploit resources and capabilities that are **valuable, rare, and costly to imitate**.
- The company must also be well organized to take advantage of its full potential, depending on the moment and the context.
- **Five reasons to expand outside the domestic market:**
  1. Access new customers.
  2. Lower costs through economies of scale, experience, and greater purchasing power.
  3. Access low-cost production inputs.
  4. Exploit its own competencies further.
  5. Access resources and capabilities located in foreign markets.

### 8. Building an Organization Capable of Good Strategy Execution — 7.9

|  | Crafting the strategy | Executing the strategy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nature | Driven by **analysis** | Driven by **operations** |
| Focus | Market conditions, company resources and capabilities | Management of people, business processes, and organizational structure |
| Skills | Different from those of execution | Different from those of crafting |

- **Successful execution depends on six things:**
  1. Working with and through others.
  2. Building and strengthening competitive capabilities.
  3. Creating an appropriate organizational structure.
  4. Allocating resources.
  5. Implementing policies, processes, and systems that support the strategy.
  6. Instilling discipline to achieve results.
- **Executing tests the manager's ability to (Thompson 2016):**
  1. Lead organizational change.
  2. Achieve improvements in daily operations.
  3. Create and cultivate a culture that supports execution.
  4. Meet or exceed performance goals.
- **For the whole organization to act, management must:**
  1. Communicate convincingly the new strategy and its reasons.
  2. Overcome the doubts that will arise.
  3. Secure the commitment of key personnel.
  4. Build consensus on how to implement it.
  5. Move forward until all the pieces are in place and the results are achieved.
- **Common cause of dysfunction:** senior executives do not recognize that announcing a strategy does not make people adopt it.
- Even in successful organizations, employees doubt notable changes.
- Employees need and deserve to know the logic of the decisions and where execution will take them.
- The explanation must convince them that the change is the best option and that the path and the destination are worthwhile.

### 9. Managing Internal Operations: Promoting Good Strategy Execution — 7.10

- At the start of implementation, managers decide what resources are needed and how to distribute them among the units.
- That includes screening requests for staff, equipment, and other resources. Those that contribute to execution are approved and the rest are rejected.
- Funding initiatives costs money. If internal cash flow is not enough, there are two avenues: **borrowing** or **selling additional shares** to investors.
- The ability to gather resources greatly influences execution:

| Insufficient funding | Overfunding |
| --- | --- |
| Slows progress | Wastes resources |
| Prevents success | Reduces financial performance |
| — | Sends the wrong message to other managers, who will demand their "fair share" |

- Managers must thoroughly review budgets and direct the "right" amount to the units critical to the strategy.
- The "fair share" and the "right" amount are defined with **critical thinking and objective data**. Not with a "political" and subjective decision (Thompson 2016).
- Policies and procedures can support or hinder execution.
- Improving execution often requires changes in work practices. It is necessary to evaluate whether current policies support those changes, and to revise or discard them if not.

### 10. Corporate Culture and Its Impact on Execution — 7.11

- **Corporate culture:** the character or "personality" of the work environment. It describes how the company does business and what behaviors it values.
- It is considered the company's **"DNA"**. Some traits are evident and others act subtly.
- **Where to look for an organization's culture (Thompson et al. 2016):**
  1. The stated values, business principles, and ethical standards that management preaches and practices. Deeds weigh more than words.
  2. The approach to managing people and the official policies, procedures, and practices that guide expected conduct.
  3. The atmosphere and spirit of the work climate, in person or virtual. It can be competitive or cooperative, innovative or resistant to change, collegial or politicized, strictly business or fun.
  4. How managers and employees relate to each other:
     - whether they work independently or collaboratively;
     - whether communication flows freely or is scarce;
     - whether they call each other by first name;
     - whether they spend little or much time together outside work.
  5. The strength of peer pressure to conform to norms.
  6. The actions and behaviors that management encourages and rewards, and those it frowns upon.
  7. The revered traditions and the oft-repeated stories about "heroic acts" and "how we do things here".
  8. The treatment of external stakeholders:
     - vendors as partners or tough, distant relationships;
     - real commitment or not to corporate citizenship and environmental sustainability.
- **Origin of the culture:** it can come from any level of the hierarchy.
  - Typical: a founder or strong leaders who express it as principles, policies, operating approaches, and ways of treating customers, vendors, shareholders, and local communities.
  - Also: exemplary actions by staff and an evolving consensus on "how we should do things here".
  - Over time these foundations take root, everyone expects them, and they become ingrained in the way of doing business.
- **Two roles of the stated core values and ethical principles:**
  1. If truly practiced, they create a climate of shared convictions about how to do business.
  2. They guide staff on which behaviors are approved, expected, or unacceptable. They serve as a yardstick for actions and decisions.
- The result sought: **doing things right** and **doing the right thing**.

## Capítulo 8 — Crisis Management (págs. 131–140)

**Central idea:** Emergency management, crisis management, and continuity management are often used as synonyms, but they are not. Emergency management addresses the immediate impact; crisis management is strategic and protects viability and reputation; continuity returns operations to normal. Today they are grouped under the umbrella of resilience.

### 1. Three Distinct Terms and the Resilience Umbrella

- Emergency management, crisis management, and business continuity management are often used interchangeably.
- Today it is understood that a business can be affected by more than natural disasters. That is why "crisis management" and "business continuity", which are more inclusive, are preferred.
- **Resilience:** an umbrella term that today groups risk management, security management, and crisis management programs.

|  | Emergency management / response | Crisis management | Business continuity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | Planning and activity to detect, contain, and address the **immediate impact** of the event | **Strategic** process that supports emergency response and continuity, and addresses what threatens long-term viability | Processes and procedures to move from operating during the crisis to **normal operation** |
| Focus | Lives, property, containment | Aftermath, reputation, brand, and nonoperational aspects | Resuming critical functions |
| Examples from the book | Putting out the fire in a blaze. Isolating the network in a cyber breach | Product recall. Regulatory, legal, or financial matters | Recovery in place, contracting out, or relocation |

### 2. Emergency Management — 8.1

- **Definition:** planning and activity to detect, contain, and address the immediate impact of an event.
- Many methodologies divide the model into **four cyclical elements**.
- The model was originally created for **U.S. government agencies**. It is applied to governments and companies around the world, with variations in terminology and implementation.

| Phase | What it is | Examples / notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mitigation | Protective measures to reduce the probability of a disaster or its impact if it occurs. It reduces damage **before** the event | Flood barriers. Fire-rated structural elements. Bolting cabinets to the wall in seismic zones |
| Preparedness | Activities, programs, and systems created **before** the incident to support and improve mitigation, response, and recovery | Applies to disruptions, disasters, or emergencies |
| Response | Executing the plan and providing services to preserve and protect life and property, and to care for the surviving population | The continuity response must support the mission, consider stakeholders' interests, and assess the impact of the disruption |
| Recovery | Restoring processes, resources, and capabilities to meet ongoing operational requirements | May include major organizational improvements, even redirecting strategic or operational objectives |

#### 2.1 Emergency and Response Teams — 8.1.1

- Response personnel must be ready for all types of emergencies, including those not anticipated when the plan was written.
- Primary training focuses on:
  - the most likely situations;
  - the location, construction, size, and function of each site.
- Large organizations with several locations:
  - need an overall **emergency operations plan (EOP)** to coordinate all sites;
  - also need specific plans for the particular exposures of each site.
- Defining teams and structure in advance lets each member understand their role, train for it, and review it by consulting the plan during the event.

#### 2.2 Emergency Response Planning — 8.1.2

- The plan does not cover all types or details of each event. Even so, forming the team and writing the plan provides a framework for responding systematically.
- The format depends on the nature of the organization and its policy.

|  | All-hazards approach | Highly specific plans |
| --- | --- | --- |
| When it is suitable | Organizations exposed to many types of threats and hazards. It is the **best option** for them | When the type of business requires different plans for each emergency |
| Structure | A basic EOP with common sections (team and contact lists) + checklists by type of threat | Separate plans by type of event |
| Examples | Hurricane checklist in coastal areas. Flood section in flood-prone areas | A hospital: earthquake plan different from the plan for a high-level contagion outbreak |
| Why it works | The requirements are similar whether the threat is natural, human, or accidental. An evacuation plan works equally for fire, bomb, utility shutdowns, or hazardous materials (HAZMAT) spills | — |

- Each organization decides what suits it.
- Whatever the format, the plan must be **as simple as possible**, with the specific responsibilities of each person assigned.

### 3. Crisis Management — 8.2

- **Definition:** a **strategic** process that supports emergency response and continuity. At the same time, it addresses the matters that can affect the organization's long-term viability.
- It includes handling the aftermath of a destructive event and the effects of the crisis, including reputational impact and other nonoperational aspects.
- It is part of the response and recovery program.
- **It begins when any disruptive event is reported:**
  - physical emergencies;
  - nonphysical events: product recall, regulatory, legal, or financial matters.
- It is **holistic**: all functions of the organization take part in the response.
- The impact on brand and reputation requires coordination with executive management and the public-facing communications teams.
- **Ultimate goal:** to protect the core assets from the greatest possible harm caused by an event that disrupts the business:
  1. Reputation.
  2. Brand.
  3. Financial well-being.
  4. Trust.
  5. Physical and intellectual property.
  6. Key relationships.
- It must keep the company fulfilling its mission while it recovers, already in the continuity and recovery phase.
- **A sound program:**
  - prepares all functions to support all affected levels;
  - is not dominated by a single functional area.

#### 3.1 Crisis Management Teams — 8.2.1

- The **crisis management team (CMT)** is perhaps the most critical piece of crisis management.
- **First step** of the program: naming the critical personnel who will handle any disruptive event.
- **CMT members (8 areas):**
  1. Top management.
  2. Human resources.
  3. Public affairs / communications.
  4. Safety / security.
  5. Information technology (IT).
  6. Legal.
  7. Finance or other shared service.
  8. Critical operating departments.
- In large organizations, the **senior leadership team** is usually separate from the CMT.
  - The senior leadership team focuses on decisions that affect strategic goals.
  - It then passes those decisions down to the CMT to implement.
- Members change depending on the organization. They must reflect the groups most critical to continuing operations.
- Effective planning includes all the areas that may be activated if needed.
- **They are identified by position, with a primary and alternates.** That way, if the primary is absent, the team knows who can cover.
- An organization may have a single crisis team. Large ones may need tiered teams:

| Type of team | Level | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Operational | One functional segment | Recovers only that area of operation | Manufacturing crisis team. Delivery and logistics team |
| Tactical | Close to the crisis, e.g., at the geographic location of the event | Carries out the tactical recovery instructions | — |
| Strategic | Management or executive level | Overall strategic command of the response and recovery | — |

#### 3.2 Crisis Management Planning — 8.2.2

- Crisis management begins when any disruptive event is reported, physical or nonphysical.
- Security managers can design and implement measures to protect **people, information, and capabilities** against crises.
- The word "emergency" immediately brings evacuating to mind. But each type of crisis has a different ideal response.
- The general flow is similar in all crises, but a plan is needed for each type.
- The plan is adapted to the needs of the business and its mission. **Typical aspects of a crisis plan:**
  1. Crisis management team.
  2. Activation and escalation of crisis management.
  3. Crisis command and management succession.
  4. Recovery logistics and resources.
  5. Crisis communications.

### 4. Business Continuity Management (BCM) — 8.3

- **Business continuity:** processes and procedures to move from operating during the crisis to operating normally after a disruption.
- **Possible strategies, depending on the nature and needs of the business:**
  1. Resumption and recovery in place.
  2. Contracting out selected functions.
  3. Relocating critical functions and personnel to one or more sites, owned by the company or contracted from third parties.
- **Objective:** to resume critical functions as soon as possible. Restore the business to its pre-emergency condition and location. If that is not possible, move it to a new location or level of operation.
- **There is no single formula** for a BCM plan. Good practices and frameworks are adapted to:
  1. the culture;
  2. the available resources;
  3. the industry;
  4. the complexity of the business;
  5. the company's level of maturity.
- Continuity plans document the instructions that the BCM team follows to recover its area.
- **Goals of continuity planning (7):**
  1. Save lives and reduce the possibility of further injury or death.
  2. Protect assets.
  3. Restore critical business processes and systems.
  4. Reduce the duration of the interruption.
  5. Protect against reputational damage.
  6. Control media coverage (local, regional, national, or global).
  7. Maintain customer relationships.
- **Content of the plans (9):**
  1. Team members.
  2. Alternate locations or transportation needs.
  3. Checklists for preparing for a disruption.
  4. Checklists for responding during a disruption.
  5. Checklists for returning to normal operation after a disruption.
  6. Lists of needed resources.
  7. Communication routes and templates.
  8. Financial considerations.
  9. Plans for specific disruptions that require a special response.

#### 4.1 Business Continuity Management Teams — 8.3.1

- They are critical: they execute the plan to recover activities after an event.
- **Their participants must:**
  1. Be formally named to the team.
  2. Contribute to the design of the plans.
  3. Know their role in the plan.
  4. Have the capacity to respond when needed.
  5. Know when and where to report for recovery.
  6. Be evaluated with plan exercises on a regular basis.

#### 4.2 Developing Plans and Procedures — 8.3.2

- Developing a plan is part of the broader **risk management** process.
- Plans must:
  - be written in the context of the organization;
  - be tested and updated continuously;
  - go through the planning cycle.
- **Risk assessment:** identifies, evaluates, and prioritizes threats and vulnerabilities and their possible impact. It helps to understand operational needs and to focus continuity.
- **Before starting any part of the risk cycle, identify:**
  1. The company's mission.
  2. Its goals.
  3. Its critical functions and assets.
  4. The stakeholder who makes the risk decisions.
- Without knowing what must be protected, the company cannot be protected.
- To choose prevention and response measures, likelihood and consequences must be estimated:

| Element | How it is estimated |
| --- | --- |
| Likelihood of an incident | By evaluating the organization's changing profile and the security measures already in place |
| Consequences | Based on knowledge of the assets at risk |

- The assessment must be done **with enough time** to develop and practice procedures before they are needed.
- It is reviewed when the environment changes and when security plans and site surveys are prepared.
- Each risk usually requires several preventive measures, and each measure can cover several risks.
- A **threat evaluation team** can be created to evaluate all the threats the organization receives.

### 5. “Crisis Communication” — 8.4

- **Exam note:** the title says “crisis communication”, but the content deals with the **planning process**, not communication.
- Making an emergency plan takes a lot of time.
  - Software greatly reduces the work of capturing information.
  - Emergency management consultants help make the plan complete.
  - **Neither replaces** the responsible participation of the management team.
- Management must participate directly in identifying and evaluating the organization's assets. This is how the key assets to be protected are identified.
- The managers of daily operations make the risk analysis more manageable.
- **Purpose of the emergency plan** (as in any part of an ESRM, Enterprise Security Risk Management, program):
  - to show the problems that decision makers and key personnel will face;
  - to force them to think **in advance** about how to react.
- Frequent mistake: plans, if made, are put on a shelf and forgotten.
- **For a plan to be effective:**
  1. It must reflect the requirements of its own organization.
  2. Every person with responsibilities must understand them clearly.
  3. Those people must be trained to fulfill them.
  4. It must be tested with practice.
  5. It must be revised according to the results of those tests.
- An exercise or an actual activation can show the need to revise the plan, reassign responsibilities, or retrain personnel. It is then tested again.
- **Most important:** planning is a continuous process that never ends as long as the plan exists.

### 6. General Response to a Crisis — 8.5

- **Categories of crisis (4):**
  1. Natural or environmental.
  2. Human.
  3. Active.
  4. Cyber-based.
- The organization prepares for all of them with its continuity plans and general response. Some threats require specific actions.
- Each crisis may require crisis management, emergency response, and/or continuity plans.
- **Exam note:** the section is titled “general response”, but from here on it focuses on the **active assailant** event.
- **Response sequence:**
  1. Warnings, notifications, and communications are issued to the affected employees.
  2. Those warnings include the initial actions: evacuation, shelter-in-place, or lockdown.
  3. The **threat management team** coordinates and manages the overall response.
  4. It oversees internal and external responders, especially law enforcement in an active assailant situation.
  5. It establishes a perimeter to contain the incident and prevent entry to the danger zone.
- **Exam note:** the *threat management team* appears here without being defined or related to the CMT.
- Before the response, the organization will already have:
  - developed policies and procedures for this type of incident;
  - trained responders in their roles, often with specialized training, especially security personnel;
  - held drills, exercises, and tests, from tabletop exercises to full-scale exercises.
- The threat management team participates during the **entire event** and in the **later recovery phase**.

**Pre-incident considerations (9):**

1. Define the members of the threat management team.
2. Establish roles, responsibilities, procedures, and training for key response personnel.
3. Create emergency alert, warning, and notification systems using several methods and media.
4. Conduct awareness, education, and training programs for employees, with attention to:
   - warning signs of workplace violence;
   - reporting procedures;
   - the organization's guidance on the “run, hide, fight” protocol.
5. Provide response equipment and materials to key personnel:
   - portable radios;
   - flashlights;
   - keys and access cards;
   - property and floor plans;
   - bleeding control supplies.
6. Coordinate with outside public safety agencies: police, fire department, and ambulances.
7. Choose an **emergency operations center (EOC)** and an **incident command center (ICC)** to direct, coordinate, and communicate with internal and external responders.
8. Establish a **media staging area** and a **family notification and reunification area**, secure and away from operations.
9. Consider serious psychological factors, including trauma, in the preplanning, operation, post-incident recovery, and continuity phases.

**Post-incident considerations:**

| Phase | Actions |
| --- | --- |
| Immediate | Safety of lives. Account for all persons on site. Communication with employees, families, the public, and the media |
| Short-term | Preserve the crime scene. Watch for signs of psychological trauma. Help with reunification and other incident tasks. Keep communicating information |
| Long-term | Post-incident debrief with internal and external responders and stakeholders. Replenish supplies. Keep communicating with the appropriate groups |

## Capítulo 9 — Investigations (págs. 141–154)

**Central idea:** An investigation gathers and documents facts in a logical, methodical, and legal way to reach an objective conclusion. In the private sector its purpose is to protect the interests of the company, not the public good. The chapter covers the management of the investigations unit, three types of investigation, and the tools: interview, evidence, testimony, force multipliers, and reports.

### 1. Investigation Management (9.1)

#### 1.1 Definition of investigation

- It is a process for gathering and documenting information in a **logical, methodical, and lawful** way (logically, methodically, lawfully).
- Its aim is to **objectively** develop a **reasonable conclusion** based on the facts obtained.
- The investigator may not be asked for conclusions. Their results and interviews guide others, for example legal counsel.
- The definition applies equally to the public and private sectors.
- It ranges from background checks to administrative inquiries and criminal matters.

**Exam note:** the definition speaks of "developing a conclusion," but the same paragraph admits that the investigator may not be asked to conclude.

#### 1.2 Purposes of an investigation (source: Webster University 2020)

In the corporate environment, the purpose falls into one or more of these 8 categories:

1. Document incidents.
2. Identify the cause of undesirable situations when nefarious activity is suspected.
3. Document and correlate facts about misconduct or inappropriate behavior.
4. Identify, interview, or surveil suspects of a crime or of misconduct.
5. Gather information that proves or refutes an allegation.
6. Enable a decision to be made about a person or an organization.
7. Conduct threat assessments to prevent workplace violence, whether internal or from third parties.
8. Collect data on crimes and other material to mitigate the company's liability and risks.

#### 1.3 Purpose at two levels

| Level | What the purpose does |
| --- | --- |
| Case level | Provides the context for the investigation and keeps the team focused. |
| Strategic level | Dictates planning, organization, equipment, staffing, and preparation. |

#### 1.4 Five attributes of an effective and reliable investigation (9.1.1)

Objectivity · thoroughness · relevance · accuracy · timeliness.

- Management must ensure them at the strategic level and in each individual case.

#### 1.5 Public sector vs. private sector

The tools, techniques, and processes are similar. The difference is one of substance.

| Aspect | Public (generally law enforcement) | Private (private security) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Why it exists | To serve and protect society at large | To protect the interests of the employing company |
| How it is measured | Number of arrests and convictions | Recovery, restitution, mitigation of litigation, and risk reduction |
| Scope | A specific criminal act or violation | Broader: violations of internal policies and systemic problems that justify changing processes or policies |

- The difference affects four things: legal authority, resource allocation, program management, and investigation outcomes.

#### 1.6 Use of third parties

- Organizations sometimes hire outside firms for their external or internal investigations.
- In many jurisdictions, the public sector sometimes hires a private company for a specific expertise.
  - Example: forensic accounting within a police investigation.
- Private investigative units (IU) also use outside experts.
  - Example: acts or threats of workplace violence.
  - Reason: threat assessment and intervention interviews with the person of interest require experience that the team rarely has.

#### 1.7 Management functions and levels of investigative management

- Management is crucial to the success of security and criminal investigations in the company.
- Five basic functions: planning, organizing, directing, coordinating, and controlling.
- All five apply at each of the three levels:

| Level | What it covers |
| --- | --- |
| Strategic | High-level management of the entire unit and its relationship with senior management. Legal counsel must take part here. This is how investigations are focused according to company policy or civil or criminal law. |
| Operational | Technical aspects of the investigative function and how it works within its department. |
| Case | Each investigation: its investigators, techniques, and case management protocols. |

#### 1.8 Goals and objectives

Both are critical at all three levels.

|  | Goals | Objectives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Horizon | Long term; general end states | Reviewed fairly often, normally every year |
| Form | Can be expressed in general terms | Include performance metrics and improvement targets for the next period |
| Fit | Consistent with the organization's strategic direction. Incorporated into the IU's mission statement | They are the third level of defining the unit (see section 3) |

### 2. Ethics (9.1.2)

- Ethics transcends the three levels of investigative management.
- Three high-impact organizational measures:
  1. Publish written standards of ethical conduct.
  2. Adopt a zero-tolerance policy toward ethical lapses.
  3. Impose sanctions on those who commit them.
- Ethical lapses have always affected public and private investigators.
- Without sound ethical standards, the effectiveness of the IU or the investigator is seriously damaged.
- Reason: they depend on their credibility and respectability to gather, corroborate, and integrate facts.

### 3. Organization of the IU (9.1.3)

- There is no typical organizational structure.
- It depends on the industry and on the company's mission, size, and scope.

**Three levels of defining the unit:**

| Level | Document | Who / frequency | Content |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Functional charter | Issued by the CEO (Chief Executive Officer) or an equivalent position. Gives the IU credibility and authority | Concise document. Purpose and direction of the function; strategic goals; who is responsible; nature and main emphasis; essential internal relationships with the rest of the organization |
| 2 | Policy statement | The book does not say | Procedures for initiating and conducting investigations; reporting channels; coordination mechanisms; disposition |
| 3 | Objectives | Reviewed fairly often, normally every year | Performance metrics and improvement targets for the next period |

**Hiring investigators:** weigh professional qualifications and personal traits. The book gives 14 criteria:

1. Education and training.
2. Certification.
3. General and specialized experience.
4. Communication skills.
5. Ethical standards.
6. Persistence.
7. Balance.
8. Maturity.
9. Ability to deal effectively with people.
10. Self-motivation.
11. Multitasking ability.
12. Professional demeanor.
13. Observational skills.
14. Flexibility.

### 4. Investigation Process (9.1.4)

Four phases:

1. **Initiation.** Starts with an incident report, a complaint, a discrepancy report, or a request for investigation. Includes the reason and the mechanism for opening the case.
2. **The investigation itself.** It can be of five types:
   - administrative inquiry;
   - criminal investigation;
   - incident investigation;
   - background check;
   - due diligence investigation.
3. **Reporting of findings** and later use of the information. Most investigative information is formally retained.
4. **Use of the results.** For statistical analysis, program evaluation, strategic planning, and budgetary forecasting. The book clarifies that these are only some examples.

**Exam note:** the book only formally names phase 1 ("initiation"). Phases 3 and 4 overlap: phase 3 already includes the "later use" of the information, which is precisely the subject of phase 4.

### 5. Legal Issues (9.1.5)

- Aim: protect the company from legal liability.
- Also: reach a valid conclusion if the case goes to a court or other legal forum.

**Other legal considerations (4):**

1. Comply with the laws on licensing of private investigators and on the conduct of private investigations.
2. Meet regulatory reporting requirements in all applicable jurisdictions.
3. Risk of civil lawsuits on five grounds:
   - defamation;
   - false imprisonment;
   - false arrest;
   - retaliation;
   - harassment.
4. Possibility of subpoenas to testify, to hand over written records of the investigation, or both.

**Jurisdiction and venue:**

- The head of the IU must ensure that the organization knows the applicable laws, regulations, and corporate policies.
- The task grows if the company has locations in several jurisdictions or countries.
- The trial may be held outside the expected jurisdiction. For example, where:
  - the corporate headquarters is located;
  - the incident occurred (it may be off company property);
  - the complainant is located;
  - there is some other place linked to the incident.
- Laws, regulations, and restrictions may vary among those jurisdictions.

#### 5.1 Information Protection

- When the investigation is closed, its content and outcome are permanent.
- If the information is compromised, it can:
  1. damage the future effectiveness of the investigators or the IU;
  2. create civil liability for the organization;
  3. damage people's reputations and careers;
  4. damage important liaison relationships.
- Mishandling feeds the rumor mill. That harms the investigation, the stock price, market share, and corporate reputation.
- Privacy laws also count. Example: the European Union (EU) GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation).
  - It heavily restricts which personal data are collected, shared, and accessed by others.
  - It seeks to protect people's privacy.
  - It affects even pre-employment background investigations.
  - It applies even if the company or the investigator is not in the EU.

### 6. Undercover Investigations (9.2)

#### 6.1 Definition and nature

- **Definition:** surreptitious placement of a well-trained, skilled investigator in an unsuspecting workforce.
  - Poses as an employee, vendor, or customer.
  - Main aim: gather information.
- They are covert operations by nature. They are complicated and do not work in every environment.
- **Key difference from other investigations:** the undercover investigator does not hide their presence or try to go unnoticed.
- What is secret (sub-rosa) is the investigator's true identity, who is called an operative, or the purpose of the inquiry.
- Together with the interview, it is a form of **interactive investigation**.
  - It makes it possible to learn not only what is happening, but the "how" and the "why" of what the people under investigation do.

**Exam note:** it seems contradictory to say "covert" and at the same time "does not hide their presence." It is not: the identity or purpose is hidden, not the presence.

#### 6.2 Restricted use

- It must be the exception, not the rule. It is considered only when there are no other options.
- It seems simple, but it is complicated and demanding.
- It carries the risk of legal challenges and liability for the employer and for the investigator.
- It requires the company to invest time, patience, and resources.
- How it unfolds depends on the nature of the information sought.

#### 6.3 When to use it (6 typical situations)

| # | Situation |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Consistent, reliable information suggests misconduct or a crime, but details are lacking to prevent it or identify those involved. |
| 2 | There are losses in a specific area, but it is not known how they occur or who is responsible. |
| 3 | Management strongly suspects alcohol consumption, drug abuse, or drug dealing at work, or has already detected indicators. |
| 4 | Alcohol or drugs affect performance, and supervisors do not react or are unable to intervene. |
| 5 | Actual practices must be compared with required policies, and routine auditing is not possible. |
| 6 | Management believes that infiltrating operatives will yield significant results, and all other reasonable options have already been ruled out. |

#### 6.4 When NOT to use it

- General rule: never to gather personal or confidential information.
- Nor to invade anyone's private life where there is a reasonable expectation of privacy.
- That is actionable against the employer and its investigators.
- Three specific circumstances in which it should not be considered:
  1. Investigating activities permitted or protected by law, rule, or government regulation.
  2. Replacing another form of investigation that would probably yield the same results.
  3. Investigating protected union activities.

#### 6.5 Main risk: exposure / compromise

- Having it discovered during or after the investigation is usually undesirable.

| Who finds out | Consequence |
| --- | --- |
| Employees, before it ends (for example, through a management announcement) | Morale and productivity drop |
| Customers or vendors | Resentment at having been part of what was investigated. They may cut off the relationship or stop buying |
| The public, if the investigation was compromised | More resentment and adverse publicity |

- These factors must be weighed before approving an undercover investigation.

### 7. Due Diligence Investigations (9.3)

#### 7.1 Definition and standard

- **Due diligence:** in a broad sense, "doing one's homework" on a subject.
- A matter, person, deal, or other thing is investigated to find out whether it is legitimate.
- It is useful whenever one party decides to act or not to act based on existing conditions or on representations by another party.
- **There is no absolute standard.** The standard depends on 5 factors:
  1. the objective of the investigation;
  2. the specific situation;
  3. the jurisdictions or geographic areas involved;
  4. the related privacy laws;
  5. the time frame.
- There are general suggestions, but almost every case is unique and calls for its own approach.

#### 7.2 Due diligence vs. negligence

| Due diligence | Negligence |
| --- | --- |
| The positive side of due care | Describes the lack of that care |

- Failing to exercise due diligence can cause legal damages if another party is harmed, just like negligent conduct.
- It is also costly to whoever omits it, even if no one is harmed.
  - Possible losses: money, reputation, opportunities, and time, through poor business decisions.
- It is a **proactive** investigative process. It supports security risk management decisions.
- One of its most important reasons: to protect the organization's assets.
- Not knowing how legitimate and trustworthy someone or something is greatly increases the risk of any transaction or decision.

#### 7.3 Due diligence report

- Its form depends on the purpose and on the intended audience.
- Always in writing. The investigator must be ready to defend it.
- Content (7 elements):
  1. identity of the client and the provider;
  2. purpose and background;
  3. scope;
  4. general methodology;
  5. findings;
  6. conclusion;
  7. legal disclaimer, with the limitations of the investigation.
- Confidentiality and dissemination controls are critical to the integrity of the report.
- They are agreed **in advance** between client and provider.

### 8. Background Investigations (9.4)

#### 8.1 Why they matter

- A single bad hire can greatly damage productivity, profits, reputation, and morale.
  - The book gives three examples: a thief, a drug user, or a violent offender.
- A broad, lawful, and fairly applied program of background investigation and pre-employment screening helps to:
  - prevent losses;
  - mitigate the damage of a bad hire.

#### 8.2 Four similar but distinct terms

They are used as synonyms, but they are related and distinct disciplines.

| Term | Nature | What it includes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Background check | Confirmatory: verifies what the candidate declares | Education and work history |
| Background investigation or screening | Active search: looks for what the candidate might hide (criminal activity, prior dismissals) | Developing references and searching for criminal records. Ranges from checking previous employment and degrees to checking civil, criminal, and medical history |
| Preemployment screening | Determines whether the candidate is trustworthy or able to perform the duties of the position | Everything above, plus the tests in the list below |
| Personal risk assessment (PRA) | **Pending:** the book does not define it | **Pending:** the book does not define it |

**Exam note:** the PRA appears in the list of terms, but the chapter never explains what it is.

**Preemployment screening may also include (6 tests):**

1. Drug or alcohol tests.
2. Psychological tests (personality and integrity testing).
3. General intelligence or aptitude tests.
4. Medical review and physical ability tests.
5. Polygraph.
6. Job-specific tests.

- The legality of each test, and the stage at which it is administered, is heavily regulated in many places.
- The recommended course is to consult legal counsel.

#### 8.3 Liability for improper hiring

- Screening helps minimize the risk of liability for improper hiring.
- It is well established: the employer is responsible for the negligence of its employees on duty or in work-related activity.
- There is also precedent for blaming it for **intentional** employee misconduct.
  - Condition: that it knew or should have known of a background that foreshadowed it.
- Cases in the book:
  - Landlords held liable for hiring ex-convicts who attacked residents of the building.
  - Employers held liable for hiring people with criminal records who injured coworkers in ways similar to their earlier crimes.
- Lesson: if there were prior warning signs and no investigation was made, the company is liable.

### 9. Interviewing (9.5)

- The investigator's three tools are the "three I's":
  1. information;
  2. interrogation;
  3. instrumentation.
- The chapter focuses on the interview. Interrogation is associated with the police, not with private investigation.
- **Interview:** a question-and-answer session designed to elicit information.
- Interviews of victims, witnesses, informants, and confidential informants are the **most critical** aspect of the investigation.
  - Reason: they are a primary source of information.
- Location: it can be a private home, a street corner, or a formal interview room.
- Possible purposes:
  - learn a candidate's background;
  - obtain information from someone indirectly involved in the matter;
  - obtain a written statement from a witness.
- The quality of the interview is crucial to the success of the investigation and of any later litigation.

**Exam note:** the text announces that it will cover interview and interrogation. It then develops neither interrogation nor instrumentation.

### 10. Evidence (9.6)

#### 10.1 Definition and handling

- **Evidence:** provides proof or an indication of an assertion.
- Knowing how to collect it is useful. Even so, many IUs outsource collection and analysis.
  - They assign them to specialized firms, crime labs, or consultants.
  - Especially in the case of digital evidence.
- There is no universal evidence-handling standard. Each nation, state, jurisdiction, or agency has its own rules.
- The investigator must follow best practices.
- They must also know the rules of the jurisdiction, agency, or lab (public or private) that apply to the case.
- A single mistake can be costly.
- Evidence may not reach the court if the chain of custody is not preserved and the standards of that jurisdiction are not followed.

#### 10.2 Forms and categories

**Three forms:** oral, documentary, and physical.

**Two basic categories:**

| Category | Subtypes given by the book | What it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direct | Real and material | Firsthand knowledge |
| Indirect | Circumstantial and hearsay | Highly informed inference. It is inferred that something happened from testimony about a series of facts |

**Exam note:** the book says it will describe the oral, documentary, and physical forms "in detail," and does not. It also lists circumstantial as a subtype of indirect, and then says that indirect "is also known as" circumstantial.

#### 10.3 Role of the security professional after an incident

- Carefully gather (or oversee) the evidence, whether or not the police are involved.
- Uses of that evidence:
  1. establish a timeline;
  2. resolve the matter;
  3. analyze how the incident occurred;
  4. if needed, modify security measures to prevent further incidents.
- If it goes to trial, the judge decides the competency and materiality of the evidence.
  - Normally does so following case law.
- In non-criminal cases there may be more open interpretation when presenting evidence.
- In both criminal and non-criminal cases, the defense has a right to the information in the discovery phase.
- The investigator must recognize a scene staged on purpose to deceive the investigation.

#### 10.4 Physical evidence at trial: 3 requirements

1. Clearly show the chain of custody: the uninterrupted control of the evidence.
2. Correctly identify the evidence.
3. It must be relevant to the case before the court.

- These practices are always followed, even if the case is not going to go to court or to a hearing.

### 11. Testimony (9.7)

- Testimonial evidence is often a larger part of the evidence presented at trial.
- Security personnel often testify about the incidents they handle. Possible settings:
  - formal or informal regulatory hearings;
  - interviews by government authorities;
  - formal out-of-court statements (depositions);
  - grand jury;
  - civil or criminal courts at any level.
- The rules vary among countries, and even within the same country.
- Testimony must always be truthful.
- At the same time, it must follow the court's guidelines and the strategy of counsel.

**Types of witness.** Security personnel can be either of the two:

|  | Fact witness | Opinion witness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Other names | The book gives none | Expert witness or forensic witness |
| What they state | Only what they saw, heard, smelled, touched, or tasted (the five senses) | An opinion on the matter |
| Requirement | The book sets none | Demonstrate training, education, and experience in a specific subject |
| Basis | Their direct perception | Analysis of the evidence according to accepted scientific standards and academic research methods |

- Hearsay: relating what another person said. It may or may not be admissible.

### 12. Maximizing the Investigation (9.8)

#### 12.1 Force multipliers (9.8.1)

- **Definition:** tools that help the IU find and evaluate information faster.
- To maximize effectiveness, the IU must use every resource it can muster.
- The book names four:
  1. online information sources;
  2. liaison;
  3. intelligence information;
  4. technology.

**Exam note:** online information sources are named, but not developed.

**Liaison.** It is one of the most significant multipliers. It makes it possible to:

1. leverage the resources of others;
2. share best practices and lessons learned;
3. collaborate on specific cases;
4. better address common problems;
5. share information, equipment, and facilities.

- In short: do more with less.

**Joint task force (JTF).** It is a highly structured form of liaison.

- It is used for complex and often multijurisdictional issues:
  - retail theft;
  - terrorism;
  - cybercrime;
  - human trafficking;
  - major procurement fraud.
- It can be for a specific case or long-lasting.
- It usually provides special expertise and crosses jurisdictional boundaries.
- Possible members, according to the book:
  - international, federal, and local police;
  - industry resources;
  - auditors;
  - analysts;
  - consultants;
  - prosecutors;
  - "others" (the book itself leaves the list open).

| JTF diversity: positive side | JTF diversity: negative side |
| --- | --- |
| Provides many benefits (the book does not detail them) | Can cause misunderstandings, conflicts, and inefficiencies |

- The JTF must be managed to offset the negative effects and take advantage of the diversity.

**Intelligence information.**

- It is a key multiplier in almost any environment.
- Companies often lack an organized approach to managing it.
- The IU manager must create a formal intelligence management program.
- It must have policies, procedures, and systems for 9 functions:
  1. define general and specific objectives;
  2. gather intelligence information;
  3. integrate and analyze the information;
  4. organize and store the information;
  5. share it internally and externally, as appropriate;
  6. retrieve it;
  7. control access to the data;
  8. control access to information about methods and sources;
  9. periodically evaluate and improve the program.

**Advanced technology.** It is a multiplier because it provides:

1. faster data analysis and data fusion;
2. better knowledge management;
3. a more predictive approach;
4. more efficient dissemination of information.

#### 12.2 Investigative Documentation (9.8.2)

- **Investigative report:** a written document in which the investigator records the activity and the evidence gathered.
- Functions:
  - if it is not the final report, it can give leads to other investigators;
  - it is the basis of later administrative action, such as prosecution or disciplinary action.
- It must make clear right away what the investigator did, why they did it, and what resulted.

**Typical report types (4):**

| Type | When it is submitted | Content / rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Initial report | A few days after the investigation is opened | Progress and leads still to be followed, if any |
| Progress / interim report | At fixed intervals | Progress and leads still to be followed |
| Special report | When there is an out-of-the-ordinary action in the investigation | May supplement a progress report, but must stand on its own |
| Final report | In any of the 3 cases below | The book insists it is the most important one submitted |

**The final report is submitted when:**

1. the investigation is closed successfully;
2. all leads were followed without success and continuing is considered unproductive;
3. the person or office that authorized opening the case so orders.

**Distribution:** it is the final step of the report.

- Only to those with a real need to know.
- Reason: it may contain harmful information or statements about employees or other people.

**Exam note:** the book calls even prosecution "administrative action."

#### 12.3 Leveraging Investigative Results (9.8.3)

- The natural close of an investigation is usually:
  - gather all relevant facts;
  - document the findings;
  - resolve the disciplinary action or restitution.
- But in general it should not be the end.
- Investigations almost always reveal valuable information. They may uncover a condition or weakness that:
  1. allowed a crime to be committed;
  2. gave unauthorized access;
  3. created an opportunity for malicious activity;
  4. produced inadvertent hazards;
  5. allowed some other anomaly.
- The book clarifies that this list is not closed.
- Every finding that shows a condition, policy, practice, or vulnerability that puts assets at risk must be documented and addressed.
  - It includes anything that could cause or worsen reputational or brand risk.
- Result: continuous improvement of protection and risk management, mitigating known threats and vulnerabilities.

### 1. Security Officers — 10.1

#### 1.1 Industry data

- It is the most visible part of private security. It is a global industry worth billions.
- **2017:** about **20 million** private security workers worldwide.
- **2017:** an industry of about **US$180 billion**.
- Security officers are usually **the most expensive item** in the security budget.

#### 1.2 Services they provide (11)

The public sees them as simple "guards," but they do much more:

1. Control access and the movement of pedestrians and vehicles.
2. Patrol buildings and perimeters.
3. Escort materials and people.
4. Inspect security and fire exposures.
5. Monitor assets from a central control facility.
6. Respond to emergencies.
7. Deal with disruptive people.
8. Observe and report concerning activities or behavior.
9. Safety and accident prevention.
10. Public relations.
11. Special assignments.

#### 1.3 Hiring officers

- Local regulation must be known. In some places training or certification is required.
- Five personal attributes of a good officer:
  - good character;
  - communication ability;
  - coachability;
  - neat appearance;
  - appreciation for ethics.
- Preemployment screening is very important.

#### 1.4 Weapons — 10.1.1

- An armed officer exposes the organization to risks and lawsuits if the weapon is used.
- In most cases the officer is armed only if:
  - without a weapon there would be more danger to life safety; or
  - the officer is expected to possibly have to use fatal force.
- Training is usually mandatory by government mandate.
- **Less than lethal weapons:** electroshock weapons, batons, and aerosol irritants. They are also dangerous and require training and prudent use.
- All use of weapons must be in the **use-of-force policy**. That policy:
  1. sets the circumstances and limits in which force may be used;
  2. sets the level of force acceptable to the government and to the organization;
  3. serves to judge whether a specific use of force was reasonable.
- It is usually based on a **security force continuum**: it runs from simple officer presence to deadly force.
- Use-of-force training must be specific, thorough, and ongoing, with practice and drills, and must be documented.

#### 1.5 Proprietary vs. Contract Officers, or Hybrid — 10.1.2

| Model | What it is | Advantages | Disadvantages or burden |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Proprietary | Officers employed directly by the organization | More control over selection, screening, training, and supervision. Employee loyalty brings lower turnover, more knowledge of the post, and better performance. Makes it possible to look for special skills, such as detecting suspicious behavior or handling electronics | The company does everything: recruiting, processing applications, screening, administering benefits, managing performance, applying discipline, and scheduling shifts |
| Contract | Officers from a security services company | Shifts part of the burden of hiring, training, and supervising to the provider company. Gives more flexibility to raise or lower headcount | The provider company looks for generalists who can serve any client, not special profiles |
| Hybrid | A mix of proprietary and contract officers | Increasingly popular. Balance: saves costs and keeps reasonable control | The book mentions none |

- **Decision criterion according to the book:** the type of assets protected, the level of risk, and what is expected of the officers. **Not** cost.
- **Exam note:** the book justifies the hybrid model by cost savings. But it then says the decision should not be based on cost. On the exam, decide by assets, risk, and expectations.

#### 1.6 Managing the Force — 10.1.3

**General orders vs. post orders:**

|  | General orders | Post orders |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What they are | Basic principles of conduct and operation | More specific instructions |
| Scope | The entire force | A single topic and a specific post |
| Where they are | The book does not say | Available at each guard post |
| Examples | Maintain a professional demeanor. No smoking or eating on duty. Enforce rules tactfully. Do not leave the post without proper relief | Daily procedures or actions in emergencies |

**Supervision:**

- Visit posts from time to time and evaluate the officers.
- Ask the officer about their post orders to see whether they understand them.
- Observe the condition of the post and of the officer.
- Coaching on the job. It serves to evaluate and to improve performance.

**Reports and logs:**

- A written report must be made of each incident and a log kept of significant events.
- The log can be manual or electronic. There are two kinds:

| Type of log | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Main log / control log | Cumulative, consecutive history of the significant events of all shifts |
| Individual post log | Local record of the events of a specific post |

- What they are for: they are a historical record. They also provide data to measure the effectiveness of the officers and of the program.
- **Exam note:** the same log has two names: "main log" and "control log."

**Fatigue and loss of attention (vigilance):**

- The work is repetitive and tiring. Bosses and subordinates must be trained about fatigue and how to combat it.
- Measures:
  - shifts that allow real rest;
  - more light during the night;
  - a cool environment;
  - naps where possible;
  - periodic patrols, movement, or rotation;
  - having the officer call the supervisor to check in;
  - automatic calls to the officer to confirm they are okay.

**Performance appraisals:**

- It measures three things: how they perform their tasks, their general conduct, and their positive and negative traits tied to performance.
- **Minimum:** one formal individual appraisal **per year** and an informal review **at six months**.
- The supervisor reviews each post. They identify the most recurrent failures and their cause.

**Discipline:**

- Serious infractions are addressed as soon as they occur.
- Each employee must know which behaviors are prohibited and what consequence each has.
- Everything must be in a disciplinary policy.

**Robots and drones:**

| Better for people | Better for technology |
| --- | --- |
| Tasks that require deciding, perceiving, or changing course on the fly (pivot) | Monotonous, repetitive, and simple tasks. People get bored or complacent with them |
| — | Examples: patrols, inspections in remote places, and detection of hazardous materials |

### 2. Training — 10.2

- Supervisors and managers must make the officer's work serve for their growth. Their role is to teach, counsel, and coach.
- **Education, training and development:** they are related and reinforce each other. All three produce learning.
- **Learning:** a positive change in behavior on the job. The result is a more competent, capable, and professional officer.

**Main benefits of training (6):**

1. Better job performance.
2. Easier supervision.
3. Revised procedures.
4. More motivated staff.
5. Reduced turnover.
6. Legal protection.

**How to detect what training is needed (training needs).** It must be done before delivering anything:

1. First see whether the law requires any training.
2. Then, look for industry standards, guidelines, or best practices that set the parameters.
3. Also, review the failures noted in after-action reports.

**What influences the method chosen (4):** schedules, available technology, the instructor's comfort, and the number of trainees.

**Methods the manager must consider (5):**

1. Lectures.
2. Job aids.
3. Distance learning.
4. On-the-job training.
5. Mentoring.

**Strategies to make training cheaper (12):**

1. Off-duty training.
2. Tuition reimbursement.
3. Recognition programs.
4. Integrated training.
5. Videos.
6. Selling security services.
7. Supervisor training.
8. Internships.
9. Prior experience.
10. Officer selection.
11. Job rotation.
12. Membership in professional associations.

**Trends:**

- As society grows more complicated, the officer gains importance and the need for training grows.
- More weight will be given to human service skills.
- Example: in some places they do community protection in high-crime housing developments.
- That requires better communication, legal knowledge, and mastery of technological devices and weapons.

**Career path:**

- Improves recruitment, motivation, and retention.
- The officer learns on the job things that are useful for a management position.
- It is advisable that they study supervision, management, and business principles. The employee wins and the company wins.

### 3. The Security Services Contract — 10.3

- **Who hires outside officers:** governments, corporations, small businesses, residential communities, and individuals.
- **Where they work:** public, semipublic, and private environments.
- Client and provider must collaborate to achieve good results.

**Process:**

1. **Thoroughly analyze needs.** It is key to preparing the request for proposal (RFP), negotiating, and then administering the contract. It must be clear that officers are needed.
2. **Define the scope of work.** It is based on examining the site, the layout of the facilities, the building design, and internal processes.
3. **Consider the corporate culture and the objective.** Is the aim a security image or an effective asset protection force?
4. **Prepare the solicitation summary.** It gives providers as much information as possible and includes the purchasing rules.
5. **Prepare the RFP.** It describes the services the client requests.
6. **Receive the operating agreement.** The agency prepares it from the RFP and details the responsibilities of each party.
7. **Hold a bidders' conference:**
   - qualified bidders are invited;
   - the statement of work, the general and special orders, and the proposed operating agreement are discussed;
   - a walk-through of the facility is conducted;
   - the officer's main tasks are explained in detail;
   - it closes with a final round of questions and answers.
   - This way, everyone prepares their proposal with the same information.
8. **Choose with criteria that allow fair comparison:** price, availability, and quality. For example, experience in that geographic area or in that industry.

- **The client must set the hourly pay rates.** It must not let the agency set them.
- It helps to add a general statement of the client's security philosophy. It conveys intentions and nuances that the formal language of the contract does not capture.
- **Exam note:** the book uses "scope of work" and "statement of work" as if they were the same.
- **Exam note:** here it speaks of "special orders," but in 10.1.3 it spoke of "post orders." It does not define "special orders" or say whether they are equivalent.

### 4. Consultants as a Protection Resource — 10.4

**Why a consultant is hired (5 reasons):**

1. Lack of time.
2. Lack of specialized knowledge in the company.
3. An independent and objective evaluation is wanted.
4. New ideas are sought.
5. Flexibility to have staff only when needed.

- Used both by security executives and by companies with no formal security function.
- The decision usually arises from a specific problem, need, challenge, or objective.
- Case in the book: a supermarket chain with many violent crimes at one store hires a consulting firm. It seeks to reduce crime opportunities at that store or across the whole chain.
- Management usually accepts their ideas: the consultant brings experience from other companies and knows industry norms.
- The consultant must stay out of the company's internal politics.

**Three categories of consultants:**

| Category (English) | Translation |
| --- | --- |
| Security management consultants | Consultants in security management |
| Technical security consultants | Technical security consultants |
| Security forensic consultants | Security forensic consultants |

- **Internal resource:** an internal consultant or a security advisory committee. It helps detect and solve problems before outside help is needed.

#### 4.1 Choosing a Consultant — 10.4.1

- **Where to look:**
  - consultants the executive already knows (it is the logical starting point);
  - a colleague's recommendation, better if from a similar business (one of the best sources);
  - industry associations, such as the IAPSC (International Association of Professional Security Consultants) and ASIS International.

**Five selection steps** (after defining what help is needed):

1. **Identify candidates.**
2. **Invite them to submit an application.** A company-specific form is advisable for easy comparison, plus the résumé.
3. **Evaluate the applications.** Compare them with each other and with the work to be done.
4. **Interview the top two or three.**
   - have them bring samples of previous work;
   - ask about their security philosophy to see whether it fits the company's policies;
   - if possible, give them a short tour of the facility.
5. **Negotiate and close the selection.** Scope, deliverable, methodology, timelines, and expenses are negotiated. Then the contract is presented.

**Fees:**

- They depend on the subject matter, the level of experience required, and the geographic region.
- Time and quality must be weighed.
- A low rate can end up costing more: a less skilled consultant may take longer to finish the job properly.

#### 4.2 Working with Consultants — 10.4.2

1. Once the contract is signed and before starting, notify the appropriate staff.
2. Appoint a consulting project coordinator. They work with the consultant, track progress, give them information, and help them.
3. Set the scope with clear specifications. It must include a work plan, progress reports, and a final report.
4. Explain the scope and the objective from the beginning. They must appear in the initial request for quote and in the written contract.
5. Before the evaluation, client and consultant review objectives, goals, scope, and deliverables.
6. Draw up the work plan with the coordinator:
   - tasks and priorities;
   - assignments;
   - delivery schedule;
   - deadlines converted into milestone/goal charts, if appropriate;
   - frequency of progress reviews;
   - information to be given to the consultant and its schedule.
7. Track progress with periodic meetings and written reports. They are set in the plan with the proviso "if deemed necessary."
8. Always require a written final report. It must have enough detail for the client's staff to apply the recommendations.
9. If the consultant is needed to help apply them, an additional contract or an amendment is signed, with a new scope.

- **Exam note:** for officers the book speaks of an RFP (request for proposal). For consultants it speaks of a "request for quote."

### 5. Executive Protection (EP) — 10.5

- **Definition:** security and risk reduction measures to protect people with elevated personal risk. The risk comes from their employment, their notoriety, their high net worth, or their affiliations.
- **Exam note:** EP is also called "close protection." They are two names for the same thing.
- The professional must know exactly which risks their program must reduce.
  - Faced with a specific, credible threat, a specific close protection style for a period of time may be enough.
  - But most programs need a sustainable, programmatic, and flexible approach.

**Who is protected (5):**

1. Business leaders.
2. People easy to recognize by their position or fame, sought out by the public and with public personal data.
3. People with a highly controversial job or a controversial stance.
4. People of high net worth, with greater risk of extortion or kidnapping.
5. People who have received threats.

- **First step of the program:** assess the risk to the person, their family, and the organization. The program must cover today's risks and anticipate future ones.
- **Collateral benefits:** especially in the corporate, government, and celebrity sectors.
- **Soft skills of the best agents (4):**
  - clear and effective communication;
  - good logistics planning;
  - critical thinking and problem solving;
  - resolving conflicts by talking.
- EP is expensive. It is advisable to quantify the return on investment (ROI). It is usually tied to the collateral benefits.

#### 5.1 Corporate EP — 10.5.1

- The company's success may not depend on the safety of its chief executive officer (CEO).
- But if the CEO is kidnapped or incapacitated:
  - in the short term, the company loses focus;
  - in the long term, it suffers damage to its reputation;
  - business continuity is disrupted, with an effect on operations, culture, stock price, revenue, and standing.
- Risks are increasingly global, new, and combined, from cyber to privacy. Programs have had to adapt.
- It must be explained clearly why protection is needed: to the executives, their staff, and key stakeholders. This way the program is seen as a valuable professional unit.

**Three organizational models:**

| Model | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| In-house | EP staff are full-time employees |
| Outsourced | External providers are used to protect the executives |
| Hybrid | Permanent employees set the strategy, policies, and procedures, and rely on EP providers |

#### 5.2 Government EP — 10.5.2

- All levels of government have protection units for their prominent figures.
- Most of those who do EP in government are sworn law enforcement officers.

**Protection agent vs. law enforcement officer:**

| Protection agent | Law enforcement officer |
| --- | --- |
| In an incident, takes the principal out of danger and flees with them | Runs toward the problem |
| With good prior planning, avoids putting the principal in danger | — |

**Government EP vs. private EP:**

| Aspect | Government EP | Private EP |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Authority | Comes from specific laws and regulations | Often has no formal authority to act |
| Control of the environment | That authority may allow it to control the place where the principal is | The book does not mention it |
| Agent status | Often sworn officials or police officers | The book does not mention it |
| Weapons | Often legally armed | Not always permitted |
| Training | Mandatory: regular training cycles and tactical and physical tests | Not always required |
| Plans | Strict protocols. They must submit an advance report with primary and contingency plans. It may be reviewed if there is an incident | The book does not mention it |
| If something goes wrong | An injury to the principal or a failure may require a formal investigation by governing bodies | The book does not mention it |

#### 5.3 Celebrities, High Net Worth, and Other Prominent Figures — 10.5.3

- Protecting them is similar to protecting a business leader.
- Their own challenge is public events: meet-and-greets, concerts or performances, and media events.
- Those events raise the risk to privacy and the risk of being targeted by persons of interest or stalkers.
- Society increasingly expects leaders and celebrities to speak out on social issues. That can increase their personal risk.

#### 5.4 EP Program Management — 10.5.4

**Threat assessments:**

- They are similar to traditional risk assessments. They follow the enterprise security risk management (ESRM) approach.
- Unlike a fixed facility, they look at the person's risk according to:
  - their career;
  - their public image;
  - their lifestyle;
  - their current position;
  - their status;
  - their family history.
- They can include the effect of the entity they represent (a religious group, a corporation) on their risk.
- They can include their social environment. For example, family members who are soft targets for reaching the principal.

**Vulnerability assessments:**

- They look at the environments the principal passes through:
  - fixed locations, such as the home and the office;
  - boats and aircraft;
  - private aviation terminals (FBO, fixed base operations);
  - other frequent destinations.
- Types of vulnerability: psychological, administrative, technical, and physical.
- Also external factors: the capacity of local government and police to respond or curb crime.
- Understaffed police, fire, or medical services are a vulnerability that may call for extra measures.
- Example: if police take on average much longer than tolerable, shelter-in-place or evacuation plans are strengthened.

**Risk assessments.** The final risk profile has two aspects:

1. The attractiveness of the target.
2. The likelihood that a threat will succeed.

Questions the analysis answers:

- Who would want to harm the executive?
- How do adversaries obtain information about them?
- What is the likelihood of the identified threats today?
- Does the executive want, need, and accept protection? Only during the workday, only on trips, or 24 hours?
- Not all executives in a company have the same risk. Some are controversial and highly visible; others work in the background. That is why the program must have a holistic approach.

**Operations.** Once threats and vulnerabilities are set, they are carried out by in-house or outside staff. Tasks of the EP team (7):

1. Help create security policies, including the team's use-of-force protocols.
2. Design training plans in personal protection, travel, and awareness for executives and employees.
3. Manage event security and apply protection protocols for staff and important guests (VIP, very important person).
4. Communicate and build relationships with authorities, such as local police and federal agencies, especially on trips abroad.
5. Design, apply, and monitor security measures at the executives' residences.
6. Create processes, procedures, and best practices in:
   - fire and life safety;
   - privacy of executives' data;
   - crisis communication;
   - event security.
7. Manage travel security.

**What can be outsourced:**

- Ad hoc support:
  - local security drivers;
  - advance personnel who know the destination city;
  - support agents who can carry weapons in the destination city.
- Ancillary parts of the program's core:
  - protective intelligence analysts;
  - technical surveillance countermeasures (TSCM) technicians.
- The entire program, including its management and strategic direction.

**Specialized services.** The EP professional must know when to use them, how to vet providers, and how to integrate them:

| Service | What it is / what it is for | When or how |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Digital EP | A team looks after the principal's digital security. Digital risk causes stress, financial loss, loss of private data, and reputational damage. It can also lead to physical risk | Hire experts and have contingency plans in case digital security fails, just like evacuation plans for a site |
| Technical countermeasures (TSCM) | "Sweeping" places for hidden microphones | Can take several hours and be very technical. Includes educating the principal, their staff, and their family to protect private information |
| K-9 sweeps | Security dogs, especially to detect explosives, as a layer of physical protection | 1) in high-risk moments; 2) before events with large crowds; 3) at high-risk locations; 4) continuously at locations that are high-value targets |
| Others | Covert surveillance, special driving techniques, training in the sport the principal practices | According to what each team needs |

- **Doxing (doxed):** posting someone's personal data on the internet with malicious intent. It can expose the principal to physical danger.
- **Exam note:** the book spells out TSCM in two different ways. First as "technical surveillance countermeasures" and then as "technical security countermeasures." The usual form is "technical surveillance countermeasures."

### 6. Workplace Substance Abuse — 10.6

- Substance abuse can harm a person physically, mentally, or emotionally.
- It usually brings tolerance and, in the end, addiction or chemical dependency.
- It causes personal, family, and financial problems that end up spilling into work.

**Generally accepted effects on the company (9):**

1. Productivity and morale drop.
2. Turnover and absenteeism rise.
3. Accidents rise.
4. Insurance costs rise.
5. Theft and dishonesty rise.
6. Unnecessary use of benefits rises.
7. Profits fall.
8. The company's potential legal liability rises.
9. The risk of negative public exposure and damage to image rises.

- It also drains talent, vitality, and enthusiasm. It destroys teamwork and cooperation. It reduces competitiveness and damages image.
- **Security's role:** in large companies it is usually run by Human Resources. Even so, security must know the problem, detect signs, and protect the organization.

**How to achieve a drug-free workplace (9):**

1. Make a commitment.
2. Set goals and objectives.
3. Assign responsible parties.
4. Draft a comprehensive policy, with input from senior management and lawyers.
5. Communicate the policy.
6. Apply it fairly.
7. Provide education and training.
8. Help whoever asks for it, including psychological support.
9. Audit the process regularly.

**Why people use at work (2 reasons):**

1. The user convinces themselves that it does not affect their productivity, and even presents it as something noble.
2. They spend many hours at work, and that is where opportunities to use, share, and buy arise.

#### 6.1 Drugs of Abuse — 10.6.1

| Class | Examples given by the book |
| --- | --- |
| Depressants | Benzodiazepines, barbiturates, and sedatives. Valium, Quaalude, Xanax, Lunesta, Nembutal, and Seconal. **Alcohol is also a depressant** |
| Narcotics | Morphine, heroin, and codeine |
| Stimulants | Cocaine, amphetamines, methamphetamine, methcathinone, methylphenidate (Ritalin), and anorectic drugs (which suppress appetite) |
| Hallucinogens | LSD (lysergic acid diethylamide), called "acid". MDA (methylenedioxyamphetamine). MDMA (methylenedioxymethamphetamine), called "ecstasy". PCP (phencyclidine), called "angel dust". Mescaline, from the peyote cactus. Certain mushrooms |
| Marijuana | **The most used drug after alcohol.** Its legal status changes quickly |
| Analogue / designer drugs | Synthetic versions of other drugs. They can be more potent than the natural form |
| Prescription drugs | Include benzodiazepines: depressants for anxiety, tension, and muscle spasms. Librium, Xanax, and Valium |

- **Exam note:** benzodiazepines, Xanax, and Valium appear both as depressants and as prescription drugs.

#### 6.2 Addiction — 10.6.2

- **Definition:** the disease of obsession and compulsion. One can be addicted to anything.
- The usual thought is of repeated, uncontrollable use of a substance or a repeated behavior.
- The addict usually also gets hooked on the social rituals of the drug: obtaining it, preparing it, and using it.

**Signs of addiction or use (Mayo Clinic 2021):**

| Sign | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Problems at work | Frequent absences, disinterest, performance problems |
| Physical health | Lack of energy and motivation, weight gain or loss, red eyes |
| Neglect of self | Disinterest in grooming, clothing, or appearance |
| Behavior changes | Becomes secretive and changes their relationship with family, friends, and coworkers |
| Money problems | Asks for loans, or steals money or items to sell |

**What the addict loses as the addiction advances (9):**

1. Arrests and convictions for driving while intoxicated.
2. Hangovers and blackouts.
3. Dysfunctional relationships.
4. Confrontations with family, friends, and bosses.
5. Disciplinary sanctions.
6. Demotions.
7. Dismissals.
8. Loss of freedom (jail).
9. Endangering their own life and the lives of others.

#### 6.3 Addressing the Problem — 10.6.3

**Enabling:**

- **Definition:** allowing or encouraging, knowingly or not, another person's destructive behavior.
- It usually stems from denial.
- The enabler keeps the user from suffering all the consequences. It maintains the illusion that they have no problem.
- A boss enables when they accept excuses for absences or poor performance, or when they believe the justifications.

**How to avoid being an enabler (8):**

1. Know the company's substance policy and how it is applied.
2. Know the symptoms of use.
3. Consider open or anonymous channels to encourage reporting.
4. Accurately document the employee's performance.
5. Recognize enabling behaviors and cut them off as soon as they appear.
6. Communicate what is expected and demand accountability.
7. Document what was done and its results.
8. Inform senior management of the problems.

- Supervisors and managers must understand addiction and chemical dependency. They must know how to recognize three destructive behaviors: denial, enabling, and codependency.
- The book considers failing to confront them a lack of care.

**The anti-drug policy must be:**

- practical, functional, and enforceable;
- written;
- well communicated;
- signed as received by each employee;
- applied fairly.

**Workplace investigation:**

- If the policy is violated, management responds quickly. Often that includes an investigation.
- It is a fact-finding process. Ideally it is separated from the decision maker.
- If they cannot be separated, the investigator must not let their biases influence their conclusions.
- It cannot violate the law, company policy, labor agreements, or anyone's privacy.
- It is confidential. Evidence, notes, reports, and conclusions are shared only with those who need to know.
- Senior management, the legal department, and Human Resources must always take part.
- Sanctions are imposed only after a detailed review of the findings by qualified managers.
- Many times the findings do not justify a sanction. Then intervention is the appropriate response.

**Discipline, intervention, and employee assistance program:**

| Response | What it is | Keys |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Discipline | Sanction | Only after thoroughly reviewing the findings |
| Intervention | Planned interruption of the destructive behaviors of the user and of those around them. **It is not discipline:** it is a caring behavior in which one plans, prepares, and acts | It shows them the consequences of their actions. It seeks to rescue them and get them productive again. It requires documented performance. Bosses must come out of denial, not assume it will improve on its own, and not tolerate poor performance. It is usually their only hope before the sanction |
| Employee assistance program (EAP) | Referral to a help program | Born in the **1940s**, as "occupational alcoholism programs," for alcohol only. Today it covers alcohol and drugs, family problems, mental health, and other personal issues. Paid for by the employer, **free** for the employee, and usually covers their family too |

- **Recovery:** it is long and painful. After treatment, the employee often returns to the same environment where they began using.
  - If users rule in that environment, it will be hard for them to stay clean and sober.
  - If they control the culture, they can make it impossible for them.
- **Training:** all levels must receive it as part of the program. It must give accurate information about the dangers, the policies, and what is expected of each person.

#### 6.4 Drug Testing — 10.6.4

- They serve to prevent and to detect.

| Moment | Objective |
| --- | --- |
| Before hiring (preemployment) | Detect potential users before they come in |
| On reasonable suspicion | Active employees |
| After an accident or injury | Active employees |
| Random | Active employees |
| On returning to work after a violation (return to duty) | Active employees |
| As follow-up to treatment (follow-up) | Active employees |

- They also deter: they create fear of being found out.
- Many states regulate these tests. Differences among jurisdictions must be taken into account.

**Seven questions for designing the testing strategy:**

1. Which substances will be tested for?
2. When will the tests be done?
3. What type of test will be used?
4. Who will be tested?
5. Who will collect the samples?
6. Where will they be collected?
7. Who will receive the results?

- **Benefits:** more safety and fewer injuries.
- **Requirements:** strong written policies and being ready to follow rules that constantly change.

### 7. Security Awareness — 10.7

- **Definition:** knowing that a security program exists, understanding its importance, and knowing how one's own behavior reduces risks.
- It is a permanent attitude that leads to acting in support of the company's security.
- **Keys to success:** tie the security strategy to that of the organization and show how it supports the mission. Involve the asset owners.

**Education, training, and awareness:**

| Concept | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| Education | Provides general knowledge |
| Training | Develops specific skills |
| Awareness | Seeks the person's conscious attention |

- **Exam note:** do not confuse the chapter's two trios. In 10.2 it is education, training, and **development**. In 10.7 it is education, training, and **awareness**.
- Well-informed employees and non-employees multiply the strength of the program (force multiplier).
- A security culture makes everyone protect themselves and the assets.
- **Support must start at senior management.** Executives must value the program and set an example.
- Managers at all levels are crucial. If they show indifference or contempt for it, their employees will do the same.

**Foreseeability and legal liability:**

- The first-line supervisor is the closest contact with employees.
- If they receive a complaint about an unsafe condition, behavior, or practice, it is understood that the situation was foreseeable and that the company knew. For example, an employee who threatens violence.
- This holds **even if the supervisor did not escalate the complaint**.
- If an injury or death was foreseeable and preventable, and the company did not act despite the warning, it can be sued.

**Role of each group:**

- **Employees:** they must watch for intruders who slip in behind them with their badge (piggyback) or who use social engineering.
- In information technology (IT), each employee can leave the network exposed to intruders.
- They must understand that security is everyone's responsibility.
- **Non-employees** (vendors, customers, and the public): they have fewer chances to learn the rules, but must know at least some.
- Example: a vendor with access to confidential information must know the procedures that protect and control it.

## Capítulo 11 — Physical Security (págs. 179–192)

**Central idea:** Physical security combines physical measures to protect assets against realistic and credible threats. It relies on enterprise security risk management, the four Ds, and defense in depth. It integrates structure, technology, and people, and requires good project management and maintenance.

### 1. Enterprise Security Risk Management (11.1)

- Asset protection is increasingly based on ESRM (enterprise security risk management).
- It applies to the public and private sectors, to traditional security, to cybersecurity, or to both.
- **What it is:** treating risk management as the process for managing security.
  - It uses risk principles.
  - It coordinates the process with the senior management whose operations generate the risk.
- Today it is considered the foundation of security management.
- Results it seeks:
  - for the security professional to be an integral part of business leadership;
  - for the practice to shift from managing programs to managing risks.
- **Typical error, the "quick fix":** the executive buys a popular system or believes a vendor. They assume everything is protected without asking what assets they have or what threats they face.

### 2. Physical Security Functions and the Four Ds (11.2)

- Problem in the field: universally accepted definitions and boundaries are lacking.
- Knowing what physical security is and is not is key in: assessment, planning, system design, implementation, and management.
- **Purpose:** protect tangible, intangible, or mixed assets, in the form of people, property, or information.
- **Definition:** a combination of physical measures designed to protect assets against the full range of realistic and credible threats.
- Usefulness of the definition in any project:
  - narrow the scope so as not to stray from the objective;
  - broaden the scope to include all pertinent measures;
  - keep focus without being limited to a single tool, such as video surveillance.

**The four Ds** — they must be the basis of every physical security project:

| D | English | What the measure does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deter | Deter | Discourages the adversary |
| Detect | Detect | Discovers the attack |
| Delay | Delay | Slows the attack |
| Deny | Deny | Prevents the adversary's access to the target |

**Exam note:** in this book the fourth D is deny, not respond. The response appears separately, at the critical detection point (section 5).

### 3. Physical Security Assessments (11.3)

- Every project requires a good understanding of risk, from a complete design to improving a single component.
- Order within the ESRM process:
  1. Understand the context.
  2. Following the ESRM cycle, identify the assets.
  3. Identify and prioritize the security risks.
- Basic tool: the security risk assessment or the security survey.
- Cause of confusion: there are many models. They vary by industry, region or country, specialty, and even by consultant.
- There are national and international standard models. Here they are of little use because they are too general.

| Tool | Level of mastery expected | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Comprehensive security risk assessment | Know it in general terms | General framework for risk |
| Security survey | Know it in depth | It is the basis of every physical security project and most of the fieldwork to gather data and evidence that justify the countermeasures |

#### 3.1 Risk Assessment Models (11.3.1)

Sequence of a comprehensive assessment:

1. Identify the assets and assign them a value, absolute or relative.
2. Assess the threats relevant to those assets.
3. Conduct the vulnerability assessment.
4. Consider the potential impact of a loss event: attack, theft, or property damage.
5. Summarize and prioritize the risks found.
6. Recommend mitigation measures and integrate them into a comprehensive protection strategy.

- **Likelihood factor:** many models include it. It is the probability that a loss event will occur in the future.
- It is estimated from:
  - historical data from the site;
  - history of similar events at similar companies;
  - makeup of the neighborhood and the immediate surroundings;
  - general geographic location;
  - political and social conditions;
  - changes in the economy;
  - other factors (the book leaves it open this way).
- Examples:
  - A company in a flood-prone or coastal area: greater probability of flooding than one inland, even if it has never flooded.
  - A business with a crime history on and around the premises: high probability of more crimes if it does not improve its posture and the economic, social, and political factors do not change.

### 4. Physical Security Metrics (11.4)

- Context: tight budgets, more regulation, and more oversight. Executives demand more performance and accountability.
- Managers are asked for hard data to justify funds and resources, and to show the ROI (return on investment).
- The budget limits the design of the physical security system.
- Performance metrics:
  - show the status of a program;
  - identify performance trends;
  - demonstrate ROI or added value;
  - all within the existing corporate culture.
- They are an important part of the ESRM program. Management and the risk owners always need to know:
  - how security helps protect the business and the mission;
  - what the current level of risk is.
- They measure the effectiveness and efficiency of operations over time.
- Challenge: if the organization has suffered no harm or been the target of attacks, success is hard to measure.

| Type of metric | Focus | What it is for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Systems (for example, PACS —physical access control system— or video) | Operational efficiency | Reduce alarm "noise" at the SOC (security operations center) |
| Human element (security officer force) | Resources | Know the right spending and the number and type of staff needed |

- Mechanics of "noise":
  - The SOC monitors and responds to alarms from the physical systems.
  - An inefficient system floods the SOC with alarms and alerts; the SOC cannot respond in time.
  - Over time, those alarms become noise on the operator's screen.
  - Goal: for only legitimate alarms and alerts to get through.
- Uses of human element metrics: program growth, cost increases, efficiency gains, and output costs. They give a view of the resources needed to meet goals and objectives.

### 5. Design Concepts (11.5)

- Every project must apply basic design concepts in all phases, especially in planning and initial design.
- Security risk management must guide decisions about:
  1. facilities (design, layout, use);
  2. general protection strategies;
  3. structural and electronic security systems;
  4. security officer operations (and other human support);
  5. physical security program management.
- It is a model for planning, implementing, evaluating, and monitoring protection programs. It applies to everything, not just building design or electronic systems.
- **Two fundamental principles:**
  - the four Ds (deter, detect, delay, deny);
  - layered security, also called defense-in-depth or security-in-depth.

| Type of layer | Examples from the book |
| --- | --- |
| Physical | Wall, entry control point |
| Functional | Intrusion detection layer, video surveillance layer, response force layer |

- Functional and physical layers can be combined to protect a specific area.
- They must reinforce each other and back each other up, without overdesign or unnecessary duplication.
- **Critical detection point:** the point where the remaining delay time exceeds the response force time.
  - Rule: remaining delay time > response time.

**Defense in depth:**

- The adversary must avoid or defeat several protections in sequence.
- Example: defeat a sensor and get through two barriers to reach a room or a file cabinet in a restricted area.
- Each layer may call for different actions and times and have different effectiveness. But each requires a separate act.
- Effects:
  1. creates uncertainty in the attacker's mind;
  2. increases their preparation time;
  3. adds steps to the intrusion;
  4. gives time for the security or police response;
  5. may convince them to abort and look for a softer target.

#### 5.1 Architecture and Engineering (11.5.1)

- Security architecture and design is a foundational element of the integrated strategy.
- It aims for security to be built in from the start, not added later.
- It applies to new construction, renovation, or expansion.
- Ideally: the security professional works with architects, engineers, and general contractors from the start and throughout the process.
- Using the results of the risk assessment saves money and improves protection.
- **First priority in a new project:** determine the client's requirements.
  - In engineering: site layout, size of the structure, number of people it will house, and activities that will be carried out.
  - Sources: the client and the design codes and regulations of the jurisdiction.
  - Security design works the same way.

### 6. Integrated Security and Protection Strategies (11.6)

- "Integrated" and "integration" are used in different ways depending on context and audience.
- Video, access control, sensors, and alarms interact with each other. That brings benefits, but requires detail, planning, and testing.

| Network backbone | Assessment | Problems |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Independent network | Preferred approach | None cited |
| Part of the enterprise network | Not preferred | Performance problems due to limited bandwidth or traffic spikes; permission conflicts over using the network |

- Good option: a single source responsible for preventive and corrective maintenance.
  - It is the single point of contact: it diagnoses the cause and manages the fix.
- Many security companies work with system integrators: third parties that supply or maintain components.
- Also with other providers: network services, telecommunications, or application software.
- All elements must be integrated into a comprehensive strategy.

**Three similar triads (do not confuse):**

| Where | The three elements |
| --- | --- |
| 11.6 — categories of physical security elements | Electronic security systems, structural security measures, human capital |
| 11.6 — what a successful design integrates | Architectural aspects, security systems, operational factors |
| 11.7 — primary components | Structural security, electronic security systems, human element (officers and response forces) |

- A successful design recognizes technological advances on all fronts, not just in electronic systems.
- Technology does not replace personnel: it is a force multiplier.
  - It increases the capabilities of the personnel.
  - It provides checks and balances against individual misconduct.

**Exam note:** the book gives three nearly identical triads with different names. Learn them separately (table above).

### 7. Structural Measures and CPTED (11.7)

- The three primary components must act in coordination to achieve the most effective and efficient protection.
- Structural security includes:
  - the general structure of buildings and facilities;
  - physical barriers;
  - locking systems;
  - lighting.

**CPTED (crime prevention through environmental design):**

- It is based on common sense and on understanding how people use space, for legitimate or criminal purposes.
- It works best with a multidisciplinary approach, in teams with:
  - planners;
  - designers;
  - architects;
  - landscape architects;
  - law enforcement;
  - security professionals;
  - users of the place (for example, residents and employees).
- The professional must master it in order to work with local crime prevention officers, building design authorities, architects, and designers, in new construction or renovation.

**For the security professional, CPTED is a set of management tools aimed at:**

| Target | What it means | Example from the book |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Places | Physical environments designed to produce behavioral effects that reduce the opportunity for certain crimes and the fear of them | Parking lot with emergency call posts and extra lighting, to overcome the feeling of distance and isolation |
| Behavior | Some places predict, create, encourage, or allow criminal or disorderly behavior; others generate law-abiding behavior | Gives no example |
| Design and use of space | Redesigning a space or using it better encourages desirable behaviors and discourages crime | Putting the parking lot in front of a convenience store |

- Places it cites: offices, parking lots, parks and public spaces, multifamily apartment buildings, warehouses, schools, houses of worship, and shopping centers.
- **Three basic CPTED elements:** territoriality, surveillance, and access control.

**Types of CPTED measures:**

| Type | What it involves | Examples from the book |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mechanical | Physical security hardware or electronic systems | Locks, fencing, CCTV (closed-circuit television) |
| Organizational | People or activities, not equipment | Guards, police patrols, neighborhood watch programs |
| Natural | Natural features and non-mechanical objects | Terrain, layout, landscaping, gates, walls |

- Key element: design or redesign a place to reduce criminal opportunity and fear of crime. Natural, mechanical, and organizational or procedural means are used.
- Theoretical basis: environmental criminology.
  - Well-designed places (buildings, parks, parking lots, and other structures) improve quality of life.
  - They do so by deterring criminal opportunities and reducing fear of crime.
- It also improves the organization's security posture and awareness.

**Exam note:** fencing is listed as a mechanical measure, but gates and walls as natural. Also, the third category later appears as "organizational or procedural."

### 8. Access Control (11.8)

- Electronic access control systems validate one or more credentials:
  - something you know;
  - something inherent to you;
  - something you carry.
- Components of a complete system:
  1. credential reader;
  2. communications cabling;
  3. distributed processor;
  4. central database;
  5. software;
  6. complementary interfaces with external systems;
  7. applications for request-to-exit devices on the doors that require them.
- **Personnel access control:** the part of the system that authorizes entry and verifies the authorization of whoever wants to enter a controlled area.

| The person… | Summary | What it really verifies | Can someone else use it? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Carries a valid credential | What you have | That whoever asks to enter has a valid credential | Yes |
| Knows a valid PIN (personal identification number) | What you know | That whoever asks to enter knows a valid number | Yes |
| Has the unique physical characteristic recorded at enrollment (biometrics, such as fingerprint or hand geometry) | What you are | The identity of the person | No |

- Except for biometrics, the devices can be used without the authorized person being present.
- Combining technologies:
  - advantage: the system is harder to defeat;
  - disadvantage: it can reduce throughput.
- Entry authorization methods: PIN, credentials, and positive personnel identity verification or biometrics.

### 9. Security Officers (11.9)

- They remain important and often essential, despite the technological boom.
- Technology will evolve, but it is unlikely to eliminate the need for well-trained officers.
- Successful strategies take advantage of technology and strategically integrate officers.
- Today they are used in more settings than ever:
  - sometimes they replace public police or soldiers;
  - they patrol urban centers or military installations;
  - they watch crowded areas: stadiums, shopping centers, large apartment complexes;
  - they transport prisoners or detainees;
  - they are growing in regions with civil unrest and war;
  - in energy exploration they face armed adversaries; heavily armed forces are used there.

#### 9.1 Implementing a Security Force (11.9.1)

- The security plan is based on understanding the risks it must control.
- A balanced approach uses defense in depth with layered controls:

| Type of control | Examples | Area |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Behavior modification | Policies, procedures, training, awareness programs | Security management |
| Electromechanical | Security systems, locks, lighting | Security technology |
| Personnel | Personnel, including security officers | Security operations |

- Officers are not an isolated resource, but part of the comprehensive plan.
- They are usually the most expensive component of the program. But they provide capabilities that technology cannot viably provide.
- Their use must be periodically reevaluated: meet objectives and get the maximum benefit from the budget.
- Balance sought: people, process, and technology.

| Staffing model | Who employs the officers |
| --- | --- |
| Proprietary / in-house | The organization itself |
| Contract | A security services company |
| Hybrid | A combination of both |

#### 9.2 Training (11.9.2)

- The content is aligned with the officer's responsibilities and role in the mission.
- It must ensure the minimum criteria for the position, according to legal, regulatory, and contractual obligations.
- Proficiency and competency levels must be measured.
- Training must be monitored continuously to find improvements.
- Ultimate aim: build, promote, and sustain a professional culture.

### 10. Project Management (11.10)

- **Definition:** planning, organizing, and controlling resources in a project.
- **Project vs. ordinary operation:** a project is temporary, with a defined start and end, and seeks specific goals and objectives.

| Kind of security project | Example from the book |
| --- | --- |
| Specific to the security mission | Improve visitor security |
| Security part of a larger project | Manage the security-related aspects of a new building on a campus, including capital and operational needs |

- Limited in time (start and end date) and in assigned budget.
- Exceeding them has consequences for the organization, the stakeholders, the team, and, above all, the project manager.
- Challenge: achieve all goals within time and budget, with minimal impact on budget and operations.
- Well managed, it leaves lasting benefits; poorly managed, lasting negative effects.

#### 10.1 System Design (11.10.1)

- **System (in security):** a combination of equipment, personnel, and procedures, coordinated to achieve the set objectives to the fullest. It is more than hardware.
- It is evaluated by performance and cost-effectiveness in three things:
  - countering threats;
  - reducing vulnerabilities;
  - decreasing risk exposure.
- The professional examines all activities and considers assets, exposure, and losses. The same when designing, acquiring, and deploying other elements.

| Phase | Task | Content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Planning and assessment | Task 1 | Identify critical assets, potential threats, resulting vulnerabilities, probable risks, and functional requirements; gather needs, objectives, and constraints |
| Planning and assessment | Task 2 | Analyze requirements and formulate countermeasure solutions or concepts to reduce or eliminate vulnerabilities and mitigate risks |
| Design | Begins on validating the concepts operationally and budgetarily | Produces the documentation for purchasing and installing |

- Example of task 1: to deter burglary, the target is hardened (target hardening).
- Key idea: proactivity versus reactivity. Risk is managed before the event, not after.
- **Product of the design phase:** identification and location of hardware and software, integration, and performance documentation. It must be so clear that vendors and installers interpret it the same way.
- **Serial process:**
  - each phase and task must finish before the next begins;
  - the output of one is the input of the next;
  - it is an important concept for senior management;
  - it is shortened only a little with a design/build relationship with a contractor, versus design, procurement, and construction with an architect or the owner;
  - adding staff or budget generally does not shorten it substantially.
- If the PPS (physical protection system) is part of a larger construction project:
  - it is executed and documented according to the schedules and documents of the architectural design and construction;
  - it is normally overseen by a certified architect;
  - those responsible for security design work with the architect to put the requirements into the contract documents for bidding and construction.

**Exam note:** the book uses the acronym PPS without spelling it out. From context it is physical protection system.

#### 10.2 System Implementation (11.10.2)

It begins right after the contractor is chosen. Steps:

1. Site preparation.
2. Contractor coordination.
3. Installation.
4. Testing and warranty issues.
5. Training.

- Each step requires attention from the contractor, the system designer, and the owner's representative.

**Site preparation and planning — the most important step:**

- All the equipment must already be in the design package and located on drawings. For example: door hardware, card readers, sensors, panels, cameras, monitors, consoles, gates, signage, and lighting.
- The installing contractor verifies locations and notes the necessary changes.
- The project manager and the contractor review the requirements and resolve differences before moving on.

| Rule for the contractor | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Site visit | Verify that conditions match the design |
| Written report | Of all site changes or conditions that affect performance |
| Corrective actions | None without the client's written permission |
| Existing equipment | Inspect, test, and document all equipment and signal lines that will be incorporated |
| Equipment that does not work | Provide technical data sheets or written functional requirements and the estimated cost of correcting it |
| Connection date | State in the report the expected date of connection to the existing equipment |
| Disconnections | No disconnection or downtime without prior written approval from the client |
| Failures after starting | If something fails after the contractor has started working on it, they diagnose and correct it |
| Costs | Pays for repairs due to negligence or abuse of the client's equipment |

**Installation:**

- The project manager instructs the contractor to install all components, including equipment provided by the client.
- An integrator can also install.
- Everything according to the manufacturer's instructions and applicable installation standards.
- The contractor provides connectors, terminators, interconnections, services, and adjustments for a complete and operable system.
- After installation, the project manager tunes the system to the site's operations.

**Testing:**

- It can cover equipment, personnel, procedures, or any combination.
- Ideal acceptance tests take the system to the limits of the site's own threats.
- They must simulate real threats and give conclusive evidence of effectiveness.

**Exam note:** the book lists five steps, but does not develop coordination, warranty, or training separately.

#### 10.3 Follow-up and Support (11.10.3)

- Many systems fail only because of poor maintenance.
- Developing countries: sophisticated systems (for example, in critical infrastructure) degrade without maintenance. The installer came from far away and left; local resources are not enough.
- Developed countries: maintenance is also ignored at purchase and afterward.
- Relying on on-call repairs leads to the premature death of the system through deferred maintenance.
- Rules:
  1. Every system requires regular maintenance.
  2. Maintenance planning is an essential part of the design.
  3. The greatest support effort goes to the equipment whose failure would have the most impact on the system.
  4. Choose systems according to the support the local market can provide after installation.
  5. If no local provider can, plan for a maintenance budget, spare parts in the original program, and a dedicated maintenance technician.

### 3. Terrorism (12.3)

#### 3.1 Definitions of Terrorism (12.3.1)

- **General definition from the book:** an act of violence designed to achieve a political end.
  - Used by state and non-state groups.
  - There is no universal definition; this has caused confusion in many incidents.
- In the U.S., definitions vary by agency.

| Source (year of citation) | Elements of the definition |
| --- | --- |
| United Nations panel (United Nations 2005) | Any action intended to cause death or serious bodily harm to civilians or non-combatants. Purpose: to intimidate a population or compel a government or international organization to do or abstain from doing something |
| Arab Convention for the Suppression of Terrorism, adopted by the Council of Arab Ministers of the Interior (League of Arab States 1998) | Act or threat of violence that advances an individual or collective criminal agenda. Seeks to sow panic and fear by harming people or endangering their life, freedom, or safety. Or seeks to damage the environment or public or private facilities or property, occupy them, or seize them. Or seeks to jeopardize a national resource |
| Crown Prosecution Service of the United Kingdom (British prosecution service; N.D., no date) | Use or threat of action, inside or outside the United Kingdom, to influence any international governmental organization or intimidate the public. Must advance a political, religious, racial, or ideological cause |
| FBI, based on the U.S. Code (federal code of the U.S.; N.D.) — international terrorism | Violent, criminal acts by individuals or groups inspired by, or associated with, designated foreign terrorist organizations or nations (state-sponsored) |
| FBI — domestic terrorism | Violent, criminal acts by individuals or groups for ideological purposes stemming from domestic influences: political, religious, social, racial, or environmental |

**Quick comparison:**

- Only the UN limits victims to civilians or non-combatants.
- Only the Arab one names the environment, the occupation of facilities, and national resources.
- The British one requires a political, religious, racial, or ideological cause, and covers acts inside or outside the country.
- The FBI separates by the origin of the inspiration: foreign (international) or domestic (domestic).

### 4. Workplace Violence (12.4)

- A single incident can cause deaths, disabling injuries, and emotional trauma.
- It affects those involved, the company, and the community, sometimes for years.
- It is not likely to decrease, because personal and global stressors are growing.
- WEF 2021:
  - Public health gaps, digital inequality, educational disparities, and unemployment hit vulnerable groups hardest.
  - They can further weaken social cohesion.
  - "Social cohesion erosion" and "livelihood crises" are among the long-term risks of highest likelihood and impact.
  - Many people have little to lose.
- These growing factors raise the potential for workplace violence.

**Employer's obligation:**

- It must provide a safe workplace.
- It extends to employees, contractors, visitors, and guests on the premises.
- In general it does not distinguish between internal and external sources of danger.
- If the employer or its representative has reasonable cause to believe someone may commit violence, it must protect the potential victim.

**ASIS standard *Workplace Violence and Active Assailant—Prevention, Intervention, and Response* (ASIS International 2020):**

- Key resource: guidance and best practices for creating or improving prevention programs.
- Failing to follow its best practices can create legal liability.
- State and local laws and contractual obligations may also apply.
- Holistic, strategic approach in four phases:
  1. prevention;
  2. intervention;
  3. response;
  4. aftermath.

#### 4.1 Definition of Workplace Violence (12.4.1)

**Broad definition of WPV (workplace violence):** any threatening or disruptive behavior, in three categories:

| Category | Examples from the book |
| --- | --- |
| Overt acts of violence | Homicide, robbery, physical assault |
| Threats of those acts | Gives no separate examples |
| Other physical or verbal behavior that generates reasonable concern of violence against employees | Harassment, intimidation, bullying |

- **ASIS standard definition:** a spectrum of behaviors that generate reasonable safety concern about violence.
  - Includes overt acts of violence, threats, and other behaviors.
  - There must be a nexus between the behavior and the physical safety of employees.
  - It can come from any internal or external relationship.
- Sources of the threat: internal or external.

**Consequences:**

- The most serious: loss of life and physical and mental injuries.
- Business concerns:
  - liability;
  - productivity;
  - employee morale;
  - reputation;
  - continuity of operations;
  - associated costs.
- Main business concern: the cost of being found liable for negligence.
- Loss of productivity: hundreds of thousands of dollars per incident, due to:
  - business interruption;
  - absenteeism;
  - sick leave;
  - slowdown of work;
  - distraction of managers and workers;
  - general disorganization.
- Other costs (Sheely 2019):
  - critical incident care;
  - increased workers' compensation and health premiums;
  - turnover expenses;
  - shareholder value.

#### 4.2 Organizational Commitment and Policy (12.4.2)

- **Most important step when starting a WVPI (workplace violence prevention and intervention) program:** create a policy backed by senior management.
- That backing is spread throughout the organization, so no one doubts it is a serious priority.
- Senior management provides the necessary resources, funds, and authorizations.
- The policy is communicated at hiring and throughout employment.
- It declares the commitment to a safe place and a code of conduct (ASIS International 2020).
  - The code prohibits all violence, threats, and behavior that could be interpreted as intent to harm.

**What the policy must do:**

1. Define prohibited conduct.
2. Regulate or prohibit weapons.
3. Require prompt reporting of safety concerns or policy violations.
4. Offer several avenues for reporting.
5. Ensure that reports will be taken seriously and that there will be no retaliation.
6. Require reporting of protective or restraining orders.
7. Impose disciplinary measures for violating the policy.

- **Avoid the term "zero tolerance":** it reduces reporting and lowers safety.
- There are many forms of incidents. Most affect one or a few people.
- Incidents without death or serious bodily harm are probably underreported.
- Not even the best program can prevent all crimes or workplace violence events.

### 5. Cybersecurity (12.5)

**The cameras case (Meyer 2021):**

- March 2021: up to 150,000 security cameras in schools, hospitals, factories, and companies were compromised.

#### 5.1 Predominant Attack Types (12.5.1)

- Attacks are increasingly complex and advanced.
- Facial and voice recognition can be defeated with face-swap and voice alteration.
- **Case (Sawant 2021):** the "CEO" calls the finance manager and asks to transfer millions of dollars to an anonymous account. The manager does it. It was fraud: voice imitated with voice synthesis software.
- Cybersecurity is a critical component of the security posture.
- The range of vectors goes from nation-state actors to ransomware and the compromise of physical security equipment.
- The following vectors are growing in frequency and impact.

**Phishing and social engineering:**

- Attacks, leaks, and intrusions are carried out by internal and external actors.
- Internal actors can be:
  - malicious;
  - employees who made an honest mistake;
  - employees whose credentials were stolen through social engineering or phishing.
- **Social engineering, according to CISA (Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency; CISA 2020):** the attacker uses human interaction (social skills) to obtain or compromise information about the organization or its systems.
  - They may seem modest and respectable.
  - They may say they are a new employee, repair technician, or researcher, and even show credentials.
- It was born in physical security. Today it is increasingly used online, with data from social networks and profiles.
- **Phishing:** takes advantage of social engineering. The attacker poses as a trusted organization and uses email or malicious sites to request personal information.
  - CISA example: an email that looks like it is from a credit card company or bank, suggests a problem, and asks for account data. With the reply, the attacker gets into the accounts.
- COVID-19: scams and phishing that exploited fear of contagion rose.
  - In early 2020 the WHO (World Health Organization) warned that criminals were posing as it to steal money or information.
  - The emails asked for username and password, clicks on malicious links, or opening malicious attachments.
  - Eric Howes (principal researcher at KnowBe4, a security awareness and simulated phishing platform): it was a target-rich environment that exploited fear and emotions.
  - According to him, employees must be trained to expect these emails, identify them, and handle them safely.
- Besides identity theft and fraud, these tactics can give a foothold to compromise:
  - the access control system;
  - the surveillance system;
  - the electrical system.

**Ransomware:**

- It used to be called cryptoware.
- It encrypts the user's files and demands a payment to decrypt them.
- It became well known after highly visible campaigns in 2017: WannaCry and NotPetya.
- Entry routes: phishing, trojan attacks, compromised credentials, and software vulnerabilities.
- EUROPOL (the European Union's law enforcement agency; EUROPOL 2016):
  - each variant has its own traits, but many anonymize themselves with Tor or I2P to communicate;
  - they use business models that decrypt a test file for free to show their intentions;
  - traditional data-theft malware targets desktop Windows users, but ransomware has many more targets;
  - those targets range from individual devices to industry, health, or government networks.
- Hospitals, hit hard:
  - they must collect, maintain, and make available valuable PII (personally identifiable information);
  - they may pay to recover it immediately instead of restoring from backups.

**BEC (business email compromise):**

- It accompanies ransomware.
- The attacker poses as an executive to initiate a transfer to an account controlled by criminals.
- Kevin O'Brien (CEO of the cybersecurity company GreatHorn):
  - a timely email "from the CEO" gets employees to share sensitive financial information;
  - credentials stolen from executives give access to almost all sensitive information;
  - most of these attacks arrive via BEC;
  - a holistic approach to email security is needed that identifies, addresses, and reinforces the processes vulnerable to phishing.

**Intellectual property theft:**

- Trade secrets and intellectual property increasingly attract external actors and nation-states.
- In the 2020s, the U.S. government denounced China's economic espionage more forcefully.
- William Evanina, director of the NCSC (National Counterintelligence and Security Center), spring 2020:
  - the FBI had more than 1,000 open intellectual property theft cases tied to people associated with the People's Republic of China.
- China obtains technology through foreign direct investment.
- Also with a broad cyberespionage campaign, from the 2000s to today.
- James Lewis (senior vice president and director at CSIS, Center for Strategic and International Studies):
  - the Internet gave China unprecedented access to poorly protected Western networks;
  - cyberespionage is accompanied by human agents, in China and in other countries;
  - the most profitable programs shifted from human agents against Western facilities in China to cyberespionage.
- General Intelligence and Security Service of the Netherlands (2021 report): Beijing is an imminent threat to the Dutch economy.
- Finland and Canada expressed similar concerns.
- David Vigneault, director of the Canadian Security Intelligence Service:
  - China tries to steal trade secrets and classified data;
  - it also tries to coerce the Chinese community living in Canada.

**Exam note:** do not confuse the CSIS of James Lewis (a U.S. think tank) with the Canadian service of Vigneault. The book only uses the acronym CSIS for the former.

**Attackers leverage supply chains:**

- Espionage and supply chain attacks by Russia-based actors are a concern.
- December 2020: the firm FireEye revealed that an actor infiltrated the software supply chain of a vendor, SolarWinds.
  - It compromised a product and accessed the systems of FireEye and other customers.
- FireEye's analysis of the method:
  - initial dormant period of up to two weeks;
  - then it retrieves and executes commands called "Jobs": transfer files, execute files, profile the system, reboot the machine, and disable services;
  - disguises its traffic as the OIP (Orion Improvement Program) protocol;

## Apéndice — Standards in Security (págs. 209–212)

**Central idea:** A standard gathers criteria and best practices to improve quality and reliability. Standards can be voluntary, mandatory by law, or mixed. Management system standards work with the Plan-Do-Check-Act cycle.

### 1. What a Standard Is

- **Standard:** a set of criteria, guidelines, and best practices to improve the quality and reliability of products, services, or processes.
- Thanks to national and international standards, parts, processes, and systems work regardless of:
  - who creates or performs them;
  - who uses them;
  - where they are used.
- They are developed to meet a market need or fill a gap in existing standards.
- They are coordinated by **accredited standards developing organizations (SDOs)**. These bring together public and private stakeholders to identify industry best practices.
- They are building blocks for innovation and competitiveness. They provide a common language that favors national and international trade.
- **Four development principles:**
  1. Consensus.
  2. Openness.
  3. Due process.
  4. Transparency.
- In security, they help nations, communities, societies, organizations, and individuals protect assets and improve their resilience. They serve against natural and human-caused threats.

### 2. Types of Standards

| Type | What they are | Examples / notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Voluntary standards | Not mandatory, but widely adopted. Some have been integrated into the regulatory frameworks of several countries | ISO (International Organization for Standardization) and national standards institutes. In the U.S., also trade or professional associations such as ASIS International |
| Statutory or regulatory standards | Mandatory by law. Enforced by formal authorities | — |
| Mixed standards | The boundary blurs when a voluntary standard is incorporated into laws or regulations | Fire, construction, and life safety |

- **Areas of ISO standards relevant to asset protection:**
  1. Facilities management.
  2. Health, environment and safety.
  3. Risk management.
  4. Security and resilience.
  5. IT and information security.
- **Technically voluntary standard but mandatory in practice:**
  - Insurers set the availability and cost of casualty insurance according to the use of approved materials or standardized practices.
  - Contracts may include standards as requirements.
  - Adoption of standards is increasingly **client driven**.

### 3. Bodies That Issue Standards

| Body | What it covers |
| --- | --- |
| ISO (International Organization for Standardization) | International voluntary standards; areas listed above |
| National standards institutes | National voluntary standards |
| ASIS International | Security management standards. Has a broad library of standards and guidelines (asisonline.org, Standards & Guidelines section) |
| NFPA (National Fire Protection Association) | Its standards are **not all voluntary**: some are incorporated into regulations, such as building codes |
| UL (Underwriters Laboratories) | Several standards on security equipment: locks, alarms, and access control systems |
| Illuminating Engineering Society | Lighting standards and practices |
| Electronic Industries Association | Electronic components and products |

### 4. Benefits of Standards

They make the security professional's work easier. They can do one or all of these things (9):

1. Codify best practices and processes, and share lessons learned.
2. Provide tools to assess threats, risks, vulnerabilities, criticalities, and impacts.
3. Define measurement methods.
4. Document equipment performance requirements to ensure effectiveness and safety.
5. Set design requirements so that devices, systems, and infrastructure withstand threats.
6. Define effective methods for identifying people.
7. Increase knowledge and competence.
8. Improve information sharing among jurisdictions and interoperability.
9. Give consistency to services.

### 5. Management System Standards

- They help organizations improve how they deliver services and execute processes. They are widely accepted in many fields.
- **The most prominent:**
  - **ISO 9001**: quality management systems.
  - **ISO 14001**: environmental management systems.
  - The book does not give the year of these standards.
- The management system approach calls for analyzing the requirements of the organization and of the stakeholders, and defining the processes that lead to success.
- A management system provides the framework for **continual improvement**.
- It increases the likelihood of achieving **strategic, operational, tactical, and reputational** objectives, and strengthens the organization's resilience.

### 6. Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) Cycle

- It is a structured problem-solving method, focused on continual improvement.
